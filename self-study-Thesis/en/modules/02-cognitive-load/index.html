1. Cognitive architecture: narrow at the gate, wide in the store

Cognitive Load Theory (CLT) starts from a simple premise: learning is a change in long-term memory. Kirschner, Sweller and Clark (2006) write: "If nothing has changed in long-term memory, nothing has been learned." All new information must pass through working memory before it enters the long-term store, and this gate is very narrow.

  • The limits apply only to new information. Once information is stored long-term, we can call it back into working memory without time or capacity limits (Kirschner 2006, p.77).
  • Processing is harder than holding. Miller (1956) gave about 7 elements; Cowan (2001) argues it may be only 4±1; when we process (compare, organize) it may be only 2–3 (Sweller 1998, p.252; Kirschner 2006).
  • Experts are better because of knowledge, not better reasoning. In chess, masters recall real positions very well but no better than others on random positions. Sweller 1998 cites estimates that masters learn about 100,000 configurations.

Schemas and automation

Knowledge is stored as schemas: structures that categorize information by how it will be used. A schema has two functions: storing and organizing information, and reducing working-memory load, because a schema, however much information it holds, occupies the space of one element. Sweller 1998's example: the "restaurant" schema gathers food, money, buildings, furniture... yet we hold it effortlessly.

Automation (after much practice) lets us process almost without working memory. Kotovsky, Hayes and Simon (1985) showed that isomorphic problems differing only in surface description can differ in difficulty by a factor of 16 because of how automated the rules are, and that memorizing the unfamiliar rules first shrinks the gap substantially.

2. Three kinds of cognitive load

KindDefinition in the sourceExample in programming
IntrinsicFrom the nature of the content, depending on how many elements must be processed at once (element interactivity) and on the learner's prior knowledgeUnderstanding recursion: many concepts held together (call, argument, base case, stack)
ExtraneousUnnecessary load from how material is presented or what activities are requiredA diagram on one page, its explanation on another; vague exercise instructions
GermaneEffort that contributes to building schemasSelf-explaining a worked example, comparing two solutions

Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas 1998, pp.258–264. Intrinsic and extraneous load are additive; germane load can be increased only if the total stays within working-memory limits.

Element interactivity: what makes content "hard"

Sweller 1998 compares learning foreign-language vocabulary or chemical symbols (Fe is iron, Cu is copper) with learning grammar. Vocabulary can be learned item by item; intrinsic load is low despite the quantity. The word order all of the being right and of all the wrong cannot be learned word by word. Likewise, to understand multiplying both sides of a/b = c by b to get a = cb, one must consider the left and right sides together; if one merely memorizes "move b to the right as cb", load falls but understanding is lost.

An easily missed point: the level of element interactivity cannot be determined from the material alone. The same content is one element for an expert (already a schema) and several interacting elements for a novice. That is why analysis of the learners goes with analysis of the content (Sweller 1998, pp.261–262).

The word "understanding" is used only for content with high element interactivity (Sweller 1998, p.260): translating "cat" into another language we call remembering or forgetting; getting the transformation of a/b = c wrong we call failing to understand.

A worked example works only if it does not itself impose a heavy load: worked examples still fail if presented in a split or hard-to-follow way (Tarmizi & Sweller 1988, cited in Kirschner 2006).

4. Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006: why minimal guidance does not work

The paper argues from cognitive architecture: discovery, problem-based, constructivist, experiential and inquiry-based learning all make learners search the problem space. Search occupies working memory without changing long-term memory; students can search for a long time and learn almost nothing (Sweller, Mawer & Howe 1982). The authors' conclusions:

  • For novices, full guidance is more effective and efficient than minimal guidance; guidance's advantage recedes only when learners have enough prior knowledge to provide "internal" guidance.
  • Tuovinen & Sweller (1999): exploration practice imposed larger load and led to poorer learning than worked-example practice; more knowledgeable learners were unaffected.
  • The authors distinguish teaching a discipline as inquiry from teaching it by the inquiry methods of the discipline; a novice is not a miniature expert.
Intersection with Module 01: Kapur 2016 points out that this argument assumes learners have no relevant prior knowledge. If a task elicits prior knowledge (even wrong) and a consolidation phase follows, solving first and then teaching can still beat teaching first. The two sides are not fully contradictory: both rest on working memory; they differ in how they view prior knowledge and when guidance is given.

5. Measuring cognitive load

Sweller 1998 distinguishes mental load (imposed by the task) from mental effort (resources actually allocated). Learners may raise effort to keep performance constant, so scores alone do not reveal load. Three families of measures: subjective (self-report scales), physiological (heart rate, brain activity, pupil) and task- or performance-based (secondary tasks). The most widely used is Paas's (1992) 9-point scale, from "very, very low mental effort" to "very, very high". This is the scale Kapur 2014 used to compare PF and DI.

6. Calculate and try the model

The model below is a toy to show additivity, not a real measurement. Try the three presets and move the sliders.

Exercise 1. Capacity = 100. Intrinsic = 65, extraneous = 45, germane = 10. What is the total load?
65 + 45 + 10 = 120, exceeding capacity by 20: overload.
Exercise 2. Keep intrinsic = 65 and want germane = 25 with the total exactly equal to capacity 100. What is the maximum extraneous load?
Extraneous = 100 − 65 − 25 = 10. To raise effort on building schemas, the useless load must fall.
Exercise 3. Same task, but a learner with a schema has intrinsic load 25, extraneous 45, germane 10. How much capacity is left?
Total = 25 + 45 + 10 = 80, so 100 − 80 = 20 units spare: the same presentation does not overload someone with prior knowledge.

7. Applying it to teaching programming

CLT techniqueIn introductory programming
Worked exampleGive a complete model solution with line-by-line explanation before students do a similar task (for novices)
Completion problemGive code with gaps ("fill in the loop condition") rather than a blank page
Process worksheet (Van Merriënboer 1997)A sheet describing the phases of solving and hints for each: understand the task, write pseudocode, test, debug
Avoid split attentionPut comments directly on the code, not on another page
Fade guidance (expertise reversal)Reduce the AI's hint level as students improve
Where this matches the treatment model"Ask AI in a Socratic way, hints instead of complete code" can be read through CLT as a way of chunking guidance: one step per turn, less extraneous load, while students still do the schema-building part. A control group with free AI may receive a full solution at once: less load now, but possibly no germane load.

8. Interpretive traps

Trap 1: treating germane load as something to maximize freely. Sweller 1998 states germane load may be raised only while the total stays within working-memory capacity.
Trap 2: using worked examples for everyone. Expertise reversal: for skilled students a worked example is redundant and can make learning worse.
Trap 3: confusing a high score with low load. Learners may compensate with effort; measure mental effort separately.
Trap 4: equating "more guidance" with "AI gives the answer". Guidance in CLT is guidance designed to change long-term memory (explanations, worked examples, stepwise hints). Handing a novice the answer is not necessarily good guidance in this sense.

9. Link to the thesis

RQ2, measurementAdd Paas's 9-point mental-effort scale after each session to compare treatment and control. PF expects higher effort; your model should show that this effort is useful (higher posttest scores without AI).
RQ1, task designSplit students by prior knowledge (pretest score). If the treatment effect differs between weaker and stronger students, that is an expertise-reversal-type interaction worth reporting.
Design riskAvoid a treatment model that both "asks Socratically" and lets students explore freely, since novices may be overloaded exactly as Kirschner 2006 warns.
