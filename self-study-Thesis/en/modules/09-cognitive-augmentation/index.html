09 · Cognitive Augmentation

Module 9/14 · Tools do not merely "do the work for us"; they can become part of the thinking system. Three classics answer: how should humans and machines divide labor (Licklider), how can intellect be augmented without replacing the person (Engelbart), and when is an outside tool truly part of the mind (Clark and Chalmers).

Sources: Licklider (1960) Man-computer symbiosis, IRE Transactions on Human Factors in Electronics · Engelbart (1962) Augmenting human intellect: A conceptual framework, SRI (AFOSR) report · Clark & Chalmers (1998) The extended mind, Analysis 58(1), 7–19. These are theoretical and visionary papers, not empirical studies; most of their claims are predictions or philosophical arguments, not measurements.

1. Licklider (1960): man-computer symbiosis

The concept. Opening example: the fig tree is pollinated only by the insect Blastophaga grossorum, whose larva lives in the tree's ovary and gets its food there. The two are interdependent, forming not just a viable but a productive and thriving partnership. This "living together in intimate association, or even close union, of two dissimilar organisms" is called symbiosis. "Man-computer symbiosis" is a subclass of man-machine systems; the author writes that at present there are no man-computer symbioses, and the paper aims to present the concept and foster its development.

Three distinctions

ConceptContent
"Mechanically extended man" (North)In past man-machine systems the human operator supplied the initiative, direction, integration and criterion; the mechanical parts were mere extensions, first of the arm, then of the eye. There was only one kind of organism (man); the rest was there only to help.
Replacement and automationIn many areas, "mechanical extension" has given way to replacement of men; the men who remain are "there more to help than to be helped". "Humanly extended machines" are semi-automatic systems that fell short of full automation: they are not symbiotic.
Artificial intelligenceThe author concedes that in the long run machines may outdo the human brain in most functions. Example: Gelernter's IBM-704 geometry theorem-proving program proceeds about as fast as Brooklyn high school students and makes similar errors. But there will be a "fairly long interim" in which the main intellectual advances are made by people and computers working together.

A multidisciplinary group estimated it would be about 1980 before AI lets machines alone do much thinking or problem solving of military significance. The author reasons: about 5 years to develop symbiosis and 15 years to use it, and says the "15 may be 10 or 500", but they would be "intellectually the most creative and exciting in the history of mankind".

Two main aims

  1. Bring the machine into the "formulative" part of problems. Present machines only solve preformulated problems by predetermined procedures. But many problems are hard to think through in advance; they are easier through intuitively guided trial and error where the computer cooperates, turning up flaws in reasoning or unexpected turns. Some problems cannot be formulated without machine aid. Quote from Poincaré: the question is not "what is the answer?" but "what is the question?".
  2. Bring the machine into thinking that must occur in real time. Example of directing a battle on a conventional schedule: formulate today, spend tomorrow with a programmer, next week the computer takes 47 seconds and returns a 20-foot sheet of numbers; the battle would be over before the second step. Thinking with a computer as with a colleague whose competence supplements your own needs much tighter coupling.
The only evidence is an unsystematic self-measurement. In spring and summer 1957 Licklider tracked what he did during hours he regarded as work (one person, himself as subject; he admits the sampling is inadequate). Result: about 85% of "thinking" time went to getting into position to think, decide or learn something needed; more time went into finding information than digesting it; hours went into plotting graphs that made relations obvious once done; in comparing six determinations of a speech-intelligibility function, hours of calculating were needed to make them comparable, then seconds to see what he needed. He concludes "thinking" time was devoted mainly to clerical or mechanical activities (searching, calculating, plotting, transforming, deriving consequences), and that his choices about what to attempt were determined "to an embarrassingly great extent" by clerical feasibility, not intellectual capability.

Comparative capabilities of people and computers

PeopleComputers (per Licklider 1960)
"Noisy narrow-band devices" but nervous systems with very many parallel, simultaneously active channelsVery fast and accurate, but constrained to one or a few elementary operations at a time
Flexible, able to "program themselves contingently" on newly received informationSingle-minded, constrained by preprogramming
Speak redundant languages organized around unitary objects and coherent actions, with 20 to 60 elementary symbols"Speak" nonredundant languages, usually with two elementary symbols and no inherent appreciation of objects or coherent actions

Anticipated division of labor

  • People: set goals and supply motivation (at least in the early years); formulate hypotheses; ask questions; think of mechanisms, procedures and models; make approximate, fallible but leading contributions; define criteria and serve as evaluators; handle very-low-probability situations; fill gaps when the machine has no applicable routine.
  • Machines: convert hypotheses into testable models and test them against data; answer questions; simulate; carry out procedures; transform data, plot graphs, interpolate, extrapolate; turn static equations into dynamic models; do "routinizable, clerical operations" between decisions; act as statistical-inference or decision-theory machines when there is a basis; diagnose and pattern-match as profitably as possible but accept a clearly secondary status there.

Prerequisites (per the author)

HurdleProposed direction
Speed mismatch between people and computersTime-sharing; a "thinking center" 10 or 15 years hence combining library functions with symbiotic functions, into a network of centers linked by wide-band lines.
Memory hardwareStoring an appreciable fraction of technical literature means billions of bits and billions of dollars; not all papers would be stored, only what is most succinct (quantitative parts and citations); books remain important. "Indelible" and "published" (read-only) memory.
Memory organizationRetrieval by name and by pattern, faster than serial search; for example Fredkin's "trie memory" storing both arguments and functions in a branching, tree-like structure.
LanguageThe basic dissimilarity between human and computer languages may be the most serious obstacle. Instructions to computers specify courses (steps and sequence); instructions to humans specify goals. People think more naturally in goals; nobody leaves Boston for Los Angeles with a detailed route.
Input and output equipmentThe least advanced area: almost only the electric typewriter. Proposes a desk-surface display and control where human and computer draw graphs, notes and equations on the same surface.

2. Engelbart (1962): augmenting human intellect

Definition. "By 'augmenting human intellect' we mean increasing the capability of a man to approach a complex problem situation, to gain comprehension to suit his particular needs, and to derive solutions to problems." This means a mixture of: more rapid comprehension, better comprehension, a useful degree of comprehension in a situation previously too complex, speedier solutions, better solutions, and solutions to problems that before seemed insoluble. Complex situations include the professional problems of diplomats, executives, social scientists, attorneys, designers, whether the situation lasts twenty minutes or twenty years. Not isolated clever tricks but "a way of life" in an integrated domain where hunches, cut-and-try, intangibles and the human feel for a situation coexist with powerful concepts, streamlined terminology, sophisticated methods and high-powered electronic aids.

Four kinds of augmentation means (LAMT) and the H-LAM/T system

MeansEngelbart's definition
ArtifactsPhysical objects designed to provide for human comfort, for the manipulation of things or materials, and for the manipulation of symbols.
LanguageThe way the individual parcels out the picture of his world into concepts his mind uses to model that world, and the symbols attached to those concepts and used in consciously manipulating them ("thinking").
MethodologyThe methods, procedures and strategies with which an individual organizes his problem-solving activity.
TrainingThe conditioning needed to bring the human's skills in using the other means to the point where they are operationally effective.

The system to be improved is H-LAM/T: a Human using Language, Artifacts and Methodology, in which he is Trained. The new system is envisioned to use computers and computer-controlled information storage, handling and display devices as artifacts. What has the "intelligence" is the whole H-LAM/T system, not the human alone.

Hierarchical structure of capabilities

All of an individual's process capabilities rest on basic capabilities within him or his artifacts, and form an interknit hierarchical structure (a repertoire, like a tool kit). Writing prose is a high-order process using many subprocesses (planning, composing, dictating) and is itself a subprocess of still higher processes (organizing a committee, changing policy). A change at any level (language, artifacts or methodology) affects the others, so a systems approach is needed. An imagined example: a new writing machine letting you compose trial drafts from rearranged excerpts of old drafts plus new words would change the way text is composed.

"Intelligence amplifier" (Ross Ashby). The author first rejected the term (the aim was a better match between existing intelligence and problems, not making people more intelligent), then found it fits: not raising native intelligence, but organizing intellectual capabilities into higher levels of synergistic structuring; what possesses the amplified intelligence is the H-LAM/T system, not just the person. This reapplies the principle of synergistic structuring used by natural evolution.

Language and thought: the Whorfian hypothesis

If means of external symbol manipulation affect language, and language affects the way we think (the Whorfian hypothesis), there are two ways the manner of external symbol manipulation can affect thinking: directly, and indirectly via language. Integrating the computer into individual intellectual activity introduces new and very advanced means of external symbol manipulation.

The "brick tied to a pencil" experiment

To illustrate that tools shape capability, the author fastened a pencil to a brick and tried writing (a figure shows results). With the brick-pencil one is slower and less precise; if in a hurry, it tires the untrained hand and arm. The point: same basic capabilities, change the tool and the capability structure changes.

Non-serial argument structure

An "argument" is a set of statements leading to a product statement. We usually write it serially because paper and books force this, but conceptually an argument is not a serial affair: it is sequential (some statements must follow others) yet structured. With a computer display it can be structured as a linked network. The fiction-dialogue scenario (an architect uses a computer "clerk" to design a building: entering dimensions, viewing sections, checking sun glare onto a roadway, functional analysis, utility load) is meant to convey the possibilities.

Research recommendations

  • Pursue the quickest gains first, then use the increased effectiveness to pursue the next.
  • The first group to augment: computer programmers. Reasons include: they work on many large, realistic problems solvable without interaction with other humans (easing experiments); typical problems need not involve much reference information; they already work in formal methodologies with computers so new tools seem less radical; successes can be used immediately within the research program itself.
  • The regenerative feature: the researchers develop means to increase the effectiveness of humans dealing with complex intellectual problems, and they themselves deal with complex intellectual problems; so good results can feed back to improve their own tools (positive feedback).
  • Two of the conclusions: (1) human problem-solving capability is possibly society's most important resource, so any possibility of improving it deserves serious consideration; (2) we need not wait to understand all mental processes or for smarter machines: we can begin developing economically feasible augmentation systems from what we have.

3. Clark and Chalmers (1998): the extended mind

The question: "Where does the mind stop and the rest of the world begin?". Two standard replies: the boundaries of skin and skull; or an "externalism" about meaning ("meanings just ain't in the head") carried over to mind. The authors propose a third position: active externalism, based on the active role of the environment in driving cognitive processes.

Three cases and the parity idea

  1. A person at a computer screen shows 2D shapes and must mentally rotate them to fit "sockets".
  2. The person can choose to physically rotate the image with a button, or mentally rotate as before (assume physical rotation is faster).
  3. In a cyberpunk future, a person with a neural implant can rotate as fast as the computer, but still must choose between the implant and mental rotation.

The authors say the three cases are alike: if the rotation in (3) is cognitive, by what right do we count case (2) as fundamentally different? We cannot just point to the skin/skull boundary since the legitimacy of that boundary is what is at issue. This is in fact the game Tetris: according to Kirsh and Maglio (1994), physically rotating a shape 90 degrees takes about 100 milliseconds plus about 200 milliseconds to select the button, while mental rotation takes about 1000 milliseconds. Players use physical rotation not just to position a shape but to help determine whether shape and slot are compatible: an epistemic action.

Epistemic action and the parity principle. Epistemic actions alter the world to aid and augment cognitive processes such as recognition and search; merely pragmatic actions alter the world because the physical change is desirable for its own sake (for example putting cement into a hole in a dam). The principle: if, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is part of the cognitive process. "Cognitive processes ain't (all) in the head!"

Active versus passive externalism

The externalism of Putnam and Burge (Twin Earth) concerns distal, historical external features; they are passive and play no role in driving cognition in the here-and-now. In a coupled system by contrast, external features are active: with immediate causal impact, so if the external component is removed the system's behavioral competence drops "just as it would if we removed part of its brain". Example: choosing a word in Scrabble as the outcome of an extended process including rearranging tiles on the tray; "the re-arrangement of tiles on the tray is not part of action; it is part of thought".

Objections considered

  • Is cognition tied to consciousness? No: many cognitive processes (memory retrieval, language processing, skill acquisition) occur beyond consciousness.
  • Portability ("Naked Mind"): real cognition is the package of resources always carried along. Reply: what matters is reliable coupling, most of which happens within the brain but can also be with the environment. If my calculator or Filofax is always there when needed, it is coupled with me reliably enough. The biological brain also loses capacities at times (sleep, intoxication, emotion). The brain may have evolved to factor in a reliably present manipulable environment; language is a central example of extending cognition.

From cognition to mind: Inga and Otto

CharacterSituation
IngaHears from a friend of an exhibition at the Museum of Modern Art; thinks for a moment, recalls that the museum is on 53rd Street, and walks there. The belief "the museum is on 53rd Street" existed before she consulted her memory (a non-occurrent belief, like most of our beliefs).
OttoHas Alzheimer's; carries a notebook everywhere, writing down new information and looking up old. The notebook plays the role usually played by biological memory. Otto hears about the exhibition, consults the notebook, which says the museum is on 53rd Street, and walks there.

Conclusion: Otto believed the museum was on 53rd Street even before consulting his notebook, since the notebook information plays exactly the role of a non-occurrent belief, only lying beyond the skin. Explaining Otto's action via "a belief that the museum is at the address in the notebook" is needlessly complex: "in an explanation, simplicity is power". The Twin Otto case (who wrote 51st Street by mistake; a duplicate of Otto from the skin in, but believing the museum is on 51st) shows a belief can fail to be in the head, and differs from Putnam and Burge because the external features here are active with a direct impact on behavior ("Otto walks to 53rd Street, Twin Otto to 51st").

Conditions for outside information to count as belief

  1. Constant: the notebook is a constant in Otto's life; when the information is relevant he rarely acts without consulting it.
  2. Directly available: the information is available without difficulty.
  3. Automatically endorsed: on retrieving it, he automatically endorses it.
  4. Previously endorsed: the information was consciously endorsed at some point and is there as a consequence. (The authors say the fourth is arguable; the first three certainly play a crucial role.)

In more exotic cases lacking these features, the applicability of "belief" gradually falls off. For example: if I rarely act without consulting my Filofax, its status resembles Otto's notebook; if I often act without consultation (sometimes answering "I don't know"), the information counts less clearly as part of my beliefs. The Internet "is likely to fail on multiple counts, unless I am unusually computer-reliant, facile with the technology, and trusting", but information in certain computer files may qualify.

Social extension, language and the self

  • Socially extended cognition: my mental states may be partly constituted by the states of other thinkers; for example an unusually interdependent couple, or the waiter at a favorite restaurant as a repository of beliefs about favorite meals. Central: a high degree of trust, reliance and accessibility.
  • Language carries the major burden of coupling between agents; it is "not a mirror of our inner states but a complement to them", and the recent evolutionary intellectual explosion may be due as much to this language-enabled extension of cognition.
  • The extended self: Otto himself is best regarded as an extended system, a coupling of biological organism and external resources. Moral and social consequences: interfering with someone's environment may have the same moral significance as interfering with their person.

EmphasisMachines do routine work so people can thinkAugment, not raise native intelligenceConditions: constant, available, automatically endorsed
Shared limits. All three papers predate or lie outside the era of generative AI and contain no standard empirical evidence about students' learning. The only numbers: Licklider's one-person self-measurement (85%), the Tetris timings cited from Kirsh and Maglio, and Engelbart's small "brick-pencil" demonstration. Most claims are predictions, philosophical arguments or recommendations. Licklider's "predictions" (1980, billions of bits) were estimates of the time, not measurements.

5. Compute it yourself

Exercise 1. According to Kirsh and Maglio (cited in Clark and Chalmers), physical rotation takes about 100 ms and selecting the button about 200 ms. What is the total time for physical rotation, in milliseconds?
100 + 200 = 300 ms.
Exercise 2. Mental rotation takes about 1000 ms. How many times faster is physical rotation (300 ms)? (round to 1 decimal)
1000/300 = 3.33, about 3.3 times.
Exercise 3. Licklider estimated about 5 years to develop man-computer symbiosis and 15 years to use it before machines alone could do much thinking (around 1980). What is the total length of that "interim" in years?
5 + 15 = 20 years.
Exercise 4. Licklider reports about 85% of "thinking" time was spent getting into position. What percentage remained for the actual thinking, deciding and learning?
100 − 85 = 15%. This is a one-person self-measurement.

6. Interpretation traps

Trap 1: treating vision papers as empirical evidence. Licklider and Engelbart write predictions and conceptual frameworks; Clark and Chalmers give a philosophical argument.
Trap 2: confusing "augmentation" with "replacement". Licklider separates symbiosis from "mechanically extended man" and automation; Engelbart states the aim is not to raise native intelligence but to augment the H-LAM/T system.
Trap 3: forgetting "Training" and "Methodology". For Engelbart, tools are one of four means; the system needs training and methods.
Trap 4: assuming every tool is part of the mind. Clark and Chalmers set conditions (constant, available, automatically endorsed); the Internet in general "fails on multiple counts" unless the user is very reliant.
Trap 5: using "the extended mind" to justify dependence at all times. The paper only states conditions for counting a tool as part of cognition; it does not say dependence is good or bad for learning. That is the module's inference.

7. Linking to the thesis

Dividing labor between people and AI in a programming classBy Licklider, the person keeps goal setting, hypothesis formation, criteria and evaluation; the machine does the routine work. Applied to a "responsible AI" model: AI may carry routine work (syntax, lookup) while students must keep planning, predicting and evaluating results. This is design inference; what is worth measuring is which part the AI is carrying.
The H-LAM/T system and "Training"Engelbart treats training and methodology as part of the system. The treatment group can be taught a method (Socratic, self-explanation) and not merely handed a tool; the "free AI use" control has the tool but lacks T and M. This is design inference.
What does measuring with AI removed measure?By Clark and Chalmers, if AI is tightly coupled into the system, removing it lowers the capability of the whole system, "as if removing part of the brain". So there are two different questions: (1) the capability of the student-plus-AI system, (2) the capability of the biological part standing alone. The thesis measures (2) for RQ1 because the educational goal is independent ability; this must be stated when interpreting results.
Otto's three criteriaThe three criteria (constant, available, automatically endorsed) can serve as indicators of AI dependence: does the student almost always ask the AI, without verifying, and accept its output automatically? Connect with reliance (Module 10). This is design inference.
