11 · Technology Acceptance Model

Module 11/14 · Why do people accept or reject a technology? TAM answers with two beliefs: perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. This module reads three original sources: Davis's MIT thesis (1985), the paper building and validating the scales (Davis 1989), and the paper comparing TAM with the theory of reasoned action (Davis, Bagozzi and Warshaw 1989).

Sources: Davis (1985) A technology acceptance model for empirically testing new end-user information systems, PhD thesis, MIT Sloan · Davis (1989) Perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and user acceptance of information technology, MIS Quarterly 13(3), 319–340 · Davis, Bagozzi & Warshaw (1989) User acceptance of computer technology: A comparison of two theoretical models, Management Science 35(8), 982–1003. The studies concern workplace computer users and MBA students in the late 1980s (email, editors, graphics software); not AI or learning. The module rests mainly on the two papers; the thesis (over 500 KB of text) was read for its abstract and aims.

1. Two key beliefs

The problem. Computers improve organizational performance only if used. Davis (1989) opens by noting that performance gains are often obstructed by users' unwillingness to accept and use available systems; research has been constrained by the shortage of high-quality measures of the determinants of acceptance, and many measures used in practice are unvalidated, with unknown relationship to actual use.
VariableDefinitionBasis of the definition
Perceived usefulness (U)"The degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would enhance his or her job performance."From "useful": capable of being used advantageously. In organizations people are rewarded for good performance (raises, promotions); a useful system is one for which the user believes in a positive use-performance relationship.
Perceived ease of use (EOU)"The degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would be free of effort."From "ease": freedom from difficulty or great effort. Effort is a finite resource that can be allocated to other activities; all else equal, an application perceived as easier is more likely to be accepted.

Theoretical foundations for the two variables (Davis 1989)

  • Expectancy and performance: Schultz and Slevin (1975) found the "performance" dimension most correlated with self-predicted use of a decision model (r = .61); Robey (1979) found .79 and .76 with two objective usage measures. But their instrument was derived by exploratory factor analysis, with a low sample-to-item ratio (2:1) and some items outside the definition (e.g., "my job will be more satisfying"). DeSanctis (1983) found only .04 to .26 with "motivational force": the contrast shows the importance of measurement.
  • Self-efficacy (Bandura 1982): similar to perceived ease of use; self-efficacy and outcome beliefs have different antecedents and both predict behavior (Bandura: behavior is best predicted by considering both).
  • Cost-benefit: decision makers trade off the effort of a strategy against the quality of the result; the usefulness/ease-of-use distinction parallels perceived performance versus effort.
  • Adoption of innovations: Tornatzky and Klein (1982): compatibility, relative advantage and complexity have the most consistent relationships with adoption; complexity parallels ease of use.
  • Evaluation of information reports: Larcker and Lessig (1980) found two factors (perceived importance and perceived usableness); Swanson (1987) found "value" paralleling usefulness and "accessibility" paralleling ease of use, with correlations with use of only .20 and .13.
  • Outside IS: Hauser and Simmie (1981) found two dimensions, ease of use and effectiveness; HCI research mostly measured objective ease of use (time, errors), while subjective ease of use is more relevant to the decision whether to use.

2. Building and validating the scales (Davis 1989)

Procedure

StepContent
Item generation14 candidate items per variable from 37 studies; the number came from the Spearman-Brown formula (10 items needed for reliability of at least .80, plus 4 to allow elimination).
Pretest15 experienced users at MIT Sloan (5 secretaries, 5 graduate students, 5 professional staff) ranked how well each item matched the definition and sorted items into categories; this gave 10 items per variable.
Study 1 (field)120 users at IBM Canada (Toronto) rated PROFS (email) and XEDIT (file editor); 112 responded (93%). About six months of experience on average.
StreamliningSix items per variable after item analysis.
Study 2 (lab)40 evening MBA students at Boston University tried two graphics packages (Chart-Master and Pendraw) for an hour each; ratings were prospective; paid $25.

Notes on item content

  • For ease of use, ease of learning was treated as a substratum of ease of use, so two learning items were added.
  • The five negatively worded ease-of-use items were dropped for apparent common method variance; reliability was .92 for the five positive items versus .83 for the five negative ones. Ironically, reversed items are usually used to reduce common method variance.

3. TAM and the theory of reasoned action (TRA)

TRA (Fishbein and Ajzen). Behavior is determined by behavioral intention (BI); BI is jointly determined by attitude (A) and subjective norm (SN): BI = A + SN. Attitude comes from salient beliefs times evaluations (A = Σ biei); subjective norm from normative beliefs times motivation to comply. Any other factor ("external variables") acts only indirectly through A, SN or their weights. TRA is a general model that does not specify the beliefs; salient beliefs (5 to 9) must be elicited for each context.

TAM (Davis 1986, as summarized in Davis, Bagozzi and Warshaw)

RelationEquationRationale
Intention from attitude and usefulnessBI = A + UIn organizations, people form intentions toward behaviors they believe will improve job performance, over and above their feelings about the behavior (performance leads to extrinsic rewards such as pay and promotion). This is a direct belief-to-intention effect, contrary to TRA.
Attitude from usefulness and ease of useA = U + EOUWeights estimated by regression, not multiplied by self-stated evaluations.
Usefulness from ease of useU = EOU + external variablesEase of use saves effort so more can be done for the same effort; external variables (system characteristics, persuasion programs, feedback) also affect U.
Ease of use from external variablesEOU = external variablesMenus, icons, mice, touch screens, training, documentation, support.

Two mechanisms by which EOU affects attitude and behavior: self-efficacy (ease makes users feel efficacious and in control; an "intrinsic" mechanism tied to intrinsic motivation) and instrumentality (saved effort raises performance, acting through U).

Subjective norm is not in TAM because of its uncertain theoretical and psychometric status: it is hard to separate direct effects (compliance) from indirect effects through attitude (internalization, identification), and attitude may itself influence SN ("false consensus").

Key differences between TAM and TRA

  • TRA elicits salient beliefs anew for each context and treats them as idiosyncratic; TAM posits U and EOU a priori and intends them to generalize across systems and user groups.
  • TRA sums all beliefs into one term; TAM keeps U and EOU separate, allowing comparison of their relative influence and tracing of external variables, which helps diagnosis.

4. The study by Davis, Bagozzi and Warshaw (1989)

Design. A longitudinal study of 107 full-time MBA students at the University of Michigan (first semester). The word processor WriteOne was voluntarily used in two public labs. Measured right after a one-hour introduction and again after 14 weeks (end of semester), with a 2-item self-reported usage measure. Salient beliefs came from telephone interviews with 40 second-year students; seven beliefs were chosen (each mentioned by over 20% and jointly over 75% of beliefs elicited). U and EOU each used 4 items (7-point scales).

Reliability (Cronbach alpha)

BI: .84 (time 1) and .90 (time 2). Attitude: .85 and .82. U: .95 and .92. EOU: .91 and .90. The two-item usage scale: .79. Subjective norm and the beliefs (bi, ei) were single items, so internal consistency could not be computed.

Main results

QuestionResult
Does intention predict use?BI measured right after the introduction correlated .35 with usage frequency 14 weeks later (R² = .12); BI measured at week 14 correlated .63 (R² = .40). No other variable (A, SN, Σbe, U, E) affected use beyond intention: intention fully mediated.
Comparison with other workNon-IS meta-analysis (Sheppard, Hartwick and Warshaw): frequency-weighted mean intention-behavior correlation .38 (1514 subjects); other IS studies range from −.23 to .79, typically .20 to .30.
Explaining BITRA: R² = .32 (time 1), .26 (time 2). TAM: R² = .47 and .51. In TRA, A had β = .55 and .48 while SN was nonsignificant (.07 and .10). In TAM, U had β = .48 and .61; A had β = .27 at time 1 and was nonsignificant at time 2 (.16).
Explaining attitudeTRA: R² .07 and .30. TAM: R² .37 and .36; U very strong (β = .61 and .50), EOU significant only at time 2 (β = .24).

Belief structure and hybrid models

Factor analysis of the seven TRA beliefs plus the U and EOU items showed dimensions of usefulness (U, and Us: the three specific beliefs about saving time, easier editing and quality), ease of use, dependence (D) and accessibility (ACC). At time 1, Us and U were separate factors but at time 2 they converged into a total usefulness index Ut (alpha .85 and .93). The final models have three constructs:

TimeFinal modelResult
Right after 1 hourBI = Ut + EOUR² = .45; β(Ut) = .62; β(EOU) = .20
After 14 weeksBI = Ut; Ut = EOUR² = .57 (β = .76); EOU affects Ut with β = .24

The authors' interpretation: at first people process ease of use from a self-efficacy view (can I learn it?); later, once they can use the system, this concern fades and ease of use becomes instrumental, acting through usefulness. Novices tend to use abstract, general criteria (U), while specific criteria (Us) gain influence over time as real consequences are learned.

Variance in use explained by EOU when U is controlledDrops by 98%Drops by 91%

Davis's conclusion: usefulness is linked to use more strongly than ease of use in both studies, and the regressions suggest that ease of use may be an antecedent of usefulness (chain EOU → U → use) rather than a parallel determinant. He calls this a preliminary interpretation needing more experimentation. "No amount of ease of use can compensate for a system that does not perform a useful function": difficulty can discourage adoption of a useful system, but ease cannot rescue a useless one. For designers in the human-factors tradition, this implies they have overemphasized ease of use.

6. Limits (by the authors themselves)

  • Generalization: the sample is MBA students (younger, more computer literate, perhaps more motivated), one word processor; with more complex systems ease of use may matter more. Davis (1989): two user groups, two systems and two settings (lab and field) gave similar results, but generality still needs further research.
  • Measurement of use: self-reported usage frequency (not objective, accuracy unknown); U, EOU and use were on the same questionnaire, so a halo effect is possible.
  • Subjective norm: weak scale; word processing is a personal application; the result does not show social norms are unimportant elsewhere.
  • Accessibility: unvalidated scale, and the system was fairly uniformly accessible.
  • Intrinsic motivation and affect: Davis (1989) treats intrinsic motivation and the role of affective attitudes as open questions; both studies found attitude does not fully mediate.
  • Practical meaning: use is only a necessary, not sufficient, condition for performance gains; if a system is not really useful (even if users perceive it to be), it should not be "marketed" to users.
No evidence about AI or learning. The scales and results come from email, editors, graphics packages and MBA students in the 1980s. Applying TAM to an AI assistant teaching programming is design inference; the scales (especially validity) would need re-validation in the new context.

7. Compute

Exercise 1. Intention and use measured together at week 14 correlate r = .63. What percentage of the variance in use is explained (r²×100)? (round to 1 decimal)
.63² = .3969, about 39.7%. The paper gives R² = .40.
Exercise 2. At week 14, TAM explains 51% and TRA 26% of the variance in intention. By how many percentage points does TAM exceed TRA?
51 − 26 = 25 percentage points.
Exercise 3. Davis (1989) sent questionnaires to 120 users at IBM Canada and got 112 responses. What is the response rate in percent? (round to 1 decimal)
112/120 = .9333, about 93.3% (the paper says 93%).
Exercise 4. In Study 1, ease of use correlates r = .45 with use, i.e., explains about 20.25% of the variance. According to the paper, this drops by 98% when usefulness is controlled. What percentage of variance remains? (round to 1 decimal)
20.25 × (1 − .98) = .405, about 0.4%.

8. Interpretation traps

Trap 1: "ease of use does not matter". The papers show ease of use acts indirectly through usefulness, and directly early on (β = .20 after one hour); Davis even suggests it is an antecedent of usefulness.
Trap 2: treating high intention as appropriate use. TAM predicts use, not whether use is effective or beneficial; Davis, Bagozzi and Warshaw: use is necessary but not sufficient for performance gains.
Trap 3: confusing the standard TAM with the original. The 1985 model includes attitude; the 1989 study found attitude only partially mediates; these are different versions.
Trap 4: reading "no effect of subjective norm" as "social influence does not matter". The authors warn of a weak scale and a personal type of application.
Trap 5: trusting scales without re-validation. Alpha of .97 for email and editors does not guarantee the same items stay valid for AI.

9. Link to the thesis

U and EOU of AI in a programming classPerceived usefulness (does AI help me learn programming better?) and perceived ease of use (is using AI effortless?) could be measured as explanatory variables for intention to use. Items need rewriting for "learning" instead of "job performance" and the scales need validation; this is design inference.
Useful for learning or for submitting work?The original definition is "enhancing job performance". For students, "performance" may mean a grade or finished homework rather than learning; TAM measures belief, not whether it is true (Davis, Bagozzi and Warshaw: if a system is not truly useful it should not be marketed). This is where the thesis must add real learning measures.
Acceptance is not appropriate relianceTAM predicts intention to use; Module 10 says use must be appropriate. If the treatment raises U but also raises misuse, TAM alone would not detect it. Both should be measured. This is design inference.
Timing of measurementIn the study, EOU mattered early and then faded; U grew stronger over time. If the thesis measures beliefs after one session and after a semester, different patterns may appear; but this is a result for a word processor and it is unknown whether it holds for AI.
