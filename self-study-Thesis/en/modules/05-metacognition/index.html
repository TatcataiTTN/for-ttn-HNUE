1. Flavell (1979): the original concept

Flavell opens with two findings. First, preschool and elementary children were asked to study a list until sure they could recall it perfectly (Flavell, Friedrichs and Hoyt 1970): older children studied a while, said they were ready and usually were; younger children studied a while, said they were ready and usually were not. Second, in Markman's (1977) study, young children were surprisingly poor at detecting blatant omissions in instructions and wrongly thought they had understood. Flavell concludes that young children are limited in their knowledge and cognition about cognitive phenomena, i.e. their metacognition, and do little monitoring of their memory and comprehension.

Four classes of phenomena. Monitoring of many kinds of cognitive enterprise occurs through the actions of and interactions among four classes: (a) metacognitive knowledge, (b) metacognitive experiences, (c) goals (or tasks), (d) actions (or strategies).

Metacognitive knowledge

That segment of your stored world knowledge that has to do with people as cognitive creatures and with their diverse cognitive tasks, goals, actions and experiences. It consists mainly of beliefs about what factors act and interact in what ways to affect the course and outcome of cognitive enterprises. There are three categories of variables:

CategoryContent and examples in the paper
PersonEverything you could come to believe about yourself and other people as cognitive processors: differences within a person (you learn better by listening than by reading), differences between people (one friend is more socially sensitive than another), and universals (there are various degrees and kinds of understanding; how well you understand now may not accurately predict how well you will understand later).
TaskThe information available (abundant or meager, familiar or not, well or poorly organized) and task demands (it is easier to recall the gist of a story than its exact wording).
StrategyKnowledge of which strategies are likely to be effective for which goals; for example paying attention to main points and repeating them in your own words. There are metacognitive as well as cognitive strategies.

Most metacognitive knowledge concerns interactions among two or three categories, for example "you (unlike your brother) should use Strategy A (rather than B) in Task X (as contrasted with Task Y)". Flavell treats it as not fundamentally different from other knowledge in long-term memory: it can be activated deliberately or automatically, can influence cognition without entering consciousness, and can also be inaccurate, fail to be activated when needed, or fail to have an adaptive effect.

Metacognitive experiences

Any conscious cognitive or affective experiences that accompany and pertain to an intellectual enterprise, such as the sudden feeling that you do not understand something another person just said. They can be brief or lengthy, simple or complex, and occur before, during or after. Flavell guesses they are especially likely in situations that stimulate careful, highly conscious thinking: tasks that expressly demand it, novel roles or situations, weighty and risky decisions, and where high affective arousal or other inhibitors of reflective thinking are absent.

Knowledge and experiences partially overlap: some experiences are knowledge that has entered consciousness (you suddenly recall a similar problem you solved), others are not knowledge (the feeling that you are still far from your goal).

How experiences act

  1. They lead you to establish new goals and revise or abandon old ones.
  2. They affect your metacognitive knowledge: adding, deleting or revising it (Flavell uses Piagetian language: assimilation and accommodation).
  3. They activate strategies aimed at cognitive or metacognitive goals.
Cognitive and metacognitive strategies differ. Cognitive strategies are invoked to make cognitive progress; metacognitive strategies to monitor it. In the paper's example, sensing you do not know a chapter well enough for tomorrow's exam, you read it again (cognitive strategy); asking yourself questions about it and seeing how many you can answer is a metacognitive strategy. One strategy can serve both.

Educational implications and a caution

Flavell raises two open questions: how much good cognitive monitoring actually does, and whether it might do harm if used in excess or nonselectively (the obsessive paralyzed by constant critical evaluation of his own judgments). Despite lack of hard evidence, he is "absolutely convinced" that there is overall far too little, not too much, cognitive monitoring in the world, especially among children. A feasible illustration: Brown, Campione and Barclay trained children (mental age 8) in self-testing strategies; one year later they used them spontaneously and even applied modifications to a different memory task (recalling the gist of prose).

2. Garner and Alexander (1989): facts and open questions

The article summarizes "facts" from over a decade of research and poses four questions to be answered empirically.

"Fact"Content and evidence
1. "Knowing about knowing" developsOlder children make more plans in preparing for retrieval (Kreutzer, Leonard and Flavell 1975); young children often cannot report any strategy when comprehension fails (Myers and Paris 1978). Young children are not without knowledge, only limited: they are novices in the domain of thinking.
2. Children and adults often fail to monitorMuch evidence (listening to instructions, reading text, including adults). Markman: when her mind wanders while reading she understands less and less but keeps turning pages. "Understanding admits of degrees"; children often use a single criterion, understanding individual words (Baker 1984).
3. Some strategies are hard to learn, easy to abandonText reinspection and summarization are not used spontaneously by many students; asked why they do not reread, they say "it is illegal" or it takes too long. They can be taught, but the explanation for abandonment is motivation: classrooms value task completion over quality, and low self-efficacy (Bandura 1977) leaves learners unwilling to try.

Four questions for the next decade

  1. Measure "knowing about knowing" more accurately: problems with interviews (lack of awareness once processes are automated; forgetting over the interval; glib talk about processes not really understood, which Nisbett and Wilson called "telling more than we can know"; guessing what the interviewer wants to hear). Recommended: multiple methods that do not share a source of error (verbal reports, performance, eye movements, underlining, think-aloud, cross-age tutoring).
  2. Measure the effects of strategy instruction: Paris and colleagues argue standardized tests are unsuitable (they measure generalized traits, correlate highly with intelligence, and are timed so they "penalize" strategy users who need extra time); they propose near-transfer tests. Garner and Alexander do not want to drop standardized tests altogether (basketball analogy: practice discrete skills but the game is the real measure) and point to a different flaw in instruction: without explicit teaching for transfer, strategies are "welded" to the original setting.
  3. Relations between content knowledge and strategy knowledge: general strategies can compensate for missing knowledge ("intelligent novices", Brown and Palincsar); a soccer expert reading about soccer needs little monitoring. But Alexander and colleagues (1988) found students weak in both did not benefit from content or strategy instruction, suggesting strategy instruction needs a minimum of content knowledge. Conclusion: isolated research on either is no longer needed; ask about interactions.
  4. Role of achievement motivation: strategy training and attribution retraining may lose effect if the classroom does not support them. Nicholls: task-involved students ask "what must I do differently?", ego-involved students ask "am I stupid?". Ames and Archer (1988), 176 talented students: when students perceived an emphasis on mastery, they reported using more strategies, and this remained significant after controlling for perceived ability.

3. Schraw and Dennison (1994): measuring metacognition with the MAI

The problem: most studies identify highly metacognitive learners by on-line experimental testing (spotting anomalous text, calibrating comprehension) or long interviews, too costly in real classrooms. They built an easily administered instrument for adolescents and adults: the Metacognitive Awareness Inventory (MAI), 52 self-report items.

Theoretical frame. Metacognition is the ability to reflect upon, understand and control one's learning. Two components: knowledge of cognition (declarative: knowledge about self and strategies; procedural: how to use strategies; conditional: when and why) and regulation of cognition (five skills: planning, information management strategies, comprehension monitoring, debugging strategies, evaluation). Eight subcomponents in all.

The two factors correlate (.54 and .45) but there is no sign of a compensatory relation: in Experiment 2, knowledge of cognition was related to reading comprehension scores while regulation of cognition was not.

Experiment 2: validity test

Students completed the MAI, rated their monitoring ability before the test (0 to 100 scale), took four reading comprehension tests (16 items, short passages) from the Nelson-Denny and rated confidence for each item. Monitoring accuracy is the difference between mean confidence and actual proportion correct: above 0 is overconfidence, below 0 underconfidence, 0 perfect accuracy.

  • The group that rated its monitoring ability high (above 80; n = 26) had higher knowledge-of-cognition scores than the group below 65 (n = 38): 81.37 versus 72.86; and answered more correctly (0.72 versus 0.56).
  • The high-performance group (above the 75th percentile; n = 20) scored 85.15 on knowledge of cognition, higher than the middle (77.14) and low (73.30) groups.
  • Monitoring accuracy differed strongly by performance: high group −0.04, middle 0.10, low 0.28; F(2, 107) = 40.69. Poor performers were the most overconfident.
  • A surprise: no significant relation between monitoring accuracy and the MAI, or between monitoring accuracy and pre-test judgments. The authors offer two explanations: large within-group variability, and reading comprehension may reduce individual differences in monitoring because it relies on automated skills.
Lesson for the thesis. Self-report scores (MAI) did not predict actual monitoring accuracy in this experiment. To measure "knowing what you know" you need calibration from confidence and real outcomes, not only questionnaires.

The authors also cite "counterintuitive" findings from earlier work: metacognition is independent of intellectual ability (Swanson 1990) and academic achievement (Pressley and Ghatala 1990); monitoring skills may be independent of, or even negatively related to, domain knowledge (Glenberg and Epstein 1987).

4. Rhodes (2019): monitoring, control and accuracy

A review for psychology instructors. It opens with a student confident about an exam who received a far lower grade: an example of metacognition ("knowledge and cognition about cognitive phenomena", Flavell 1979, p. 906). The focus: metacognition helps only when it is accurate, because monitoring has a causal role in control.

The Nelson and Narens (1990) framework

It distinguishes monitoring (assessing one's learning) from control (regulating learning based on information from monitoring). There are two levels: the object level (behaviors and cognitions as they occur) and the meta-level (a model of the task and processes). Information flows from the object level up to the meta-level (monitoring) and influence flows from the meta-level down (control). Separating the levels resolves "Comte's paradox" (the same person is observer and observed); for example the sentence "Thiss sentence contains threee errors." has two misspellings, but the claim of three errors is a third error on a different level. The framework does not assume the meta-level is accurate: the participant can be treated as an imperfect measuring device of their own cognition.

Measurement: two kinds of accuracy

KindMeaningHow measured, example
Absolute accuracy (calibration)Overall correspondence between judgment and performanceSigned or absolute difference; calibration curve (judgments binned 0 to 10%, ..., 91 to 100%). Foster and colleagues: mean prediction 84.44%, mean score 77.69% over 13 exams, about 7 points overconfident.
Relative accuracy (resolution)Whether judgments discriminate between correct and incorrect outcomesCorrelation between judgment and outcome, commonly Goodman–Kruskal gamma. Koriat and Goldsmith (1996): g = .87 for general-knowledge questions.

Limits of absolute accuracy: changes in performance can change absolute accuracy while judgment stays the same (if judgments stay put and the score rises on a second exam, absolute accuracy "improves" without better monitoring); and it assumes participants can map their monitoring experience onto a percentage scale.

Judgments can be wrong, and wrongness has consequences

The loudness illusion (Rhodes and Castel 2009). Participants heard a word list, some words loud, others at normal volume. They predicted remembering loud words better, but actual recall did not differ. In Experiment 2 they chose to restudy normal words nearly twice as often as loud ones, despite no memory difference. So monitoring affects control causally: an illusion in monitoring leads to a wrong study decision.

Factors that moderate accuracy

  • Task knowledge. Usually goes with higher accuracy; for example Dunning–Kruger: the bottom group estimated 5.5/10 correct, in reality under 1; explained as a "double curse of incompetence" (lacking skill and not knowing it). There are exceptions (Glenberg and Epstein: domain knowledge sometimes negatively related to monitoring in text comprehension), so the relation is not lawful.
  • Prospective versus retrospective judgment. Retrospective judgments (after the task) tend to be more accurate than prospective ones (Dougherty, Scheck, Nelson and Narens 2005).
  • Quality of cues. Koriat's cue-utilization framework: judgments are accurate when the cues used match the factors that affect later performance. Nelson and Dunlosky (1991): 66 word pairs; delayed predictions (minutes later) had g = .90, immediate ones only g = .38, because delay forces retrieval from long-term memory and retrieval success or failure is highly diagnostic.
  • Also: the veracity of metacognitive knowledge about the task, well-defined tasks, and feedback.

Teaching metacognition

Because students' assessments of learning determine how they prepare, make sure those assessments are accurate and free of illusions of learning: gain task knowledge and create environments where high-quality cues disclose mastery (for example extensive self-testing). Three class activities: (1) watch poor television auditions (Lang 2012) to ask whether it matters to know one's own ability; (2) predict and postdict exam scores then compare with actual scores; (3) delayed testing to separate "immediate performance" from "longer-lasting learning", since students often mistake cramming for durable learning (Kornell 2009).

6. Interpretation traps

Trap 1: thinking metacognition is always good. Flavell warns excessive or nonselective monitoring can harm (the obsessive); still, he concludes the world overall has too little monitoring.
Trap 2: confusing "knowing" with "knowing that you know". Metacognition is independent of intellectual ability and achievement (Schraw and Dennison citing Swanson; Pressley and Ghatala); the weakest performers are often the most overconfident.
Trap 3: relying on questionnaires alone. The MAI did not predict monitoring accuracy; Garner and Alexander recommend multiple methods not sharing a source of error.
Trap 4: assuming immediate judgments are accurate. Predictions made right after study are less accurate than delayed ones (g = .38 versus .90).
Trap 5: using Liles (2000) as a conceptual source. It is a short book review in a physiotherapy journal with a single defining paragraph. Cite Flavell (1979) for the concept.

7. Linking to the thesis

AI and the illusion of understandingRhodes's result (the loudness illusion leading learners to restudy the wrong items) shows that the feeling of "I understand" can be driven by cues unrelated to real learning. Clear, fluent AI-written code might be such a cue: this is a design hypothesis, not a conclusion of the paper.
RQ2, measurementCombine three layers: (1) the MAI or a similar questionnaire, with its limits stated (Schraw and Dennison); (2) calibration: have students state confidence (0 to 100%) before each no-AI test, then compute the gap to actual scores and the Brier score; (3) resolution: correlate item-level confidence with correct/incorrect.
Designing the interventionFrom Rhodes: ask for predictions then compare (predict, postdict), delayed self-testing, and feedback on calibration error. These are components that could go into a "responsible" treatment (design inference).
