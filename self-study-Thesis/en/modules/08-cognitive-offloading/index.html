1. Risko and Gilbert (2016): concept and mechanisms

Definition. Cognitive offloading is the use of physical action to alter the information processing requirements of a task so as to reduce cognitive demand. Examples in the paper: tilting your head to perceive a rotated image, programming a smartphone to remind you of an appointment, relying on smartphones and search engines to store and retrieve information.

Our unaided abilities have limits (we accurately perceive only a small region of the visual field and hold limited information in memory). Offloading helps overcome capacity limits, minimize computational effort and achieve feats otherwise impossible; it improves performance in perception, memory, arithmetic, counting and spatial reasoning. The phenomenon is ancient (finger counting, abacuses, knotted quipus) but only recently the target of systematic study. The paper divides offloading onto the body (head tilts, gestures, pointing) and into the world (writing down, typing, sketching).

Three main phenomena

PhenomenonContent and results
External normalizationFaced with a rotated stimulus, people tilt their head to normalize orientation instead of mental rotation. The more disoriented the display or the more items, the higher the rotation cost, and the more likely spontaneous tilting.
Intention offloadingCreating cues in the environment to trigger a delayed intention (sticky notes, leaving an item by the door, reminders). More likely when memory load rises or with interruptions. People less confident in their memory set more reminders, even after controlling for objective ability; the link even holds with confidence in perceptual judgments (domain-general).
Transactive memoryKnowledge distributed across two or more individuals so the system knows more than any one; extended to human-technology systems. Defining trait: a shift from remembering "what" to remembering "where".

Internal demand is not everything

Internal demand strongly predicts offloading, but the two dissociate: when both words and frame are rotated versus only words within an upright frame, rotation costs and a physiological measure of demand are similar, yet spontaneous head tilting is much higher when both are rotated. This is argued to come from an erroneous metacognitive evaluation of demand: people (wrongly) report that word-and-frame rotated displays are more time-consuming, error-prone and effortful.

Superfluous offloading. In a short-term memory task, participants wrote items down about 40% of the time when they needed to remember only 2 items (where performance was already at ceiling), and erroneously judged that writing would improve performance. Three explanations: an undetected benefit; a bias against cognitive effort ("cognitive miser"); an erroneous metacognitive belief.

Costs of offloading

  • Memory (transactive): those who expected information to be saved remembered less than those who expected erasure (Sparrow 2011). Sometimes saving even helps memory for other information (a second list) by reducing proactive interference.
  • Metacognition: searching online makes people believe they have more knowledge "in the head"; people who recently used Google on a quiz reported higher cognitive self-esteem and predicted better performance on the next quiz even without help. But when they knew they could look answers up, they said "don't know" more and reported lower feeling-of-knowing.
  • GPS: drivers following turn-by-turn directions outperformed those with no aid, but had poorer memory for route scenes and drove worse when re-driving without aid.
  • Cameras: photographing an object impaired memory for it a day later; photographing only part of it reduced the cost.
  • Automation: "automation complacency" (insufficient vigilance) and "automation bias" (uncritical reliance on automated output), plus "skill decay".

The metacognitive framework of offloading

The decision to offload is a strategy chosen after metacognitive evaluation (arrow A) of one's internal capacity and "extended" options. Using a strategy alters later evaluations (arrow B) and directly affects lower-level cognition (arrow C). GPS example: judging GPS more accurate than memory, we offload; offloading reduces spatial memory (C) and confidence in it (B), which increases future reliance (A). The model predicts a self-reinforcing loop, a drift away from internal capacity. The paper cites Inuit wayfinding as an example of such drift.

Educational implications

There is longstanding interest in aids that let children offload demand while learning (manipulatives, calculators). Gesture helps children by "lightening the load" and this benefit appears to outstrip that of offloading onto external manipulatives. The key condition: any benefit of offloading holds only if the offloaded demand is unnecessary for the learning goal (distinguishing intrinsic, germane and extraneous load), and what is "saved" must be redistributed productively rather than to superfluous activities. Open questions: long-term effects ("does Google make us stupid?"), individual differences, and whether different forms of offloading are cognitively equivalent.

2. Sparrow, Liu and Wegner (2011): Google effects on memory

Thesis: the Internet has become a form of external / transactive memory, where information is stored collectively outside ourselves. Four studies show that when faced with difficult questions people are primed to think about computers, and that when they expect future access to information they recall the content less but recall where to find it better.

Authors' view. This can be an adaptive use of memory. Experiments 2 and 3 show people forget items they think will be available externally and remember items they think will not. In the conclusion: "we... remember less by knowing information than by knowing where the information can be found". The authors also note caveats in Experiment 4 (a cue for the folder that statements lacked; folders not counterbalanced with statements). The study concerns trivia and lookup, not structured learning.

3. Lee and colleagues (2025): survey of knowledge workers

The study surveyed 319 knowledge workers and collected 936 examples of real GenAI use at work: 374 (39.96%) Creation, 303 (32.37%) Information, 259 (27.67%) Advice. Two questions: when and how do they perceive enacting critical thinking when using GenAI (RQ1), and when and why does effort for critical thinking change (RQ2). Critical thinking is defined through Bloom's taxonomy (knowledge, comprehension, application, analysis, synthesis, evaluation); the paper acknowledges the definition is contested.

Main results. Participants self-reported enacting critical thinking in about 60% (555/936) of examples; overall they perceived critical thinking activities to require less effort with GenAI than without. Strong predictors: self-confidence in the task (more critical thinking) and confidence in GenAI (less). A general tendency to reflect also related to critical thinking; overall trust in GenAI had no significant correlation with enacting it (the authors suggest task-level confidence factors captured that variance).
  • The nature of critical thinking shifts toward information verification, response integration and task stewardship.
  • Motivators (frequency among 319): work quality (74), avoiding negative outcomes (116 mentioned potential negative outcomes), skill development (13).
  • Inhibitors: awareness (for example reliance on AI), motivation (for example lack of time: 44), ability (for example barriers to improving AI output; 58 cited barriers to inspecting responses).
  • Methods: random-intercept logistic and linear regression by participant, multiple-comparison correction (Benjamini–Hochberg, threshold 0.007), plus open-coded qualitative analysis.
Key limit. All variables are self-reported (perceived enaction of critical thinking, perceived effort). The paper opens with Bainbridge's "irony of automation": mechanizing routine tasks and leaving exceptions to the human deprives users of practice, so skills atrophy when exceptions arise. But this study does not measure actual skill.

4. Gerlich (2025): AI, cognitive offloading and critical thinking

A mixed-method study: surveys and in-depth interviews with 666 valid participants (669 collected) in the United Kingdom, recruited by convenience and purposive sampling promoted on social media. Three age groups: 17 to 25, 26 to 45, and 46 and older. A 23-question questionnaire (items from the Halpern Critical Thinking Assessment and Terenzini's self-report measures), 6-step scale; semi-structured interviews with 50 people. The minimum sample was 384 (95% confidence, 5% margin).

Read carefully. This is a cross-sectional survey with self-reported variables and a convenience sample; correlations of −0.68 to −0.75 are very large compared with what is usual in social science, so caution is needed. The author states limitations: "reliance on self-reported measures and the potential for sample bias" and proposes experiments manipulating AI use for causal evidence. Phrasing it as "AI reduces critical thinking" goes beyond what cross-sectional correlation allows (this module's own remark); what the paper shows is an association, not causation. The author also recommends active learning strategies and metacognitive skill development, citing Freeman (Module 07).

5. Kosmyna and colleagues (2025): "Your Brain on ChatGPT"

An unreviewed preprint (distributed under CC BY-NC-SA). Question: the cognitive cost of using an LLM to write an essay. Design: 54 participants in three groups of 18: LLM (ChatGPT), Search Engine, Brain-only. Three sessions with the same group, each choosing one of three SAT essay topics; in session 4 (18 completed) the LLM group wrote without tools (LLM-to-Brain) and the Brain-only group used the LLM (Brain-to-LLM). Four sessions over 4 months. Measures: EEG (neural connectivity), language analysis (NER, n-grams, topics), interviews after each session, scoring by teachers and an "AI judge".

FindingDetail
Brain connectivityConnectivity "scaled down with the amount of external support": Brain-only strongest and widest, Search Engine intermediate, LLM weakest.
Session 4LLM-to-Brain: weaker connectivity and under-engagement of alpha and beta networks; Brain-to-LLM: higher memory recall and re-engagement of occipito-parietal and prefrontal nodes.
Essay ownershipLLM group low; Search Engine strong but less than Brain-only.
Quoting ability (session 1)83.3% (15/18) of the LLM group failed to give a correct quote, versus 11.1% (2/18) in both Search Engine and Brain-only (p < 0.001 for the two comparisons; Search Engine versus Brain-only p = 1.0). On correct quoting: LLM group 0/18 correct; Search Engine 3/18 failed and Brain-only 2/18 failed (as the paper words it).
Language homogeneityWithin each group, essays were homogeneous in named entities (NER), n-grams and topic ontology.
"Cognitive debt"The authors' term: repeated reliance on systems like LLMs replaces the effortful cognitive processes needed for independent thinking; it defers mental effort in the short term but yields long-term costs (diminished critical inquiry, vulnerability to manipulation, decreased creativity).

The paper's design-leaning conclusion: "an educational model that delays AI integration until learners have engaged in sufficient self-driven cognitive effort".

Limitations (stated by the authors). A small sample from a narrow area (a few large institutions close together), not gender balanced; ChatGPT only, so not generalizable to other LLMs; the writing task was not divided into subtasks; EEG reports connectivity without spectral power and has limited spatial resolution; findings are tied to essay writing and may not generalize; the session 4 finding is preliminary (only 18 people). The document itself says it is a preprint. It also includes an energy-cost section (an LLM query is estimated at about 10 times a search query).

6. Compute it yourself

Exercise 1. In Gerlich, the correlation between cognitive offloading and critical thinking is r = −0.75. Compute r² (shared variance, 2 decimals).
0.75² = 0.5625, about 0.56. Note: this is a cross-sectional correlation.
Exercise 2. In Kosmyna, 15/18 of the LLM group could not give a correct quote in session 1 (the paper says 83.3%) versus 2/18 (11.1%) in the other two groups. What is the difference in percentage points? (1 decimal)
83.3 − 11.1 = 72.2 percentage points.
Exercise 3. In Sparrow experiment 4, recall of location was M = 0.49 and recall of statements M = 0.23. By how much is location recall higher?
0.49 − 0.23 = 0.26.
Exercise 4. In Gerlich the indirect effect through offloading is −0.25 and the direct effect −0.17. What is the sum of their absolute values (compare with the total effect)?
0.25 + 0.17 = 0.42, matching the total effect. The indirect share is 0.25/0.42, about 60%.

7. Interpretation traps

Trap 1: treating offloading as always bad. Risko and Gilbert: offloading helps overcome limits and improve performance; what matters is whether the offloaded demand is needed for the learning goal.
Trap 2: confusing correlation with causation. Gerlich is cross-sectional; Lee is self-report; Kosmyna is a small unreviewed preprint.
Trap 3: generalizing from trivia to deep learning. Sparrow used trivia statements with lookup; not structured concept learning.
Trap 4: treating EEG as a "direct measure of learning". Kosmyna reports connectivity only; the authors themselves note limited resolution and scope.
Trap 5: ignoring erroneous metacognitive evaluation. People offload superfluously (writing when remembering only 2 items) and may misjudge both need and benefit.

8. Linking to the thesis

What to offload?From Risko and Gilbert: the benefit exists only when the offloaded demand is unnecessary for the learning goal. In teaching introductory programming, "extraneous" load (typing syntax, looking up APIs) may go to the AI, while load tied to the learning goal (analyzing the problem, planning, debugging) should stay with students. This is design inference and needs testing.
The self-reinforcing loopThe A, B, C model predicts: offload a lot, confidence in internal ability falls, offload even more next time. It can be tested by measuring confidence (Modules 05, 06) and the number of AI queries over time.
A session with AI taken awayKosmyna used session 4 to swap conditions. The thesis should have a no-AI test as the main measure (RQ1) and a task where students quote or re-explain the work they just did, to gauge ownership and recall as an indicator of superfluous offloading.
Do not rely on cross-sectional correlationGerlich gives strong correlations but no causation; the thesis needs comparison groups and repeated measures (Module 13) to avoid this error.
