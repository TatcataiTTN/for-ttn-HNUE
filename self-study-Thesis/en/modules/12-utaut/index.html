12 · UTAUT (Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology)

Module 12/14 · There are too many models explaining why people accept technology. Venkatesh et al. (2003) compare eight models, then unify them into UTAUT: four core determinants (performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, facilitating conditions) and four moderators (gender, age, experience, voluntariness).

Source: Venkatesh, Morris, Davis & Davis (2003) User acceptance of information technology: Toward a unified view, MIS Quarterly 27(3), 425–478. Participants: employees at six organizations (entertainment, telecommunications, banking, public administration, financial services, retail electronics) using business systems introduced around 2003; not AI or learning. 1. Why unify: eight models

The problem. IT acceptance research produced many competing models with different sets of determinants; the models routinely explain over 40% of the variance in intention. Researchers must "pick and choose" constructs or choose a favored model and ignore the others' contributions. The paper has four aims: (1) review eight models; (2) compare them empirically; (3) formulate a unified model; (4) validate it empirically. Since the 1980s, about 50% of organizations' new capital investment has gone to information technology.
ModelCore idea
TRA (theory of reasoned action)Behavior is determined by intention; intention by attitude and subjective norm.
TAM (technology acceptance model; including TAM2)Perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use; TAM2 adds subjective norm, experience and voluntariness.
MM (motivational model)Extrinsic and intrinsic motivation.
TPB/DTPB (theory of planned behavior)Adds perceived behavioral control to TRA; DTPB decomposes the beliefs.
C-TAM-TPBCombines TAM and TPB.
MPCU (model of PC utilization)Job-fit, complexity, long-term consequences, affect toward use, social factors, facilitating conditions.
IDT (innovation diffusion theory)Relative advantage, ease of use, image, visibility, compatibility, result demonstrability, trialability, voluntariness.
SCT (social cognitive theory)Outcome expectations (performance and personal), self-efficacy, affect, anxiety.

The eight models hypothesize between two and seven determinants, 32 constructs in total; four moderators have been found significant alongside them: experience, voluntariness, gender and age.

2. Empirical comparison of the eight models

Design. A longitudinal field study at four organizations among people being introduced to a new technology at work; data collection was timed with a training program. A questionnaire with items from all eight models was given three times: post-training (T1), one month after implementation (T2) and three months after (T3). Usage behavior was measured as duration of use from system logs over the six months after training. Intention used a three-item, seven-point scale. A focus group of five business professionals reviewed the questionnaire.

Manipulation check: perceived voluntariness was very high in study 1 (M = 6.50 and 6.51) and very low in study 2 (M = 1.50 and 1.49), so the data were split into two sets. PLS was used (500 bootstrap samples); 48 separate validity tests; most loadings were ≥ .70 and all internal consistency reliabilities exceeded .70.

Main results

  • All eight models explained acceptance: variance in intention explained ranged from 17% to 42% in the results comparing the original models (the paper's abstract says 17% to 53%).
  • Key difference between studies: social influence constructs were significant in mandatory settings but not in voluntary ones.
  • Determinants of intention changed over time; some went from significant to nonsignificant as experience increased.
  • When data were pooled (N = 645 = 215 × 3) and moderators added, explained variance rose, e.g., TAM2 to 53% and TAM with gender to 52%, versus about 35% in cross-sectional tests without moderators. The authors note that the TAM family has more moderators in the literature, which may have unintentionally biased results in their favor.
  • For use: intention predicted strongly (β about .58 to .61); perceived behavioral control became a direct determinant of use at T3, i.e., with increasing experience.

Three consistent findings leading to UTAUT

  1. Every model has at least one construct significant at all times that is also the strongest: attitude (TRA, TPB), perceived usefulness (TAM, C-TAM-TPB), extrinsic motivation (MM), job-fit (MPCU), relative advantage (IDT), outcome expectations (SCT).
  2. Several constructs were significant at first then lost significance: perceived behavioral control, ease of use, complexity, self-efficacy and anxiety.
  3. Voluntary versus mandatory context affected the significance of social constructs (subjective norm, social factors, image: significant only in mandatory settings).

3. UTAUT: four core constructs and four moderators

The idea. Of the seven constructs that were significant direct determinants in one or more models, the authors theorize that four play a role as direct determinants; attitude, self-efficacy and anxiety are not direct determinants of intention. The construct labels describe their essence, independent of any particular theoretical perspective.
ConstructDefinitionMerged from
Performance expectancyThe degree to which an individual believes that using the system will help him or her attain gains in job performance.Perceived usefulness (TAM, C-TAM-TPB), extrinsic motivation (MM), job-fit (MPCU), relative advantage (IDT), outcome expectations (SCT)
Effort expectancyThe degree of ease associated with use of the system.Perceived ease of use (TAM), complexity (MPCU), ease of use (IDT)
Social influenceThe degree to which an individual perceives that important others believe he or she should use the new system.Subjective norm (TRA, TAM2, TPB, C-TAM-TPB), social factors (MPCU), image (IDT)
Facilitating conditionsThe degree to which an individual believes that an organizational and technical infrastructure exists to support use of the system.Perceived behavioral control (TPB, C-TAM-TPB), facilitating conditions (MPCU), compatibility (IDT)

The hypotheses

HypothesisRelationModerators and direction
H1Performance expectancy → intentionGender, age: stronger for men and younger workers (especially younger men)
H2Effort expectancy → intentionGender, age, experience: stronger for women, older workers and early experience (Table 22). The wording of H2 in the text says "particularly younger women", while the results and discussion say older women
H3Social influence → intentionGender, age, voluntariness, experience: stronger for women, older workers, mandatory settings, early stages
H4aFacilitating conditions → intentionNonsignificant because the effect is captured by effort expectancy
H4bFacilitating conditions → useAge, experience: stronger for older workers with increasing experience
H5a, b, cComputer self-efficacy, computer anxiety, attitude → intentionNo significant direct effect
H6Intention → useDirect effect

Reasoning for each construct

  • Performance expectancy is the strongest predictor at all times in both voluntary and mandatory settings. Gender role theory suggests men are more task-oriented so performance is more salient; younger workers value extrinsic rewards more.
  • Effort expectancy is significant in both settings but only in the early period (T1) and vanishes with sustained use; process issues are hurdles at first and are later overshadowed by instrumentality. More salient for women and older workers (difficulty processing complex stimuli).
  • Social influence works via three mechanisms: compliance, internalization and identification. In mandatory settings it acts directly via compliance; in voluntary settings it acts through perceptions of the technology. The effect fades with experience.
  • Facilitating conditions do not predict intention once performance and effort expectancy are present (Venkatesh 2000: effort expectancy fully mediates); but they have a direct effect on use, especially for older workers as experience grows.
  • Attitude is significant only when performance and effort expectancy are absent from the model: the attitude-intention relation is considered spurious. Self-efficacy and anxiety are distinct from effort expectancy but act indirectly through it.

4. Empirical validation of UTAUT

Initial test (N = 215; T1 data pooled across four organizations)

A measurement model was estimated for the seven direct determinants of intention; all internal consistency reliabilities exceeded .70; only eight loadings were below .70. Reverse-coded affect items were dropped (loadings below .60). Because of sample size, the structural model was re-estimated using the four highest-loading items for each construct (three for intention, one indicator for use). Data from the three time points were also pooled (N = 645) with time (experience) as a moderator.

Hypothesis results: performance expectancy acted through a three-way interaction (gender and age: more salient for younger workers, particularly men), supporting H1; effort expectancy through a three-way interaction (women, older, decreasing with experience), supporting H2; social influence through a four-way interaction (mandatory settings, women, older, early stages), supporting H3; facilitating conditions were nonsignificant for intention (H4a); self-efficacy, anxiety and attitude had no direct effects (H5a, b, c). Power analysis: the likelihood of detecting medium effects was over 95% but small effects under 50%, so "no effect" results should be read with caution.

Use: intention (H6) and facilitating conditions were both significant; the effect of facilitating conditions was moderated by age (stronger for older workers) and stronger with experience (H4b). R² for use was .37 (direct effects only) and .43 (with interactions) at T1; pooled: .38 and .53.

The paper's conclusion. UTAUT explains up to 70% of the variance (adjusted) in usage intention, a substantial improvement over the eight original models and their extensions, while integrating the 32 main effects and four moderators of the eight models into four main effects and four moderators. The authors suggest we may be approaching the practical limits of our ability to explain acceptance and usage decisions in organizations.

5. Limits (by the authors themselves)

  • Scales: for degrees of freedom, each construct used only the highest-loading items; this favors internal consistency but may drop facets (e.g., no MPCU items in performance expectancy). UTAUT's scales are regarded as preliminary and need further development with emphasis on content validity.
  • Outcome variables: intention and use; no link to individual or organizational outcomes (productivity, satisfaction); it is often assumed that use leads to good outcomes, but this is untested.
  • Moderator cells: 24 cells in total (2 levels of voluntariness, 3 of experience, 2 of gender, at least 2 of age) need studies with enough representation in each; the age and gender effects interpreted through gender roles call for research into underlying mechanisms (computer literacy, social and cultural background).
  • Pooling: repeated measures on the same people mean measurement errors may be correlated; the authors ran Chow tests to confirm (Appendices A and B).
  • Non-effects: "not significant" results (H4a, H5) have good power only for medium effects; the authors suggest studying other constructs such as behavioral expectation and habit.
  • Potential bias: because TAM-family models have more moderators in the literature, they may gain an advantage in the comparison.
No evidence about AI or learning. UTAUT was tested on employees using business systems. Applying it to students using an AI assistant is design inference; the moderators (gender, age) may matter differently among university students with a narrow age range; the voluntariness of AI use in class differs from organizational settings.

69 − 27 = 42 percentage points.

7. Interpretation traps

Trap 1: reading 70% as predicting learning. It is the adjusted variance of intention to use with moderators and interactions; main effects alone are about .27 to .30 in pooled data.
Trap 2: treating social influence as always important or never important. It is significant only under conditions (mandatory, early stage, women, older) and not when only main effects are considered.
Trap 3: reading "facilitating conditions do not predict intention" as "infrastructure does not matter". It has a direct effect on use, especially for older workers and with growing experience.
Trap 4: ignoring interactions. The main effect of performance expectancy is not interpretable because of higher-order interactions; interpret by gender and age.
Trap 5: treating "not significant" as "no effect". Power to detect small effects is under 50%.
Trap 6: treating high acceptance as a good outcome. The paper notes it is often assumed that use leads to positive outcomes, but this remains untested.

8. Link to the thesis

Four factors for students' use of AIPerformance expectancy (does AI help me learn programming?), effort expectancy (is AI easy to use?), social influence (do instructors and peers think I should use it?), facilitating conditions (are tools and support available?). Items need rewriting for learning and validation; this is design inference.
Voluntariness in the classroomIn the paper social influence was significant mainly in mandatory settings. In the thesis, decide whether AI use in the treatment group is mandatory or voluntary and measure perceived voluntariness as in the paper (manipulation check); this is design inference.
Timing of measurementEffort expectancy is salient early then fades; performance expectancy is more durable. The thesis should measure beliefs at several points (start, middle, end of term) to see the trajectory; this is design inference based on the paper's pattern.
Acceptance is not appropriate reliance or real learningUTAUT explains intention and use, not whether use is good for learning. The paper itself notes no link to outcomes. The thesis needs appropriate reliance (Module 10) and learning outcomes without AI; this is design inference.
