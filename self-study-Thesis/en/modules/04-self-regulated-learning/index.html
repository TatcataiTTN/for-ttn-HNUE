1. What SRL is

Zimmerman (2002) opens with Tracy, a high-school student with a math exam in two weeks. She studies while listening to music "to relax", sets no goal beyond "trying my best", has no memory strategy, does not plan and so crams a few hours before the exam, has vague self-evaluation standards, blames failure on "lack of math ability", does not ask anyone because she is afraid of "looking dumb", finds studying anxiety-provoking, and has little confidence and little intrinsic interest. She is a mirror image of a self-regulated learner.

A process definition (Zimmerman 2002). Self-regulation is not a mental ability or an academic skill; it is the self-directive process by which learners transform mental abilities into task-related skills. It means "self-generated thoughts, feelings, and behaviors that are oriented to attaining goals". Learning is seen as something students do for themselves in a proactive way, not something that happens to them in reaction to teaching.

Three features across definitions (Zimmerman 1990)

  1. Use of SRL strategies: actions and processes directed at acquiring information or skill, with awareness of the link between strategy and outcome.
  2. A self-oriented feedback loop: learners monitor how effective their learning methods are and react, from changing their self-perceptions to changing their behavior. Bandura adds positive feedback: raising the goal after reaching it.
  3. Motivation: because self-regulation takes preparation time, vigilance and effort, learners may choose not to do it. Learning and motivation are interdependent; skill and will are integrated parts.

In scope, a self-regulated learner is an active participant in metacognition (planning, goal setting, self-monitoring, self-evaluation), motivation (self-confidence, attribution, interest) and behavior (selecting, structuring and creating learning environments; seeking advice; self-instruction and self-reward).

Why it matters

Education aims to develop lifelong learning skills. Zimmerman (2002) cites the workplace (learning a new position by observation and practice), self-employment, and long-term creative projects. Studies of experts (Ericsson and Charness 1994) show they learn and practice about four hours a day and are highly motivated; the amount and quality of practice strongly predict expertise.

2. Zimmerman's three-phase model

PhaseProcess groupSpecific processes and examples in the paper
ForethoughtTask analysisSet specific, near goals (a spelling word list); plan strategy use (split words into syllables)
Self-motivation beliefsSelf-efficacy, outcome expectations, intrinsic interest, learning-goal orientation
PerformanceSelf-controlImagery (remember "pan" as a bread pan), self-instruction, attention focusing (pick a spot away from noise), task strategies (group "pan" with food words)
Self-observationRecord study time; self-experiment (study alone versus with a friend to see if the friend helps or distracts)
Self-reflectionSelf-judgmentSelf-evaluation against standards (earlier level, others, absolute); causal attribution of success and failure
Self-reactionSatisfaction and affect; adaptive reactions (drop or fix a poor strategy) versus defensive ones (drop the subject, skip the test)
Why attribution matters. Attributing a low score to fixed ability lowers motivation because it implies further effort is futile; attributing it to a controllable process (a wrong strategy) preserves motivation because it implies a change of strategy could succeed (Zimmerman 2002).

The cycle has evidence. Self-reactions from one attempt affect preparation for the next: dissatisfaction leads to low self-efficacy and less effort. Zimmerman and Kitsantas (1999) found high correlations among the use of processes across the three phases; students who set specific, near goals tend to self-observe more, achieve more and have higher self-efficacy (Bandura and Schunk 1981).

3. Evidence

Structured interviews about strategies (Zimmerman and Martinez-Pons)

They used structured interviews with scenarios such as "it is hard to finish homework because something more interesting is on: how do you motivate yourself?". Answers were coded into strategy categories: self-evaluating, organizing and transforming, goal setting and planning, seeking information, keeping records, self-monitoring, environmental structuring, self-consequences, rehearsing and memorizing, seeking social assistance (peers, teachers, adults), and reviewing (notes, books, tests). Open interviews were chosen over multiple choice because self-generated answers better simulate natural conditions.

StudySample and result
1986: strategies and track80 high-school students: 40 advanced, 40 lower track. The advanced track reported significantly more use of every strategy except self-evaluating (difference not significant). Discriminant analysis with weighted strategy scores classified 93% of students into the correct track.
1986: non-SRL answersThe lower track often gave "reactive" answers ("I do what the teacher says") and "will" answers ("I try harder"), indicating lack of initiative, not poor verbal skill.
1988: teacher ratings and standardized scoresFactor analysis gave one SRL factor explaining nearly 80% of the variance. Self-reported strategy use correlated .70 with that factor, separate from a general-ability factor.
1990: by grade and giftednessStrategy use rose from grade 5 to 8 and from 8 to 11; gifted students had higher early self-efficacy.

Monitoring outcomes does not happen by itself

Pressley and Ghatala: even when told two strategies differ in effectiveness, adults do not track the difference while doing the task; only when prompted to monitor recall outcomes do adults draw and use the information. Older elementary students draw it but do not yet use it; young children do not draw it. Ghatala and colleagues (1986) showed children must be taught to monitor outcomes, attribute them to strategies and use that information to decide. Elliott-Faust and Pressley (1986): teaching third graders verbal self-instruction and self-monitoring improved reading comprehension and maintenance of strategy use more than teaching the strategy alone.

Development and teaching

According to Paris and Newman (cited in Zimmerman 1990), children under 7 are naive and overly optimistic about their ability to learn, reflect little and think effort is enough (Dweck and Elliott 1983); near adolescence self-perception is more accurate. Zimmerman (2002) points out few teachers prepare students for self-directed learning: they rarely let students choose tasks, methods or partners, and rarely encourage goal setting, strategy instruction or self-evaluation. A common wrong view is that SRL is "asocial": self-regulated students often seek help from others; what makes it "self-regulated" is initiative, persistence and adaptive skill.

Zimmerman 1990's conclusion. Training that targets only one or two processes rarely has lasting effects; all three dimensions should be developed together: metacognitive, motivational and behavioral. Even in training studies it is unclear which component is essential (Sawyer, Graham and Harris 1989: multi-component packages did better, but the added self-regulation part did not reach statistical significance).

4. Five SRL models (Puustinen and Pulkkinen 2001)

The review selects five models that were new or further developed in the previous decade and had plenty of empirical support: Boekaerts, Borkowski, Pintrich, Winne and Zimmerman, and compares them on four criteria: theoretical background, definition of SRL, components, and empirical research.

ModelFocusDistinctive features
Boekaerts (adaptable learning)Appraisal at the center; several control systems (metacognitive, motivational, emotional)Positive appraisal leads to widened resources; negative to ego protection. Two action modes: fast/automatic (familiar situations) and conscious. On-line Motivation Questionnaire (OMQ) before and after a task.
Borkowski (process-oriented metacognition)Strategy selection and use; executive functions; the self-systemStrategy training and transfer research; Reid and Borkowski 1987: self-control training plus attribution gave the best effect, still present 10 months later.
Pintrich (general framework)Four phases: forethought, monitoring, control, reflection; four areas: cognition, motivation/affect, behavior, contextPresented as a table serving as a heuristic; goal-orientation analysis (mastery/performance, approach/avoidance); the MSLQ questionnaire.
Winne (four stages)Task definition; goals and planning; strategy enactment; metacognitive adaptationCOPES structure (Conditions, Operations, Products, Evaluations, Standards) in each stage; metacognitive monitoring at every stage; trace methodology.
Zimmerman (social cognitive)Three cyclical phases; triadic reciprocal determinism (person, behavior, environment)Four development levels: observation, emulation, self-control, self-regulation; the SRLIS interview (14 strategy categories); self-efficacy is the most-studied factor.

Comparison

  • Most alike: Pintrich and Zimmerman (same social-cognitive base, SRL as a goal-directed process, similar research). Most different: Borkowski and Winne, far from the other models in theory.
  • Two kinds of definition: goal-oriented (Boekaerts, Pintrich, Zimmerman) and metacognitively weighted (Borkowski, Winne). On close reading the difference blurs: both assume intrinsically motivated, goal-directed learners; they differ in the weight of each component.
  • Common ground: every model runs from a preparatory phase through performance to evaluation or adaptation, and all are cyclical.
  • Specific differences: Winne has metacognitive monitoring in every phase (recursive); Boekaerts concentrates on the preparatory phase and treats performance lightly.
  • Three research orientations: motivation (Boekaerts, Pintrich), strategies (Borkowski, Winne), both (Zimmerman).
A notable finding from Winne. Winne and colleagues (2000) compared students' self-reports of using seven study strategies with actual traces (highlighting, writing notes): the correlation was low, meaning self-reports do not accurately reflect what learners do. Hadwin and colleagues (1997) found students report different strategies depending on context (reading to learn, writing a short essay, exam review).

5. Compute it yourself

Exercise 1. Self-reported strategy use correlates r = .70 with the SRL factor. Compute r² (shared variance).
0.70² = 0.49, i.e. about 49%.
Exercise 2. Strategy scores classified 93% of students' tracks correctly. What percentage were misclassified?
100 − 93 = 7%.
Exercise 3. Experts learn and practice about 4 hours a day. How many hours per week (7 days)?
4 × 7 = 28 hours per week.
Exercise 4. In the 80-student sample of Zimmerman and Martinez-Pons 1986, 40 students were in the advanced track. What percentage of the sample is that?
40/80 = 50%. Note: the sample is split evenly, so "random guessing" is right about 50% of the time, far below 93%.

6. Interpretation traps

Trap 1: treating SRL as a fixed trait. Zimmerman 2002 is explicit that SRL is not a trait people have or lack; it is the selective use of processes, adapted to each task, and it can be taught.
Trap 2: confusing SRL with studying alone. Self-regulated learners seek help; the "self" is about initiative, not social isolation.
Trap 3: measuring with self-report only. Winne and colleagues found low correlations between self-reports and behavioral traces. Zimmerman also used teacher ratings to validate self-reports.
Trap 4: teaching a single strategy in isolation. Knowing one strategy is not enough; learners must monitor outcomes and decide. Training one or two processes rarely has lasting effects.

7. Linking to the thesis

The treatment model as an SRL cycleYour sequence can be read as three phases: forethought = writing pseudocode and predicting the outcome (goal setting, planning); performance = Socratic questions to the AI, testing (self-control, self-observation); self-reflection = reflection and explanation (self-evaluation, attribution). This is a design mapping, not an empirical result.
RQ2, measurementChoose an SRL instrument and weigh its limits: self-reports (Pintrich's MSLQ, SRLIS-type interviews) may diverge from real behavior (Winne), so combine them with traces: AI interaction logs, number of self-run tests, time spent solving before asking.
FadingZimmerman's four development levels (observation, emulation, self-control, self-regulation) echo cognitive apprenticeship (Module 03): the treatment group should have a structure that moves from AI as a model to students working alone without AI.
