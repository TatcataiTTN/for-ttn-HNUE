1. Bastani et al. (2025): GPT without guardrails can harm learning

Question. Generative AI raises productivity, but how does it affect learning new skills? Learning matters because AI can be wrong (hallucination) and users must check its outputs; without the underlying skills they may lack the expertise to do so. A similar case: overreliance on autopilot led the FAA to recommend that pilots minimise its use (link to Module 10).

Design

ElementContent
SettingA large high school in Turkey, fall 2023–2024; RCT; nearly 1000 students in grades 9, 10, 11 (about 50 classes); four 90-minute sessions, about 15% of the maths curriculum for each grade.
Structure of each session(1) The teacher reviews the topic and solves examples (identical in all arms); (2) assisted practice: students solve problems, the only part randomly intervened on; (3) unassisted evaluation: closed-book, closed-laptop exam, each problem conceptually very similar to a practice problem.
Three arms (assigned by class)Control: only books and notes. GPT Base: ChatGPT-like chat interface using GPT-4, prompt only says to act as a tutor helping students solve problems. GPT Tutor: same interface but the prompt has guardrails: hints without giving the answer directly, plus problem-specific information provided by teachers (correct solutions, common student mistakes and how to give feedback).
RandomisationStudents were randomly assigned to classrooms (honors classes excluded from the main sample); each classroom was assigned one of the three arms; the unit of randomisation is the class; standard errors are clustered by classroom.
ControlsA teacher and a staff member were present so students did not use other apps; work was submitted on paper; independent graders followed the teachers' rubric, with papers spread evenly across arms; scores from both the practice and exam parts counted toward the final grade (incentive compatible).
PreregistrationThe primary analysis was preregistered: comparing unassisted exam scores across arms.

Main results

MeasureGPT BaseGPT Tutor
Effect on practice score (scale 0 to 1; control mean 0.284)+0.137 (SE 0.031), i.e. +48%+0.361 (SE 0.032), i.e. +127%
Effect on unassisted exam score (control mean 0.321)−0.054 (SE 0.022), i.e. −17%, statistically significant−0.004 (SE 0.013), no different from control

Analysis sample: 2848 student-session observations. Intention-to-treat analysis; five sessions did not use the assigned arm because of outside circumstances (for example laptops arriving late).

Mechanism and other analyses

  • GPT Base errors: asked "what is the answer?" ten times for each of 57 practice problems, GPT Base was correct only about 51% of the time; logical errors 42%, arithmetic errors 8%. GPT Tutor does not give the answer and was given the correct solutions.
  • "Crutch": students used GPT Base to ask for and copy solutions; GPT Base's errors lowered this arm's practice scores but no spillover to the corresponding exam problems was found; arithmetic and logical errors had similar effects on practice scores. The authors conclude this suggests students copied answers rather than understanding.
  • Interaction: GPT Tutor students sent more messages and spent more time; in GPT Base most conversation was "repeat the question" or "ask for the answer" (first interaction in session 1: 56% GPT Base vs 42% GPT Tutor; across all session-1 problems: 67% GPT Base vs 37% GPT Tutor).
  • Overly optimistic student perceptions: the GPT Base arm did not feel they did worse or learned less; the GPT Tutor arm did not do better on the exam but felt they did significantly better.
  • Individual differences: little or no evidence of heterogeneous effects by student group (preregistered).
  • Score dispersion: both GPT arms reduced score dispersion in practice (narrowing the skill gap), but had no effect on the exam: the reduced gap did not persist once AI was removed.
Limits (per the paper itself). One topic (maths), two GPT tutors, one school in Turkey; fall 2023 when generative AI was very new; only short-term outcomes; objective grading criteria exist for maths but not for subjects like writing; the "crutch" mechanism needs further controlled experiments. The authors recommend reasonable guardrails, teaching students and teachers how to use it, and considering "co-pilots" for human tutors.

2. Kestin et al. (2025): a research-designed AI tutor vs active learning

Main idea. AI chatbots are generally designed to be helpful, not to promote learning; they are not trained on good pedagogical practice; and they are confidently wrong. The authors built an AI tutor using prompt engineering enriched with targeted content and pedagogical practices, then compared it with an in-class active-learning lesson.

Design

ElementContent
SettingIntroductory physics for the life sciences (PS2) at Harvard, fall 2023; 233 enrolled students; 194 eligible (consented, took part in both formats, completed all pre-tests and post-tests).
Crossover designTwo groups, two lessons in consecutive weeks (surface tension, fluid flow): in week 1 group 1 learned with AI at home while group 2 did active learning in class; the next week they swapped. Every student experienced both conditions; a pre-test, lesson and post-test for each.
RandomisationAt the level of peer-instruction groups (2 to 3 students) rather than individuals, to keep study groups together; a regression clustered at student level; a model clustered at group level gave similar results (p < 0.001).
ControlsIdentical content and worksheets; identical introductions (live vs pre-recorded video); tests built by a team member other than those who designed the AI or taught the lessons, based on learning goals not lesson content; two lessons taught by two different instructors; over 90% of students had not studied the topics in depth; same participation credit for both conditions; students knew pre/post scores did not affect their course grade.
AI tutor ("PS2 Pal")System prompt to promote active learning, manage cognitive load, promote a growth mindset; the platform guides students sequentially through each part of a lesson (a prompt alone could not hold the order); prompts were enriched with step-by-step solutions to avoid hallucination.

Results

MeasureResult
Post-test score (median)AI group 4.5 (N = 142); active learning group 3.5 (N = 174); pre-test baseline 2.75 (N = 316). Mann-Whitney z = −5.6, p < 10⁻⁸. The AI group's median gain was more than double.
Effect sizeLinear regression: 0.63 (underestimated because of a ceiling effect); quantile regression: 0.73 to 1.3 standard deviations.
Time on taskAI group median 49 minutes (70% under 60 minutes, 30% over 60); the in-class group assumed 60 minutes. No correlation between time and post-test score.
Perceptions (5-point Likert)Engagement: AI 4.1 (SD 0.98) vs 3.6 (SD 0.92), t(311) = −4.5, p < 0.0001. Motivation: AI 3.4 (SD 1.0) vs 3.1 (SD 0.86), t(311) = −3.4, p < 0.001. Enjoyment and growth mindset: no significant difference.
Quality of explanations83% of students said the AI tutor's explanations were as good as or better than the in-class instructor's.

Seven pedagogical practices and the explanation

(i) Facilitating active learning; (ii) managing cognitive load; (iii) promoting a growth mindset; (iv) scaffolding content; (v) ensuring accuracy of information and feedback; (vi) targeted and timely feedback; (vii) allowing self-pacing. Practices (i) to (iii) were built in through the prompt; (iv) through the platform; (v) through written solutions; (vi) and (vii) are hard to provide in an ordinary class: the authors attribute the better AI learning largely to personalised on-demand feedback and self-pacing. In the in-class group, the students who found the class "too fast" (3.8%) all spent more than the median time with the AI; those who found it "too slow" (2.2%) all spent less than the median.

Limits (per the paper itself). Applies to a first encounter with content at the understand, apply and analyse levels (Bloom: 33% analyse, 41% apply, 21% understand, 4% remember); one subject, one school, short term; a ceiling effect means the true benefit may be larger; results may depend on high-quality videos, a model like GPT-4, question-specific prompts written by experienced instructors, a structured framework, and a non-homogeneous student population. The authors do not claim structured AI tutoring always beats active learning, for example for complex synthesis and higher-order critical thinking; they advise not using AI where students may use it as a "crutch" to circumvent critical thinking; they propose a flipped-classroom use (AI before class; class time for higher-order skills) and not replacing in-person teaching. Preparing each lesson took a few days for prompts; the software platform took several months.

Careful reading (design inference): the two papers do not fully contradict each other because they measure different conditions: Bastani compares GPT Base and GPT Tutor with no AI on an exam without AI; Kestin compares one carefully designed AI tutor with active learning, and both sides use on-site tests. Still, the differences in context (level, subject, scale, software, motivation when answering, amount of prompt preparation) mean we cannot conclude that Bastani's GPT Tutor is "better" or "worse" than Kestin's tutor; and neither studies programming.

4. Kasneci et al. (2023): opportunities and challenges of large language models

Type of paper. This is a position paper, not an experiment. The authors set out the benefits and challenges of LLMs in education from the learner and teacher perspectives; they argue that a set of competencies and literacies is needed to understand the technology and its limits, a clear strategy and a pedagogical approach focused on critical thinking and fact-checking; they conclude that the risks are manageable if handled sensibly.

Opportunities

GroupExamples in the paper
Primary school studentsReading and writing, questions and prompts for critical thinking, summarising and explaining complex texts.
Secondary school studentsLanguage and style learning, generating exercises and quizzes, explanations and step-by-step solutions.
University studentsSummaries and outlines, research sources and topics, writing support.
Groups and distance learningDiscussion structure, immediate feedback, joint-writing suggestions, level-appropriate questions and tasks.
People with disabilitiesCombining speech-to-text or text-to-speech, with the help of experts.
Professional trainingField-specific language, programming, report writing, project management.
TeachersPersonalised learning, lesson planning, language teaching, research and writing, professional development, semi-automated assessment.

Studies reviewed

  • Models: GPT (2018), BERT (2018), XLNet (2019), RoBERTa (2019), T5 (2020), GPT-3 (175 billion parameters), BLOOM (open source, 46 natural languages and 13 programming languages, 1.6 TB).
  • Learners: generating reading-comprehension multiple-choice questions with GPT-3 (Dijkstra et al.); a GPT-3 agent stimulating children's curiosity (Abdelghani et al.); GPT-3 explaining code (MacNeil et al.: computing education); Bhat et al. (data science); Jia et al. (BERT assessing peer reviews); ChatGPT near the passing threshold of the US medical licensing exam (Kung et al.).
  • Teachers: grading chemistry answers with fine-tuned GPT-3 (Moore et al.); adaptive feedback (Zhu; Sailer); grading effort reduced by up to 85% with NLP feedback (Bernius et al.); Codex generating programming exercises with solutions, automated tests and code explanations; Blender and GPT-3 replied to students adequately but remained "well behind human performance" when helping students.
  • Teachers' views: a pilot survey of European teachers: positive attitude but low AI skills; constructivist teachers integrate more easily than transmission-oriented ones; perceived usefulness, ease of use and trust are determinants of acceptance (link to Modules 11 and 12).

Challenges and risks (section 4 of the paper)

RiskMitigation named (examples)

Interface and fair access

  • Interface: human-computer interaction research is needed; suitable for the users' age and ability; design should foster 21st-century skills (critical thinking, creativity, communication, collaboration).
  • Multilingualism and fair access: most LLM research is on English, which can create unfair access for non-English speakers; financial disparities may require government regulation; without fair access the technology "may seriously widen the education gap like no other technology before it"; the paper cites UNESCO's call that AI must not widen the technological and educational divides.
Limits. The paper is a position and overview piece, mainly about GPT-3 and ChatGPT in early 2023; the "mitigations" are proposals, not empirical evidence; many studies cited are pilots or content generation and do not measure learners' learning.

5. Holmes et al. (2022): the ethics of AI in education

Main idea. Research on AI in education (AIED) has at its core the ethical motive of supporting learning, but as seen in other AI domains, ethical intentions are not by themselves sufficient: fairness, accountability, transparency, bias, autonomy, agency and inclusion must be considered. One must distinguish "doing ethical things" from "doing things ethically", make ethical pedagogical choices, and allow for the ever-present possibility of unintended consequences. The authors repeat an earlier observation that no framework, guidelines, policy or regulation has been issued for the specific ethical issues of AI in education.

Method

  • Invited 60 leading researchers of the AIED community (chosen by citations and standing, plus a few early- and mid-career researchers with clear impact), received 17 responses; the questionnaire had 10 open questions; quotes verbatim and with consent; approved by the Open University research ethics committee; seven authors were among the respondents (conflicts of interest declared).

Main results

  • Enough attention to ethics? All 17 respondents answered, showing overall agreement that there is room for improvement; 5 of 17 saw growing urgency in the community; only 1 outright disagreed (but only spoke about research ethics via ethics boards); 4 were "on the fence". One said: "collectively we lack the training in frameworks and principles that would allow us to grapple with major ethical questions" (Walker).
  • Most important issues: 9 respondents directly referenced "data" (ownership and control, privacy expectations, data limits and bias, informed consent, transparency), along with educational quality, agency and fairness.
  • Most ignored issues: data (collection, ownership, anonymity), representativeness and equity (for example personalisation may overlook underserved groups), and human agency, such as the misplaced belief that AIED can wholly replace human teachers.
  • What distinguishes AIED ethics: AI applied to children and young people (consent, long-term impact), but two respondents noted AIED decisions are usually not "life and death" like self-driving cars or medicine; one suggested teachers may prefer a less accurate but more transparent system.
  • Teaching ethics to AIED students: many said few are trained in ethics; suggestions to work with ethics experts; learning by doing (ethical considerations in the evaluation criteria of products); even letting students try a tool that ignores ethics to see the consequences.
  • Requiring an ethics statement for every journal paper: of 16 respondents, only 2 disagreed (mainly because the ethics of AIED is not yet defined sufficiently to be useful); most supported it but feared it becoming a "tick box".

Proposed preliminary framework (Figure 1, "strawman")

Three foundational foci: data, computational approaches, and education; a second layer is the overlaps (data ethics in AI generally, data ethics in education, algorithmic ethics applied in education, the least developed area); at the centre are the "unknown unknowns" at the intersection of data, computation, education and the interaction between AI systems and individual cognition. The ethics of education includes: the purpose of learning (exams or self-realisation), the choice of pedagogy, the role of technology relative to teachers (replace or augment), and access to education.

  • Reference frameworks mentioned: Aiken and Epstein (2000): a negative principle (technology should not diminish the student along fundamental dimensions of human being) and a positive one (it should enhance at least one dimension), ten principles including that technology should give teachers new roles and not try to replace them; the Montréal Declaration (ten principles); the DELICATE checklist for learning analytics; the World Economic Forum's RESET principles (safety, efficacy, data protection, agency, accountability, transparency, fairness, explainability, integrity, inclusion).
  • Three levels of ethics: ethics of AIED research; of tools and practices in and outside classrooms; of commercial products. A suggestion to provide pros and cons in the style of ingredient lists and allergy warnings on food.
  • AIED is a design science: it aims at changing users' behaviour, often at key moments of intellectual development, which adds ethical responsibility; human-centred design should be used (though it can also cause harm).
  • Ethics is not only preventative: the ethical cost of inaction must also be weighed against the benefits of innovation; the framework should be both protective and facilitative; and there is a risk of "ethics washing" and of stifling innovation.
Limits. A small survey (17 of 60 invited), of leading researchers, some of whom are also authors; an open qualitative survey that does not measure learning outcomes; the framework is an untested "strawman" draft.

6. Jobin, Ienca and Vayena (2019): a global map of AI ethics guidelines

Question. Over the past five years many companies, research institutes and public bodies have issued principles and guidelines for "ethical" AI. Is a global consensus emerging? These are "soft law" (not legally binding but persuasive), unlike "hard law" (binding regulation). The authors carried out a scoping review of grey literature.

Method

StepResult
Strategy 1: four link hubs68 sources; 30 eligible; 27 after removing duplicates.
Strategy 2: Google keyword search (incognito)Tracking the top 30 results: 10 additional sources (AI principles) and 3 additional (articles mentioning principles); results up to the 200th: 15 more non-duplicate documents.
Strategy 3: citation chaining17 more sources.
Monitoring until 23 April 201912 new sources added.
Total84 documents (27 + 10 + 3 + 15 + 17 + 12).

Criteria: written in English, German, French, Italian or Greek; issued by institutional entities from both the private and public sectors; explicitly referring to AI or related notions in the title or description; expressing a normative ethical stance. Content analysis: two researchers independently, two cycles of coding and one cycle of code mapping in NVivo; 3457 codes, of which 1180 related to ethical principles; 13 groups, merged into 11 principles; consistency checked through reflective equilibrium.

Who issued them, where, for whom

  • 88% were released after 2016.
  • Type of organisation: private companies 19 (22.6%) and government agencies 18 (21.4%); academia and research 9 (10.7%); intergovernmental or supranational organisations 8 (9.5%); non-profits and professional associations 7 each (8.3%); private-sector alliances 4 (4.8%).
  • Geography: the USA 21 (25%) and the UK 13 (15.5%) account for more than a third; Japan 4; Germany, France and Finland 3 each; the European Union 19 in total. Africa and South America are not represented independently of international organisations; Central Asia is also missing.
  • Audience: multiple stakeholders 27 (32.1%); self-directed 24 (28.6%); public sector 10 (11.9%); private sector 5 (6.0%); not stated 13 (15.5%).

Eleven principles (by number of documents out of 84)

PrincipleDocumentsMain idea in the paper
Transparency73/84 (86.9%)Explainability, interpretability, disclosure; to minimise harm, build trust, legal reasons; forms: disclosure, audits, monitoring, dialogue.
Justice and fairness68/84Fairness, preventing bias and discrimination, diversity and inclusion, right to appeal and redress, fair access.

Beneficence41/84Often mentioned but seldom defined; human wellbeing, prosperity, peace.

Trust28/84Responsibility of developers too; one guideline warns against excessive trust in AI; ways to build it: education, reliability, accountability.

Discussion and limits (per the authors themselves)

  • Conditional convergence: no principle appears in all documents; there is convergence around five principles (transparency, justice and fairness, non-maleficence, responsibility, privacy) named in more than half; but there are substantive divergences in interpretation, why they matter, the issue, domain or actors, and how to implement them.
  • More emphasis on prevention than promotion: non-maleficence (60) outnumbers beneficence (41) (in another OCR copy of the paper: about 1.5 times more); possibly negativity bias or a way to restrain developers.
  • Power imbalance: more developed countries shape the debate; Africa, South and Central America and Central Asia are missing; risk of overlooking local knowledge and cultural diversity.
  • Conflicting principles: the need for larger, more diverse data to reduce bias can conflict with privacy and autonomy; avoiding harm at all costs versus accepting some harm when balancing risk and benefit; risk-benefit assessment depends on "optimal for whom".
  • Underrepresented: sustainability, dignity and solidarity.
  • Methodological limits: grey literature is harder to replicate; language bias (towards English); limitations of qualitative content analysis; new documents may appear after the search.
Limits for use in the thesis. The map describes what documents say, not how they are implemented or how well they work; it is not specific to education; and soft law is not empirical evidence.

8. Interpretation traps

Trap 1: "AI makes learning worse". In Bastani only GPT Base (no guardrails) lowered the exam score without AI; GPT Tutor did not differ from control; Kestin found a well-designed AI tutor produced more learning.
Trap 2: "AI makes learning better". Kestin is one subject, one school, short term, for first-time content; Bastani found no exam improvement even with GPT Tutor although practice rose.
Trap 3: confusing scores with AI for learning. Bastani: practice scores rose 48% to 127% but the unassisted exam did not rise.
Trap 4: trusting students' perceptions. In Bastani, students did not notice they did worse (GPT Base) and felt better (GPT Tutor).
Trap 5: treating a position paper as evidence. Kasneci is a position paper; the mitigations are proposals.
Trap 6: reading Jobin's "convergence" as "consensus in implementation". The paper stresses large divergences in interpretation and implementation.
Trap 7: extrapolating to programming. Both RCTs are maths and physics; none studies programming.

9. Link to the thesis

Guardrails as an intervention variableBastani shows that the same model with a different prompt yields different learning results. The thesis's "responsible AI" model can be defined through concrete guardrails (hints instead of answers, solutions and common mistakes provided by the instructor) and compared with unguarded AI and no AI. This is design inference; there is no evidence for programming yet.
Measuring outcomes without AIBoth Bastani (closed-book exam) and the thesis's measurement concept (RQ1) stress distinguishing performance with AI from independent learning. Add a with-AI score to understand the gap, and make it incentive compatible (Bastani counts exam scores toward the final grade; Kestin used tests that do not affect the grade, a difference worth noting). This is design inference.
Pedagogical practices in designing the interventionKestin's seven practices (active learning, cognitive load, growth mindset, scaffolding, accuracy, timely feedback, self-pacing) can frame the description of the thesis's AI tutor; link to Modules 01, 02, 03 and 05. This is design inference.
Appropriate reliance and hallucinationGPT Base was right 51% of the time and students copied answers; link to misuse in Module 10. The thesis can measure the rate of accepting wrong AI answers and rejecting right ones, and observe "shallow interaction" (asking for the answer) versus "not shallow", as Bastani did. This is design inference.
Research ethics and Jobin's principlesThe 11 principles can yield criteria for the thesis: transparency (state AI limits), fairness (check results by student group), non-maleficence (measure harms such as dependence on AI), responsibility (who is responsible when AI is wrong), privacy (conversation data), trust (do not maximise trust; design for appropriate trust). Holmes reminds us that good intentions are not enough and the ethics of education must be considered. This is design inference.
Reporting by CONSORTThe two RCTs are examples to read with the Module 13 checklist: preregistration, cluster randomisation, attrition, analysis by assigned group, primary outcome. This is design inference.
