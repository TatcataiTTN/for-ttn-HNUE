13 · Quasi-experiment and RCT (Experimental design)

Module 13/14 · The randomised controlled trial (RCT) is regarded as the gold standard for evaluating interventions, but only when properly designed, conducted and reported. CONSORT 2010 is the reporting guideline: 25 items and a flow diagram. This module reads the CONSORT 2010 statement (Schulz, Altman, Moher) and links it to the thesis's experimental design.

1. Why CONSORT

The idea. Randomised controlled trials, when appropriately designed, conducted and reported, represent "the gold standard in evaluating healthcare interventions". But randomised trials can still yield biased results if they lack methodological rigour. To assess a trial accurately, readers need complete, clear and transparent information on its methods and findings; but authors of many reports neglect to describe that critical information lucidly. This lack of adequate reporting fuelled the creation of CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) in 1996 and its revision five years later; those statements improved reporting quality for some trials but many reports remained inadequate; together with new methodological evidence since 2001, this led to CONSORT 2010.

Intent of CONSORT 2010

  • Components: the CONSORT 2010 Statement is this paper, the 25-item checklist (Table 1) and the flow diagram (Fig. 1).
  • Scope: guidance for all RCTs, but focused on the most common type: individually randomised, two-group, parallel trials. Other designs (cluster, non-inferiority) need extra information; CONSORT extensions exist for them.
  • Explanation and elaboration article: explains the rationale of each item, methodological background and published examples of transparent reporting.
  • Reporting, not design: CONSORT 2010 has no recommendations on designing, conducting and analysing trials; it addresses only reporting what was done and found.
  • Indirect effect: transparent reporting reveals deficiencies if they exist, so investigators who conduct inadequate trials but must report transparently cannot easily pass through publication without their inadequacies being revealed; this is a secondary indirect goal. CONSORT can also help researchers when designing.

3. Background and development

  • Background: from the mid-1990s, efforts to improve RCT reporting accelerated, partly spurred by methodological research: empirical evidence showed that poorly conducted or reported aspects of trials were associated with bias. Two initiatives led to the first CONSORT (1996), organised by Moher and Rennie; revised in 2001.
  • Evidence base: more than 700 studies in the CONSORT database; the group monitors the literature continually; items are added, dropped or modified based on evidence and recommendations of an international group of clinical trialists, statisticians, epidemiologists and biomedical editors.
  • Support: more than 400 journals worldwide explicitly support it; thousands more implicitly via the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE); the Council of Science Editors and the World Association of Medical Editors officially support it. When authors and journals use CONSORT, reporting seems to improve.
  • The 2010 version: 31 members of the CONSORT Group met in Montebello, Canada, in January 2007 to update the 2001 statement; some had primary responsibility for synthesizing evidence on particular items. After seven major iterations of the checklist, feedback from the larger group, two in-person meetings of the executive, drafts and finalisation. The executive then drafted an updated explanation and elaboration.
  • Minimum criteria: only items deemed absolutely fundamental were kept; some important items were not included, e.g., institutional ethical review approval, because funders strictly enforce ethical review and journals usually address it in author instructions. Some items seem desirable, such as reporting on-site monitoring, but a lack of empirical evidence or consensus cautions against inclusion.

4. Changes in CONSORT 2010

Nature. Changes are evolutionary, not revolutionary; the flow diagram changed by one word. Items kept their old numbers except renumbering of items 2 to 5 and inserted new items.

General changes (Box 1)

  • Simplified and clarified wording (items 1, 8, 10, 13, 15, 16, 18, 19, 21).
  • Consistent style by removing the imperative verbs of the 2001 version.
  • Greater specificity of appraisal by breaking some items into sub-items, because many journals expect authors to give a manuscript page for each item. For example, item 4 covers both eligibility and settings; in 2001 an author could give a page number while having reported only eligibility.

5. Implications and limits (by the authors themselves)

  • Intended users: authors writing reports, editors and peer reviewers, and readers appraising articles. Use it together with the explanation and elaboration article.
  • Design: focused on the two-group parallel trial (over half of trials in the literature), but most items apply to all randomised trials; other types or situations need extra information and extensions (cluster, non-inferiority and equivalence, non-pharmacological treatments, herbal, pragmatic).
  • No rigid structure: the authors intentionally declined to impose a rigid article structure; SORT tried a rigid format and failed in a pilot with an editor and authors. Items just need to be addressed somewhere with ample detail and lucidity.
  • Risk: a reporting guideline might encourage some authors to report fictitiously the information suggested rather than what was done; authors, reviewers and editors should be vigilant, e.g., checking protocols, trial registers and regulatory agency websites.
  • Not a quality tool: items ask for clear statements of what was done and found, with no judgment on what should have been done; thus CONSORT 2010 "is not intended as an instrument to evaluate the quality of a trial" and it is inappropriate to construct a "quality score" from the checklist.
  • Recommendation: researchers should begin trials with the end publication in mind. Poor reporting lets authors escape scrutiny of weak aspects; with wide adoption of CONSORT, most authors will have to report all important aspects transparently, and the scrutiny rewards well-conducted trials and penalises poorly conducted ones.
  • This version supplants 2001; it is an "evolving" guideline needing continual reappraisal. The EQUATOR Network helps develop and disseminate reporting guidelines.
Limits of the source. CONSORT 2010 is written for medical trials and says nothing about education or AI. The ideas applied to a programming-class design in this module are design inference.

6. Quasi-experiment: what the source does not contain

This module's source folder holds only CONSORT 2010; Campbell and Stanley's (1963) paper on experimental and quasi-experimental designs has not been downloaded. So the module cites no content on quasi-experiments. What can be said without a source citation (design inference): when the thesis cannot randomise (e.g., must keep existing classes intact), the design becomes quasi-experimental, and many of CONSORT's transparency items (participants, interventions, pre-specified outcomes, participant flow, baseline data, limitations and generalisability) remain useful as a reporting checklist; but the randomisation items (8 to 10) do not apply, and other threats (e.g., baseline differences between groups) need separate handling. Obtain Campbell and Stanley before drawing conclusions on quasi-experiments in the thesis.

8. Compute

Exercise 1. The CONSORT 2010 checklist has 25 items. Items 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 11, 12, 13, 14 and 17 each have two sub-items (a and b); the other 13 items have none. How many sub-items (lines) are in the checklist in total?
12 × 2 + 13 = 24 + 13 = 37.
Exercise 2. The first CONSORT appeared in 1996 and the update is 2010. How many years lie between the two versions?
2010 − 1996 = 14 years.
Exercise 3. (Illustrating item 7a; not from the paper.) To detect d = 0.5 with two-sided α = .05 and 80% power, what is the sample size per group under the normal approximation n = 2(z.975 + z.80)²/d²? Use z.975 = 1.96 and z.80 = 0.8416; round up.
2 × (1.96 + 0.8416)² / 0.25 = 2 × 7.849 / 0.25 = 62.8, rounded up to 63 per group.
Exercise 4. (Illustrative example for item 13, not real data.) 120 students are randomised (60 per group); group A loses 6 and group B loses 9 before the primary outcome is measured, with no other exclusions. How many people have data to analyse in total?
120 − 6 − 9 = 105 (group A: 54; group B: 51). Item 13b asks to report these losses with reasons.

9. Interpretation traps

Trap 1: treating CONSORT as guidance for designing a good trial. The paper says it is only about reporting, with no recommendations on design, conduct or analysis.
Trap 2: computing a "CONSORT score" as a quality score. The paper says it is inappropriate to construct a "quality score".
Trap 3: assuming complete reporting means an unbiased trial. Transparent reporting only reveals weaknesses; it cannot repair a poor design.
Trap 4: applying medical CONSORT directly to education without extensions. The paper names extensions (cluster, non-inferiority, non-pharmacological, pragmatic); classrooms often randomise by cluster (design inference).
Trap 5: changing the primary outcome after seeing the data. Items 6b and 24 (protocol) exist to deter selective outcome reporting.
Trap 6: using "intention to treat" loosely. The paper replaced it with a request to state whether analysis was by original assigned groups (item 16).

10. Link to the thesis

Use CONSORT as a design-stage checklistAlthough the paper is only about reporting, writing out each item in advance (participants, interventions detailed enough to replicate, pre-specified primary and secondary outcomes, sample size, allocation method, blinding, analysis) reveals gaps at design time. This is design inference, since the paper says CONSORT can help design but is not a design guide.
Pre-register primary outcomes and the protocolItems 6, 23 and 24 address selective reporting. The thesis should fix the primary outcome in advance (e.g., a test score without AI), secondary outcomes (reliance, self-efficacy, acceptance) and the analysis plan, distinguishing pre-specified from exploratory analyses (item 18). This is design inference.
Randomise by class or by studentIf groups are assigned by class, it is a cluster trial; CONSORT says extensions are needed for this design. If randomisation is impossible, state clearly that it is quasi-experimental and handle selection bias; this paper has no content on that (see the quasi-experiment section).
Flow, attrition and harmsReport numbers at each stage (item 13), reasons for losses, and harms or unintended effects (item 19), e.g., students becoming dependent on AI or scoring lower without it. This is design inference.
