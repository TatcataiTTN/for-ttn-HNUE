1. Concept: the order of "hard" and "taught"

Kapur (2014) names the familiar sequence Direct Instruction (DI): teach the concept and procedures first, then set problems. The reverse sequence, solve problems first, then teach, he calls Productive Failure (PF). "Failure" has a narrow meaning here: students typically do not find the correct solution themselves. If they can use prior knowledge to generate suboptimal or even wrong solutions, the process is still useful because it prepares them to learn better from the instruction that follows.

The two phases of PF (Kapur 2016): (1) the problem-solving phase: students generate and explore many solutions to a complex problem containing an unlearned concept; (2) the consolidation phase: the instructor compares, organizes and assembles the students' solutions into the canonical solution. Without phase 2 it is no longer PF.

The four cells (Kapur 2016)

Kapur builds on Schmidt and Bjork (1992): performance during learning and long-term learning do not move together. From this he derives four design possibilities.

CellMeaningExample in the paper
Productive successStructure activities for good early performance and durable learningPBL and guided inquiry with scaffolds that fade
Productive failureLow early performance but better long-term learningSolve on an unlearned concept, then consolidate
Unproductive successHigh early performance, illusion of learningRote learning, drill-and-practice
Unproductive failurePoor in both the short and long termPure discovery with no guidance

Kapur 2016, pp.1–2. Example from Miller & Gildea (1987): children who learned word meanings from dictionary definitions misused "correlate" in real sentences, a picture of unproductive success.

2. Evidence: Kapur 2014

The paper reports two randomized controlled experiments at the same private school in the national capital region of India, with ninth graders (14–15 years old) who had not yet learned standard deviation. One shared task: "Who is the most consistent basketball player?", with the points scored by Mike and Dave over 20 games; students had to design as many measures of consistency as they could. The instruction phase used the same teacher and four problems with worked examples and feedback. Each phase lasted 1 hour; both groups had the same time, the same number of problems and the same materials.

Study 2: adding a "vicarious failure" group (VF, n=111)

The Vicarious Failure (VF) group did not solve the problem but studied and evaluated six solutions produced by Study 1 students (turned into clear worked examples). The aim: test whether generating solutions is necessary, or whether being "warmed up" by others' solutions is enough.

Kapur 2014, Table 2. Pairwise: PF beats DI on conceptual d=2.25 and transfer d=1.29; PF beats VF on conceptual d=1.35 and transfer d=1.23; VF beats DI only on conceptual (d=0.80).

Reading the table: both "warm-up" conditions beat DI on conceptual understanding, but only PF (generating solutions) beats it on transfer. Generating solutions has its own value that reading others' solutions does not replace.

Process correlation. In PF, the number of solutions students generated correlates with conceptual understanding (r=0.82) and transfer (r=0.88) in Study 2; in Study 1 the values are 0.65 and 0.81. There is no such correlation in DI, and the number of valid or invalid evaluations in VF does not predict outcomes. Kapur uses the number of solutions as an indicator of the degree of prior-knowledge activation and differentiation.

The original 2008 study (Cognition and Instruction)

Kapur 2008 was the first "existence proof": eleventh graders learned Newtonian kinematics in a CSCL environment, randomly assigned to two conditions. Group 1 solved ill-structured problems in groups and then well-structured problems individually; group 2 solved well-structured problems in groups and then individually. Finally everyone solved ill-structured problems individually. The ill-structured groups struggled to define the problem and their group solutions were poor, but they later outperformed the other group on near and far transfer measures. This is the origin of the name "productive failure".

3. Mechanism, and the debate with Cognitive Load Theory

Kapur 2014 proposes these mechanisms: (a) solving first activates and differentiates prior knowledge (even wrong solutions); (b) students notice what they are missing; (c) comparing their own solutions with the canonical one helps them attend to critical features; (d) students who act with agency are more engaged. On the other side, Kirschner, Sweller and Clark (2006) argue that novices have limited working memory, so trial-and-error search overloads them; give maximal guidance (Module 02).

Kapur 2016's answer has three parts:
  1. "No benefit comes from unguided problem solving"? False: the right comparison is with "solve first, then teach". Klahr and Nigam's (2004) study actually contains a "baseline" phase like a discovery phase, so the effect credited to DI may be the effect of discovery plus instruction.
  2. "Because pure discovery is poor, maximal guidance is the answer"? No: Mayer (2004) shows guided discovery beats both extremes.
  3. "CLT opposes unguided problem solving when learning something new"? CLT assumes learners have no prior knowledge; if a task is designed to elicit prior knowledge (even wrong), the expandable part of working memory can be used.

Kapur 2016, sections on Unproductive Failure and Productive Failure. Kapur 2014 also cites the expertise reversal effect (Kalyuga et al. 2003): learners with more prior knowledge may benefit from more cognitively demanding activities.

4. Calculate: effect sizes from Kapur's tables

Cohen d with pooled SD
d = (M₁ − M₂) / sp  ·  sp = √[ ((n₁−1)s₁² + (n₂−1)s₂²) / (n₁+n₂−2) ]
  • M₁, M₂the two group means (here PF and DI)
  • s₁, s₂standard deviation of each group
  • n₁, n₂number of students per group
  • sppooled SD, a degrees-of-freedom-weighted average
Exercise 1. Study 1, conceptual understanding: PF M=6.33, SD=1.25, n=37; DI M=3.84, SD=1.24, n=38. Compute the pooled SD sp (3 decimals).
Solution: (36·1.25² + 37·1.24²)/73 = (56.25 + 56.891)/73 = 1.5499; square root = 1.245.
Exercise 2. With sp from Exercise 1, compute Cohen d for conceptual understanding (PF − DI).
d = (6.33 − 3.84)/1.245 = 2.00, matching the d=2.00 Kapur reports.
Exercise 3. In Study 2, r=0.82 between the number of solutions students generated and conceptual understanding in PF. What proportion (0–1) of the variance in conceptual understanding is explained by the number of solutions, i.e. r²?
r² = 0.82² = 0.6724, about 67%. Note: correlation does not prove causation.
Exercise 4. Study 1, transfer: PF M=5.37 SD=1.46 n=37; DI M=3.11 SD=1.51 n=38. Compute Cohen d.
sp = √[(36·2.1316 + 37·2.2801)/73] = √2.2069 = 1.4856; d = 2.26/1.4856 = 1.52. Matches d=1.52 in the paper.

5. Bringing PF into teaching programming

Kapur 2014 taught standard deviation to ninth graders, not programming. Moving to programming is a hypothesis to be tested, not an existing result. Below is a concrete PF design for an introductory data-structures course:

PhaseActivityAI
1. Problem solving (20–30 min)Task: "Check whether a string of brackets ()[]{} is balanced". Students write pseudocode and list as many approaches as possible (count brackets, delete adjacent pairs...). Predict the result on 3 examples.Off
2. Compare solutionsPick 3–4 representative approaches and run them on a hard example such as ([)] to expose failures.Off, or only to run code
3. ConsolidationThe instructor teaches the stack and matches each of the class's approaches against the canonical solution.Not needed
4. ExtensionStudents ask AI in a Socratic way to extend (complexity, edge cases) and write a reflection.On, with constraints
Where this matches your treatment modelStep "1. Write pseudocode first, predict the output" in your notes is exactly PF's problem-solving phase. AI enters only in phase 4, so it does not cut off solution generation; this is the difference between the treatment and the free-AI group.

6. Interpretive traps

Trap 1: "failure" is useful by itself. Kapur 2016 stresses unproductive failure: pure discovery without consolidation does poorly in both the short and long term. The consolidation phase is mandatory.
Trap 2: ignoring the cost. PF groups reported clearly higher mental effort (Study 1: 7.65 vs 6.18, d=1.70). Students may find it unpleasant; warn them and measure effort as well as scores.
Trap 3: generalizing too far. Studies 1 and 2 used the same school, the same teacher and the same topic, in one day. There is no direct evidence on university programming or on using AI. Kapur 2014 also says failure is not a necessary condition for learning.
Trap 4: confusing procedural with conceptual scores. PF does not improve procedural knowledge (9.24 vs 9.47), only conceptual understanding and transfer. If your exam measures only procedure, you will not see an advantage.

7. Link to the thesis

RQ1 and designIf you want the treatment to raise programming ability without AI, measure all three: procedural (writing correct syntax), conceptual (explaining how the exercise works), transfer (a new problem not taught). PF predicts an advantage on the last two.
RQ2Measure mental effort and engagement in each phase, exactly as Kapur 2014 did (Paas's 1992 9-point scale for effort, a Likert scale for engagement).
RQ3Record the number of solutions each student generates in phase 1. This is the strongest process indicator in the original paper (r up to 0.88).

Glossary

Productive Failure (PF) solve on an unlearned concept, then be taught · Direct Instruction (DI) teach first, then exercises · Vicarious Failure (VF) evaluate others' flawed solutions before instruction · Consolidation the phase that compares and assembles solutions into the canonical one · Desirable difficulties difficulties that help long-term learning (Schmidt & Bjork 1992) · Ill-structured problem a problem with unclear goals, data or method.
