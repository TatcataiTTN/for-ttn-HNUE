10 · Appropriate Reliance & Calibrated Trust

Module 10/14 · Using automation (or AI) is not just "use it or not". What matters is using it at the right time: too much trust leads to misuse, too little to disuse. Three classic papers give us the vocabulary: four ways of using automation (Parasuraman and Riley), automation bias in an experiment (Skitka et al.), and calibrated trust (Lee and See).

Sources: Parasuraman & Riley (1997) Humans and automation: Use, misuse, disuse, abuse, Human Factors 39(2), 230–253 · Skitka, Mosier & Burdick (1999) Does automation bias decision-making?, Int. J. Human-Computer Studies 51, 991–1006 · Lee & See (2004) Trust in automation: Designing for appropriate reliance, Human Factors 46(1), 50–80. All three come from aviation, process control and pilot simulation; none studies generative AI in a classroom. Parasuraman and Riley, and Lee and See, are reviews or conceptual frameworks; only Skitka et al. is a single experiment.

1. Parasuraman and Riley (1997): use, misuse, disuse, abuse

What automation is. "The execution by a machine agent (usually a computer) of a function that was previously carried out by a human." What counts as automation changes over time: when the reallocation of a function from human to machine is complete and permanent, it is seen simply as a machine operation (e.g., a car's starter motor). Automation does not remove humans from systems; it changes the nature of what humans do, often in ways designers did not anticipate. It is a continuum of levels, not all-or-none: Sheridan (1980) identified 10 levels, from full manual control to full machine control.

Four concepts (no pejorative intent)

ConceptDefinitionExample in the paper
UseVoluntary activation or disengagement of automation by the operator.Deciding to engage the autopilot.
MisuseOverreliance: using it when it should not be used, failing to monitor it effectively.A pilot with low confidence in manual skills relied on the autopilot and did not monitor airspeed during a snowy night approach (Columbus, Ohio, 1994).
DisuseUnderutilization; e.g., ignoring or turning off alarms.After the 1987 Baltimore accident, inspectors found operators taping over the buzzers that warned of speed violations.
AbuseDesigners automating and managers implementing without due regard for consequences for human performance and operator authority.A weight-on-wheels sensor that blocks deployment of stopping devices exactly when needed if it fails.

Factors influencing the decision to use

  • Attitudes: vary widely between individuals; only a minority of pilots agreed that "automation reduces workload" (Wiener); attitudes and behavior do not necessarily match: two studies found no relation between attitudes and actual reliance.
  • Workload: the evidence is unclear. Riley (1994a) found that raising tracking difficulty did not make students use automation more (overall usage below about 50%); a replication with pilots found no relation either. Operators still often cite workload.
  • Cognitive overhead: if the benefit is unclear or engaging and checking the automation takes effort, people may not use it (e.g., OCR-scanning one sheet of paper). Kirlik (1993): there are conditions in which the optimal choice is not to use the automation.
  • Trust and self-confidence: Muir (1988) and Lee & Moray (1992) found reliance correlated with subjective trust; Lee and Moray (1994) found people chose manual control when their self-confidence exceeded their trust in the automation, and vice versa. But students in Riley's studies could not accurately assess their own performance.
  • Conclusion: the use decision is a complex interaction of many factors with large individual differences, so it is hard to predict for specific people.

Misuse: overreliance

  • Automation bias: decision aids encourage heuristics; it causes omission errors (missing a problem because the aid did not flag it) and commission errors (following an inappropriate automated directive). Mosier et al.: professional pilots show error rates similar to students, so expertise gives no immunity.
  • Monitoring failures: human monitoring can be efficient, but is poor when other manual tasks must be done at once. Parasuraman, Molloy and Singh (1993): when participants performed two manual tasks while monitoring an automated engine task, detection of automation failures dropped sharply compared with when monitoring was the only task (above 95%). Mosier et al. (1994): 77% of ASRS incidents in which overreliance was suspected involved a probable monitoring failure.
  • Constant reliability produced worse monitoring than variable reliability; a high absolute reliability level also lowered the detection rate of automation failures.
  • Remedies: make automation state salient (the integrated EMACS display helped participants detect twice as many failures as the non-integrated display); adaptive task allocation (10 minutes of manual work after 40 minutes of automation improved later monitoring).

Disuse: false alarms and base rates

Loss of trust often stems from false alarms. Two factors matter: the decision criterion and the base rate of the hazardous condition. For an alarm system with sensitivity d′ = 4.7, set to miss only 1 in 1000 hazards (hit rate .999), the false alarm rate is .0594. But if the base rate is only .001, only 1 in 59 alarms is true (posterior probability .0168). No wonder operators ignore or switch off alarms ("cried wolf once too often"). Getty et al.: participants responded more slowly as the probability of a true alarm fell from .75 to .25. Proposal: multi-level "likelihood" alarms instead of treating the alarm as the final authority.

Abuse: when design ignores humans

  • Human error cannot be removed by removing the human: automation substitutes the designer for the operator; a system less vulnerable to operator error is more vulnerable to designer error.
  • Automation can also act as a surrogate for the manager (e.g., the decision not to let the operator run manually in a snowstorm at Gaithersburg).
  • The technology-centered approach puts operators in a role poorly suited to humans (monitor), which can lead to overreliance. Billings (1991): human-centered automation gives the operator an active role even if the machine could do better.
  • Vicious circle: operators distrust the automation; designers or managers distrust the operators and give even more authority to the automation; more misuse and disuse follow.

Summary of the paper's recommendations

  • Use: operators who know how automation works use it more appropriately; teach rational use decisions; do not make it costly to turn on or off.
  • Misuse: recognize that overreliance happens; teach recognition of biases; make feedback on automation state salient enough.
  • Disuse: consider the effect of false alarms on trust when setting performance requirements; consider both the threshold and the base rate.
  • Abuse: define the operator's role from their responsibilities and capabilities; keep the operator actively involved even if that reduces performance compared with full automation.

2. Skitka, Mosier and Burdick (1999): does automation bias decision-making?

The question. Automated decision aids are introduced with the goal of "reducing human error", but few studies evaluate whether they do, or whether they just create a different class of errors. The authors compare the same simulated flight task with and without an automated monitoring aid (AMA). Three possible sources are proposed: the "cognitive miser" using heuristics; social loafing and diffusion of responsibility when sharing work with a machine "teammate"; and seeing the machine as an authority (citing obedience research, e.g., 21 of 22 nurses gave a dangerous drug dose on a phone order).

Design

ElementContent
Sample80 undergraduates; 40 in the AMA (automated) condition and the rest without an AMA.
TaskNASA Ames W/Panes simulation, eight "flights" (8 min 20 s each): joystick tracking, button presses at waypoints, and monitoring 4 gauges, pressing a button when a gauge entered the red zone (all three in red: reset).
Automated conditionThe AMA announced events and recommended actions. Participants were told the AMA was not completely reliable but that all other indicators (gauges, map) were always 100% accurate.
Omission opportunities6 of 100 events were omission opportunities: the AMA did not prompt; compared with the unaided group on the same events.
Commission opportunities6 times the AMA gave a directive contradicted by the 100% accurate indicators. Only in the AMA group, so descriptive only.

Results

MeasureResult
Omission (6 events with no AMA prompt)AMA group missed on average 2.44 (about 59% correct); unaided group 0.18 (about 97% correct); F(1,62) = 44.32, p < .05.
Following the aid (6 opportunities)Mean 3.92 errors (about 35% correct); only one participant made none; 23.1% erred on all 6.
88 non-error eventsAMA group more correct (M = 83.03 vs 71.85; F(1,77) = 26.37, p < .05) when the AMA directed correctly.
TrackingNo significant difference between groups.
Perceived workloadNo support that the AMA reduced perceived workload.
Estimated reliabilityThe AMA was actually about 94% reliable (6 misses in 100). Participants estimated on average only 81.35% (notification) and 81.89% (directives). They underestimated its reliability yet still made errors.

Exploratory correlations (AMA group only, N = 40)

  • Belief that the AMA leads to high accuracy: r = .44 with omission and r = .59 with commission errors.
  • Diffusion of responsibility to the AMA: r = .44 (omission) and r = .66 (commission).
  • Estimated % of events the AMA gave inappropriate directives: negative correlation with both types of error (−.35 and −.44).
  • Perceiving the task as easy: more commission errors (r = −.37 with perceived difficulty).
  • Perceived effort was unrelated to error rates. Trial order had no effect.
Limits (by the paper itself). The W/Panes task is only an analogue; it cannot capture the complexity of a real glass cockpit or the mindset of experienced pilots; the sample is students; generalization needs replication; correlation is not causation; other studies found that verification prompts and adding a second "crewmember" did not reduce errors in students or pilots, while explicit training about the bias had short-term benefits. The paper does not show that automation is bad: when the aid was right, people did better; the cost appears when it is wrong.

3. Lee and See (2004): trust and appropriate reliance

Definition. Trust is "the attitude that an agent will help achieve an individual's goals in a situation characterized by uncertainty and vulnerability". In the Ajzen and Fishbein framework: beliefs shape attitudes, attitudes shape intentions, intentions shape behavior. Trust is the attitude and reliance is the behavior. Trust guides but does not completely determine reliance: other factors (workload, self-confidence, effort to engage the automation, time constraints) intervene. Trust matters most when automation is too complex to understand fully and situations demand adaptation.

Appropriate trust: three aspects

ConceptMeaning
CalibrationThe correspondence between a person's trust and the automation's capability. Overtrust: trust exceeds capability, leading to misuse. Distrust: trust falls short of capability, leading to disuse.
ResolutionHow precisely trust differentiates levels of automation capability. Poor resolution: a large range of capability maps onto a small range of trust.
SpecificityThe degree to which trust is tied to a particular component or function (functional specificity) or to changes over time and situation (temporal specificity).

Three bases of trust: performance, process, purpose

BasisAnswers the questionCorresponds to (Mayer et al. 1995)
PerformanceWhat the automation does: reliability, predictability, ability in the current situation.Ability
ProcessHow it works: whether its algorithms are appropriate and understandable.Integrity
PurposeWhat it is for: whether it is used within the designer's intent; the designer's intent.Benevolence

These three are levels of attributional abstraction, not fixed developmental stages: early on a user may have no performance history but a clear statement of purpose, so initial trust can rest on purpose. Trust based on several bases is robust; trust based on a single basis can be fragile.

Three processes governing trust

  • Analytic: rational-choice cost-benefit calculation; but it may overstate cognitive capacity and understate the role of affect.
  • Analogical: based on categories, roles, rules, reputation, gossip; fragile when abnormal situations disrupt familiar roles.
  • Affective: the core influence of trust on behavior; emotion fills in when reasoning lacks resources. Neurological evidence: patients with ventromedial prefrontal lesions can still reason but decide poorly due to lack of affect (somatic marker hypothesis).

Dynamic model: the closed loop of trust and reliance

  • If a system is not trusted it is used less; if it is used less, the user has little information about its capability, so trust hardly grows. Conversely, highly trusted automation is monitored less, so trust can keep growing despite occasional faults. This feedback can push people to fully automatic or fully manual control and explains individual differences.
  • Self-confidence interacts with trust: high self-confidence with low trust favors manual control; the reverse favors the automation.
  • Trust declines gradually after faults and recovers gradually; a first-order differential equation accounted for 60% to 86% of the variance in reliance patterns (Lee and Moray 1994). Trust has "inertia".
  • Trust tends to be conditioned by the automation's worst behaviors; negative interactions weigh more than positive ones; initial experience has a lasting effect: an initially low reliability that later improves gives lower trust than starting high and declining. Small but unpredictable faults hurt trust more than large constant faults.
  • The reliability threshold below which trust falls quickly depends on the system: estimates range from 90% (Moray) and 70% (Kantowitz) to 60% (Fox); no single level is universal.

Display effects and differences from trust between people

  • Trust often rests on displays because direct observation of automation is rarely possible. Surface features (realistic images, consistent speech, balanced layout) can raise trust without any link to true capability; the paper warns these may be seen as manipulative.
  • Automation lacks intentionality and human-machine trust is not symmetric; but automation embodies its designers' intent, and users may attribute intentions to it as it becomes more sophisticated. Care is needed extrapolating from human-human trust.

Design recommendations: "trustable automation"

  • Design for appropriate trust, not greater trust.
  • Show past performance; show the process and algorithms; simplify algorithms; show purpose and range of application; train users on expected reliability and mechanisms; evaluate any anthropomorphizing carefully.
  • Relate context to capability: show how performance depends on the situation; evaluate trust by calibration, resolution and specificity.
  • Consider the trade-off between trustworthy (efficient, reliable) and trustable (easy for trust to match): sometimes simpler but less capable automation may be worth more.

94 − 81.35 = 12.65 percentage points.

7. Interpretation traps

Trap 1: treating reliance as bad. All three papers are about appropriate reliance. In Skitka, people did better when the AMA was right; and Lee and See note it can be reasonable to rely on imperfect automation.
Trap 2: assuming that knowing the machine is imperfect is enough. In Skitka, participants were told the AMA was not completely reliable and even underestimated its reliability, yet still made omission and commission errors.
Trap 3: confusing trust with reliance. Trust is an attitude; reliance is a behavior; self-confidence, workload and time intervene. Riley found attitudes and actual reliance did not necessarily match.
Trap 4: reading correlation as causation. The correlations between belief in the AMA, diffusion of responsibility and errors in Skitka are exploratory, in a sample of 40.
Trap 5: transferring aviation numbers directly to the classroom. The figures (59%, 35%, reliability thresholds of 60% to 90%) belong to a specific simulation and specific systems.

8. Link to the thesis

Misuse and disuse as two outcome variablesWith AI teaching programming, misuse corresponds to accepting wrong AI code or solutions; disuse to rejecting correct AI suggestions. The two rates can be measured separately (as in the simulation widget) rather than only the final score. This is design inference.
Calibration of trust in AIStudents' trust in AI could be compared with the AI's actual accuracy on programming questions to measure overtrust or distrust. Function-specific items (specificity) are needed, not just one general question. This is design inference.
A "responsible AI" design after Lee and SeeShow past reliability, explain the process, state purpose and limits: the recommendations for "trustable automation". The goal is appropriate trust, not more trust. The thesis must test this empirically; this module does not show it holds for generative AI.
Training to recognize biasSkitka notes that verification prompts alone did not help while explicit training about the bias had short-term benefits. This suggests the treatment group should be taught about automation bias rather than just told "check it". This is design inference; the paper's statement is a hint with short-term effects.
