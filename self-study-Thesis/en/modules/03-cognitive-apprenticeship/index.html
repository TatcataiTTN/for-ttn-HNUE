1. The idea: make thinking visible

Collins, Brown and Holum (1991) open by noting that in ancient times we learned to speak, farm and sew by watching skilled people and being helped to do it. Schools replaced much of apprenticeship, but with a loss: in apprenticeship the processes of the work are visible; in school, the processes of thinking in problem solving, reading comprehension and writing are often invisible to students and teachers alike. Cognitive apprenticeship is a model of instruction that makes thinking visible.

Four elements of traditional apprenticeship: modeling (watch the demonstration), scaffolding (support), fading (gradually removing support), coaching (the thread through the whole experience: choosing tasks, hints, diagnosing, encouraging, giving feedback).

Why observation matters. Lave (1988) suggests observation helps learners develop a conceptual model of the task before executing it. The model has three uses: an organizer for first attempts, a structure for interpreting the master's feedback, and an internal guide for independent practice.

Three things to do to move from traditional to cognitive apprenticeship

  1. Identify the processes of the task and make them visible to students.
  2. Situate abstract tasks in authentic contexts so students see their relevance.
  3. Vary the diversity of situations and articulate common aspects so students can transfer.

The paper says clearly that it is not the only way to learn and is not suited to rote tasks such as French verb conjugation or the periodic table.

3. Three examples: reading, writing, math

Reading: reciprocal teaching (Palincsar and Brown 1984)

Four skills: questioning, summarizing, predicting, clarifying. Teacher and students take turns as teacher. Results quoted in the paper: from 15% to 85% accuracy after about 20 sessions (poor readers, individually), 30% to 80% (groups of two), 40% to 80% (groups of 4 to 7); after 6 months 60%, back to 85% after one session. These are figures cited from other studies, not experiments of this paper.

Writing: cue cards (Scardamalia and Bereiter)

Novices use knowledge telling; experts use knowledge transforming. Cue cards ("An even better idea is...") are procedural facilitation for planning and revising. Results: planning time up tenfold; idea-level revisions up tenfold without loss of stylistic revisions.

Math: Schoenfeld

Teaching heuristics (after Polya) plus control strategies: generating alternatives, evaluating, monitoring progress. The teacher thinks aloud on hard problems students bring, including failures; small groups with three questions (what are you doing, why, how will it help); and a postmortem (abstracted replay) to extract generalizable strategies.

Where this matches the treatment modelThe step "students explain how the exercise works" is articulation; the reflection on what else they learned is reflection. A postmortem after each programming task is a natural way to implement both.

4. Situated cognition (Brown, Collins, Duguid 1989)

The claim: knowledge is situated, in part a product of the activity, context and culture in which it is developed and used. The opening evidence is vocabulary: 17-year-olds learn about 5,000 words a year (13 a day) from everyday communication, whereas classes teach about 100 to 200 a year through definitions and sample sentences, and many pupils misuse them ("Mrs. Morrow stimulated the soup").

  • Concepts as tools. One can have a tool and be unable to use it (the pocket-knife device for removing stones from horses' hooves). Carpenters and cabinet makers use chisels differently because their communities differ.
  • Learning as enculturation. People join groups by observing and practising the group's behaviour and beliefs.
  • Authentic activity is "the ordinary practices of the culture"; much school activity is ersatz (hybrid): implicitly framed by school culture but attributed to the culture of mathematicians or historians.
  • JPFs, students, practitioners (Table 1): JPFs reason with causal stories, students with laws, practitioners with causal models; JPFs' problems are emergent, students' well-defined, practitioners' ill-defined.
The Weight Watchers example (Lave 1988). Asked for three-quarters of two-thirds of a cup of cottage cheese, the dieter poured out two-thirds of a cup on a cutting board, patted it into a circle, marked a cross and removed one quadrant. Problem, solution and procedure coincided; the setting was part of the calculation. It illustrates "distributed cognition" (see also Module 08 on offloading).

Two examples of math teaching: Schoenfeld (the magic square problem, teaching the strategy "exploiting extreme cases") and Lampert (multiplication through coins, students inventing stories, only then the standard algorithm; four kinds of knowledge: intuitive, computational, concrete, principled).

5. Original scaffolding: Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976)

Thirty children aged 3, 4 and 5 (10 per age) were tutored by one tutor to build a pyramid of 21 wooden blocks with pegs and holes. The tutor's rule: let the child do as much as possible, use words first, and intervene directly only when the child does not follow. The authors state clearly that this is a systematic description, not a test of a hypothesis.

Six scaffolding functions

FunctionMeaning in the paper
RecruitmentEnlist interest, draw the child away from imaginative play
Reduction in degrees of freedomCut the number of constituent acts so the child can recognize a "fit"
Direction maintenanceKeep the goal, sustain motivation, make the next step worth the risk
Marking critical featuresAccentuate relevant features, interpret discrepancies with a correct product
Frustration controlProblem solving less stressful with a tutor; the major risk is too much dependency
DemonstrationAn "idealized" model, often completing a solution the child partly made

Comprehension precedes production. The 3-year-olds were poor at building correctly yet as sensitive as 4-year-olds to correct structures: they rebuilt two-thirds of correct constructions they had taken apart but only 14% of incorrect ones. Feedback helps only when the learner can recognize a correct solution.

6. Calculate from Tables 2 and 3 of Wood 1976

Exercise 1. For 3-year-olds: 262 construction operations in total and 201 interventions. How many operations per intervention? (1 decimal)
262/201 = 1.30, matching the table.
Exercise 2. For 5-year-olds: 280 operations and 112 interventions. Compute operations per intervention.
280/112 = 2.50.
Exercise 3. How many times more operations can 5-year-olds do between two interventions than 3-year-olds? (divide 2.5 by 1.3, 1 decimal)
2.5/1.3 = 1.92: about 1.9 times.
Exercise 4. For 5-year-olds, showing succeeds 80% of the time and telling 57%. What is the difference in percentage points?
80 − 57 = 23 percentage points.

7. Interpretive traps

Trap 1: treating cognitive apprenticeship as "apprenticeship in general". Its distinctive point is making thinking visible and situating it in authentic contexts.
Trap 2: treating 15% to 85% as this paper's result. Collins 1991 is a synthesis quoting Palincsar and Brown; read the original before citing.
Trap 3: over-generalizing Wood 1976. Thirty preschoolers, one tutor, one fun and suitably sized task; the authors themselves note their results are constrained by the task.
Trap 4: scaffolding means doing it for them. In Wood, the tutor reduces degrees of freedom and lets the child perfect the parts within reach; doing everything is dependence.

8. Link to the thesis

The Socratic AI modelThe treatment can be described as scaffolding with fading: each kind of AI hint belongs to one of the six functions, and a fading rule is needed (for example fewer hints when the student fixes bugs alone). This is a design hypothesis.
RQ2, RQ3Log AI interactions by type of support; compare interventions per operation between the start and end of the semester (as in Wood's Table 3) to check fading and measure dependence.
