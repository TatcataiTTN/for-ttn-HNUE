## Zimmerman 2002: what SRL is
Q: In Zimmerman (2002), how is self-regulation described most accurately?
+ A self-directive process by which learners turn mental abilities into learning skills
- An innate intellectual ability that determines a learner's grades
- A separate academic skill taught as an independent subject
- A fixed learning style that a learner either has or does not have
E: The paper: "Self-regulation refers to self-generated thoughts, feelings, and behaviors that are oriented to attaining goals", and it is "a self-directive process by which learners transform their mental abilities into task-related skills". It is not a mental ability or an academic skill.

Q: What is the character Tracy in the paper's opening used to illustrate?
+ A student lacking self-regulation: no specific goals, no plan, vague standards
- A gifted student who uses every learning strategy skillfully
- A student with a learning disorder needing medical diagnosis
- A lazy student who lacks innate mathematical ability
E: Tracy studies inefficiently: no specific goals, no plan, last-minute cramming, vague self-evaluation, and she blames failure on "lack of ability". She is the mirror image of a self-regulated learner.

Q: According to Zimmerman (2002), how is learning viewed in the SRL perspective?
+ As something students do for themselves, not something that happens to them
- As a passive process that occurs whenever the teacher lectures well enough
- As an automatic response to stimuli in the classroom environment
- As an outcome that depends only on the intellectual ability already present
E: The paper stresses that learning is "something students do for themselves in a proactive way", not "something that happens to them in reaction to teaching".

Q: Which benefit of SRL does Zimmerman (2002) mention for the workplace and life beyond school?
+ Learners can teach themselves new positions, run their own business and carry out long projects without anyone teaching them
- Learners no longer need to seek help from colleagues
- Learners score higher on every standardized exam
- Learners need not set goals because work already comes with goals
E: The paper points to the need for continuous self-directed learning at work and in self-employment; SRL is central to lifelong learning.

Q: What did research on experts (Ericsson and Charness 1994), cited by Zimmerman (2002), show?
+ Experts learn and practice about four hours a day and are highly motivated
- Experts practice only about one hour a day thanks to talent
- Experts practice a lot but need no special motivation
- Experts rely on innate gifts so hours of practice do not matter
E: The paper: experts practice about four hours a day, and the amount and quality of practice strongly predict the level of expertise reached.

Q: Which view does Zimmerman (2002) reject when discussing SRL?
+ That SRL is asocial and self-regulated students never turn to others
- That SRL can be taught and varies across tasks
- That SRL includes both motivational and cognitive aspects
- That SRL is the selective use of processes rather than a fixed trait
E: The paper says self-regulated students often actively seek help from others; the "self" is in initiative, persistence and adaptive skill, not social isolation.

Q: According to Zimmerman, Bonner and Kovach (1996), cited in the paper, what is the state of classroom practice?
+ Teachers rarely prepare students to learn on their own: they seldom allow choices of task or method or encourage goal setting
- Teachers regularly teach self-regulation strategies in every subject
- Teachers teach all three phases but students refuse to apply them
- Teachers teach SRL only to high-achieving students in the final grades
E: Zimmerman (2002) points out that few teachers prepare students for self-directed learning; they seldom let students choose tasks, methods or partners, or encourage goals and strategy instruction.

Q: Which feature shows SRL is a process rather than a fixed trait?
+ Learners use processes selectively, adapt them to each task, and can be taught them
- Learners have the same high SRL in every subject and situation
- SRL appears only after a learner has scored high on a previous exam
- SRL changes only when the learner changes school or teacher
E: Zimmerman (2002) says SRL is not a trait that people have or lack; it is the selective use of processes and it can be taught.

## Zimmerman 1990: three features of SRL
Q: Zimmerman (1990) found three features common to SRL definitions. Which set is right?
+ Use of SRL strategies, a self-oriented feedback loop about effectiveness, and motivation tied to learning
- Long-term memory, processing speed and emotion control
- Good teachers, good textbooks and small classes
- Goal setting, frequent exams and external rewards
E: The three features: (1) use of SRL strategies, (2) a self-oriented feedback loop about learning effectiveness, (3) motivation related to learning, interdependent with the other two.

Q: What is an "SRL strategy" in Zimmerman (1990)?
+ Actions and processes directed at acquiring information or skill, with awareness of the link between strategy and outcome
- Tricks for guessing answers on multiple-choice tests
- School rules about how students must study at home
- Study habits formed by copying classmates
E: SRL strategies are actions and processes directed at acquiring information or skill, involving purpose and instrumentality perceived by the learner.

Q: What does the "self-oriented feedback loop" mean?
+ Learners monitor how effective their learning is and react by adjusting their thinking or behavior
- The teacher grades work and returns the score so students know where they stand
- Students receive external rewards after each task
- Students compare their scores with classmates to find their rank
E: In Zimmerman (1990), it is a loop in which learners monitor the effectiveness of their methods and react, from changing self-perceptions to changing behavior or strategy.

Q: Why is motivation counted as the third feature of SRL?
+ Self-regulation takes time, vigilance and effort, so learners may choose not to do it without motivation
- Because motivation is an external reward given by the teacher
- Because motivation determines innate intellectual ability
- Because motivation appears only after a learner has scored high
E: The paper says self-regulation requires preparation time and effort; skill and will are integrated parts, and learning and motivation are interdependent.

Q: What does Bandura add about feedback in SRL (cited by Zimmerman 1990)?
+ Feedback can also be positive: learners raise the goal after reaching it
- Feedback works only when it comes from a teacher or adult
- Feedback should occur only after the whole lesson has ended
- Feedback must always be corrective, never praise
E: The feedback loop is not only error correction; Bandura stresses positive feedback, where after reaching a goal learners raise their own standard.

Q: According to Zimmerman (1990), in which three dimensions does a self-regulated learner participate actively?
+ Metacognition, motivation and behavior
- Memory, attention and perception
- Emotion, physical ability and social skills
- Theory, practice and exams
E: Self-regulated learners are active in metacognition (planning, goal setting, self-monitoring, self-evaluation), motivation (confidence, attribution, interest) and behavior (selecting, structuring and creating learning environments).

Q: Which of these belongs to the "behavior" dimension of a self-regulated learner?
+ Selecting, structuring and creating a learning environment; seeking advice; self-instruction and self-reward
- Believing in one's own ability and seeing learning as valuable
- Making a weekly study plan and setting long-term goals
- Evaluating results against the original goal
E: In Zimmerman (1990), the behavioral dimension covers choosing, structuring and creating learning environments, seeking advice, self-instruction and self-reward.

## Zimmerman 2002: the three-phase model
Q: What are the three phases of Zimmerman's (2002) cyclical self-regulation model?
+ Forethought, performance and self-reflection
- Planning, testing and grading
- Observation, imitation and self-control
- Encoding, storage and retrieval
E: Zimmerman (2002) describes three cyclical phases: forethought (preparation), performance, and self-reflection.

Q: What are the two main process groups of the forethought phase?
+ Task analysis and self-motivation beliefs
- Self-control and self-observation
- Self-judgment and self-reaction
- Modeling and coaching
E: Forethought has task analysis (goal setting, strategic planning) and self-motivation beliefs (self-efficacy, outcome expectations, intrinsic interest, learning-goal orientation).

Q: To which group do goal setting and strategic planning belong?
+ Task analysis in the forethought phase
- Self-observation in the performance phase
- Self-judgment in the self-reflection phase
- Self-reaction in the self-reflection phase
E: Task analysis belongs to forethought and contains two main processes: goal setting and strategic planning.

Q: In the paper's spelling example, which planning strategy is named?
+ Splitting words into syllables to learn them part by part
- Copying each word a hundred times in a notebook
- Reading the word list aloud to a classmate
- Asking the teacher to repeat the dictation many times
E: The example names planning the use of a strategy, splitting words into syllables, for a spelling word list.

Q: Which four factors are self-motivation beliefs in the forethought phase?
+ Self-efficacy, outcome expectations, intrinsic interest and learning-goal orientation
- Grades, rankings, rewards and teacher praise
- Memory, attention, processing speed and reasoning ability
- Free time, books, equipment and a quiet room
E: The paper lists self-efficacy, outcome expectations, intrinsic interest or valuing, and learning-goal orientation as self-motivation beliefs.

Q: What are the two main process groups of the performance phase?
+ Self-control and self-observation
- Task analysis and self-motivation beliefs
- Self-judgment and self-reaction
- Modeling and coaching
E: Performance has self-control (imagery, self-instruction, attention focusing, task strategies) and self-observation (self-recording, self-experimentation).

Q: Remembering "pan" through the image of a bread pan is an example of which process?
+ Imagery within self-control
- Self-evaluation within self-judgment
- Causal attribution within self-judgment
- Goal setting within task analysis
E: The paper uses imagery, a self-control process, as the example of picturing "pan" as a bread pan to remember the word.

Q: Choosing a study spot away from noise is an example of which process?
+ Attention focusing, part of self-control
- Self-experimentation, part of self-observation
- A defensive reaction, part of self-reaction
- Outcome expectation, part of motivation beliefs
E: Attention focusing, such as removing distractions and choosing a place to study, belongs to self-control in the performance phase.

Q: Recording study time and experimenting (studying alone versus with a friend) belong to which group?
+ Self-observation in the performance phase
- Self-control in the performance phase
- Self-judgment in the self-reflection phase
- Task analysis in the forethought phase
E: Self-observation has self-recording and self-experimentation, such as studying alone and with a friend to see which helps.

Q: What are the two main process groups of the self-reflection phase?
+ Self-judgment and self-reaction
- Self-control and self-observation
- Task analysis and self-motivation beliefs
- Self-evaluation and material self-reward
E: Self-reflection has self-judgment (self-evaluation, attribution) and self-reaction (satisfaction, affect, adaptive or defensive reactions).

Q: How many kinds of self-evaluation standards are named, and what are they?
+ Against one's earlier level, against others, and against an absolute standard
- Only against the teacher and against the class average
- Only against the school's long-term goals
- Against the textbook and against the answer key
E: Zimmerman (2002) names the standards: one's own previous level, the performance of others, and an absolute (mastery) standard.

Q: How does an "adaptive" reaction differ from a "defensive" one?
+ Adaptive means fixing or dropping a poor strategy; defensive means avoiding learning, such as dropping the subject or skipping the exam
- Adaptive means reusing the same old strategy; defensive means changing strategy entirely
- Adaptive means trying harder; defensive means seeking help from friends
- Adaptive occurs only after success; defensive only after failure
E: The paper: adaptive reactions aim to improve the method, while defensive ones aim to avoid embarrassment, such as dropping out or skipping the test.

Q: Why does attributing failure to "lack of ability" reduce motivation?
+ It implies that further effort would be futile because ability does not change
- It makes the student forget what was learned
- It makes teachers reduce the support given to the student
- It makes the student set goals that are too hard next time
E: Zimmerman (2002): attributing to fixed ability lowers motivation; attributing to a strategy (controllable) preserves motivation because changing strategy might succeed.

Q: Which attribution helps learners keep their motivation better?
+ To the use of a strategy or method that can be changed
- To innate ability that cannot be changed
- To luck or random difficulty of the exam
- To unfairness on the part of the marker
E: Attributing to strategy means attributing to a controllable process, implying a change of strategy could succeed next time.

Q: What does the "cyclical" nature of Zimmerman's model mean?
+ The results of the self-reflection phase influence preparation for the next learning attempt
- The three phases must always take place within a single hour
- Learners must repeat the same task until they reach full marks
- Teachers must repeat a lesson three times so that students remember
E: The paper: self-reactions from a previous attempt affect the next preparation; for example dissatisfaction leads to low self-efficacy and less effort.

Q: What does Zimmerman and Kitsantas (1999) show, as cited?
+ High correlations between the use of processes across all three phases
- Only the forethought phase affects learning outcomes
- The three phases are empirically independent
- The self-reflection phase is unrelated to self-efficacy
E: The paper reports that processes in the three phases correlate highly, supporting the cyclical nature of the model.

Q: What does Bandura and Schunk (1981) support?
+ Setting specific, near goals goes with more self-observation, better results and higher self-efficacy
- The vaguer and more distant the goal, the more effort learners make
- Goals imposed by the teacher are always better than self-set goals
- Self-efficacy is unaffected by how goals are set
E: Zimmerman (2002) cites Bandura and Schunk: specific, proximal goals go with self-observation, achievement and self-efficacy.

## Zimmerman 2002: novices and experts
Q: What is typical of a novice regarding goals and plans?
+ Vague goals, poor-quality preparation, and reactive self-regulation
- Hierarchical goals from process to outcome and careful preparation
- Goals set by others that are always reached
- Clear goals but no plan for carrying them out
E: Novices set few specific goals, prepare poorly and regulate in a reactive way.

Q: How do experts typically set goals?
+ Hierarchically from process to outcome, such as splitting an essay into opening, body and conclusion
- Vaguely, such as "do my best"
- Only outcome goals such as a score
- They set no goals because the task is familiar
E: Zimmerman (2002): experts set hierarchical goals from process to outcome (for example splitting an essay into introduction, body and conclusion).

Q: How do novices typically judge their own results?
+ By comparison with others, so standards rise whenever someone does better
- By comparison with their own goals and previous progress
- By an absolute standard of the subject
- They do not judge, because they believe every result is worthwhile
E: The paper: novices usually judge themselves against others, which is hard to satisfy because someone is always better; experts use their own goals.

Q: What do experts mainly attribute failure to?
+ A strategy or method that did not fit
- A lack of innate ability in the subject
- Unfair marking by the teacher
- An exam too hard for the curriculum
E: Novices attribute to lack of ability (normative), while experts attribute to strategy, which helps them keep improving.

Q: What are the emotional and motivational consequences for experts compared with novices?
+ More satisfaction and continued improvement; self-efficacy, expectations and interest are nourished
- Less satisfaction because their standards are too high
- The same satisfaction, since both reach the same score
- No consequences, since emotion does not affect learning
E: Novices are less satisfied and react defensively; experts are more satisfied and their self-efficacy, outcome expectations and intrinsic interest grow.

Q: A graphic organizer filled with key information is an example of what for experts?
+ Planning with a powerful strategy and tracking its effect
- A defensive reaction to failure
- Evaluating oneself against others
- Attributing success to luck
E: The paper uses a graphic organizer filled with key information as an expert's strong strategy whose effect is tracked.

Q: Why do novices often fail to monitor their learning systematically?
+ With no specific goals there is no standard to monitor against
- Because they are so good they do not need to monitor
- Because monitoring is forbidden in ordinary classrooms
- Because they believe scores depend entirely on luck
E: Without a specific goal there is nothing to compare against, so novices track little in a systematic way (Zimmerman 2002).

## Zimmerman and Martinez-Pons: 14 strategies and the evidence
Q: What instrument did Zimmerman and Martinez-Pons use to collect data on SRL strategies?
+ A structured interview with learning scenarios and open answers
- A questionnaire with four choices per item
- Direct observation of students in a laboratory
- A standardized end-of-year achievement test
E: They used structured interviews with learning scenarios and let students state their own strategies; self-generated answers were preferred over multiple choice as closer to natural conditions.

Q: Why did they choose open interviews over multiple choice?
+ Self-generated answers simulate more closely the conditions in which students decide for themselves how to learn
- Because multiple choice always gives wrong results and cannot be used
- Because open interviews take less time than questionnaires
- Because students cannot read multiple-choice questions
E: The paper explains that free responses better reflect how students really cope with a situation.

Q: Which of these is a scenario in the interview?
+ "It is hard to finish homework because something more interesting is on: how do you motivate yourself?"
- "Tell us your math score from last term."
- "Do you like the teacher of this subject?"
- "Solve the following quadratic equation and explain your method."
E: One scenario asks how students motivate themselves when homework competes with a more attractive activity.

Q: Which of these is NOT among the SRL strategy categories that were coded?
+ Asking parents to do all the homework
- Self-evaluating
- Environmental structuring
- Goal setting and planning
E: The 14 strategies include self-evaluating, organizing and transforming, goal setting and planning, seeking information, keeping records, self-monitoring, environmental structuring, self-consequences, rehearsing and memorizing, seeking social assistance and reviewing. Having someone else do the work is not an SRL strategy.

Q: Who is included in "seeking social assistance"?
+ Peers, teachers and adults
- Only the homeroom teacher
- Only the desk partner
- Only a paid tutor
E: The category "seeking social assistance" is split into asking peers, teachers and adults.

Q: What does "reviewing" cover in the strategy list?
+ Looking back at notes, textbooks and old tests
- Looking at the whole class's grade sheet
- Looking again at one's own school application
- Looking at related scientific articles
E: Reviewing covers reviewing notes, textbooks and old tests.

Q: How many students were in the 1986 study and how were they divided?
+ 80 students: 40 in an advanced track and 40 in a lower track
- 40 students: 20 boys and 20 girls
- 100 students: 50 in grade 5 and 50 in grade 8
- 93 students, all in the advanced track
E: Zimmerman and Martinez-Pons (1986) used 80 high-school students, 40 in the advanced track and 40 in the lower track.

Q: What percentage of students did discriminant analysis classify correctly into their track in the 1986 study?
+ 93%
- 50%
- 70%
- 80%
E: Weighted strategy scores predicted the track of 93% of the students.

Q: What does the 93% mean when the sample is split 40/40?
+ It is far above the roughly 50% chance level, so strategies discriminate strongly between the groups
- It equals the chance level so it has no meaning
- It is below chance so strategies are useless
- It holds only for male students in the sample
E: With equal groups, random guessing gives about 50%; 93% shows strategy scores separate the two groups well.

Q: Of the 14 strategies, which one did NOT differ significantly between tracks?
+ Self-evaluating
- Goal setting and planning
- Keeping records
- Environmental structuring
E: The paper: advanced-track students reported significantly more use of every strategy except self-evaluating.

Q: What form did the lower track's "reactive" answers take in the interview?
+ "I do what the teacher says"
- "I make my own weekly review plan"
- "I set my own deadlines"
- "I ask a friend to explain it again"
E: "Reactive" answers show students passively waiting for direction, unlike proactive strategies.

Q: What do the lower track's "will" answers ("I try harder") show?
+ They say "try harder" without naming a method, so they lack proactive strategies
- They have many strategies but do not dare to say them
- They lack the verbal skill to express themselves
- They use strategies better than the advanced track
E: The paper explains that such non-SRL answers reflect lack of initiative, not poor ability to express.

Q: In the 1988 study, teacher ratings and standardized scores were factor analyzed. What resulted?
+ One SRL factor explaining nearly 80% of the variance
- Three independent factors, each explaining about 30%
- No significant factor
- One general-ability factor explaining 93% of the variance
E: Factor analysis showed a single SRL factor accounting for nearly 80% of the variance of the rated scores.

Q: What was the correlation between self-reported strategy use and the teacher-rated SRL factor?
+ r = .70
- r = .93
- r = .08
- r = .50
E: Zimmerman (1990): self-reported strategy use correlated .70 with the SRL factor, separate from a general-ability factor.

Q: If r = .70, what is the shared variance (r squared)?
+ 0.49
- 0.70
- 0.35
- 0.84
E: 0.70 times 0.70 is 0.49, i.e. about 49% shared variance.

Q: What trend across grades 5, 8 and 11 was found for SRL strategies?
+ The number of strategies rose from grade 5 to 8 and from grade 8 to 11
- The number of strategies declined with grade
- The number did not change between grades
- Only grade 8 used more strategies than grade 5
E: Zimmerman (1990) reports strategy use increasing with grade, and gifted students had higher self-efficacy.

Q: What do the "general-ability factor" and the "SRL factor" in the 1988 study tell us?
+ SRL strategy use is a separate dimension, not identical to general ability
- SRL and general ability are one and inseparable
- SRL has nothing to do with learning outcomes
- SRL appears only in students with high general ability
E: The paper stresses that self-reported strategy use correlates with the SRL factor, separate from a general-ability factor.

## Monitoring, self-instruction, development
Q: What did the Pressley and Ghatala research show about adults learning two strategies of different effectiveness?
+ They do not track the difference while doing the task and draw it only when prompted to monitor outcomes
- They always discover the better strategy without any prompt
- They always choose the worse strategy even when prompted
- They discover the difference only when working in a group
E: The paper: outcome monitoring does not happen spontaneously even in adults; only when prompted to track recall outcomes do they draw and use the information.

Q: What did Ghatala and colleagues (1986) show children need to be taught?
+ To monitor outcomes, attribute them to the strategy and use the information to decide
- To memorize more strategies
- To learn faster to finish in less time
- To work alone without feedback
E: Teaching outcome monitoring, attribution to strategy and deciding on that basis are skills that must be taught.

Q: What did Elliott-Faust and Pressley (1986) find with third graders?
+ Teaching verbal self-instruction and self-monitoring improved comprehension and maintenance of strategy use more than teaching the strategy alone
- Teaching the strategy alone was enough; adding self-monitoring did not help
- Teaching self-instruction reduced comprehension because students were distracted
- There was no difference among the training conditions
E: The paper cites research showing that adding self-instruction and self-monitoring to strategy training improves comprehension and keeps strategy use going.

Q: According to Paris and Newman (cited in Zimmerman 1990), what is typical of children under 7?
+ Naive and overly optimistic about their ability to learn, reflecting little
- Very accurate about their own ability
- Frequent use of many sophisticated strategies
- Always underestimating their own ability
E: Young children are often overly optimistic and assume effort is enough (Dweck and Elliott 1983); nearer adolescence self-perception is more accurate.

Q: What did Sawyer, Graham and Harris (1989) find about strategy training packages?
+ Multi-component packages did better, but the added self-regulation part did not reach statistical significance
- The added self-regulation part gave the best and a significant effect
- No package did better than strategy instruction alone
- Only the self-regulation part worked, the other parts were useless
E: Zimmerman (1990) uses this example to show it is unclear which component is most essential in training studies.

Q: What is the conclusion of Zimmerman (1990) about SRL training?
+ Training only one or two processes rarely has lasting effects; all three dimensions should be developed together
- Teaching a single strong strategy is enough for every subject
- Only motivation needs raising, no strategy instruction is needed
- Only metacognition needs teaching, motivation will follow
E: The paper concludes that training should cover all three dimensions: metacognitive, motivational and behavioral.

Q: What does Schunk stress about goal setting and self-efficacy (cited by Zimmerman 1990)?
+ They influence each other: goals nourish self-efficacy and self-efficacy helps in setting goals
- Goals reduce self-efficacy because they create pressure
- Self-efficacy fully determines goals in one direction
- The two have no relationship
E: Zimmerman (1990) cites Schunk on the reciprocal relation: proximal, specific goals nourish self-efficacy and vice versa.

## Puustinen and Pulkkinen 2001: five models
Q: How many models does the Puustinen and Pulkkinen (2001) review compare, and on which criteria?
+ Five models, on theoretical background, definition of SRL, components and empirical research
- Three models, on cost, time and number of participants
- Ten models, on learner age and school subject
- Two models, on gender and culture
E: The review compares the models of Boekaerts, Borkowski, Pintrich, Winne and Zimmerman on four criteria: theoretical background, definition, components, empirical research.

Q: Who are the authors of the five models compared?
+ Boekaerts, Borkowski, Pintrich, Winne and Zimmerman
- Bandura, Vygotsky, Piaget, Bruner and Dewey
- Kapur, Sweller, Collins, Brown and Paas
- Schraw, Flavell, Brown, Kluwe and Nelson
E: The paper selects five models with plenty of empirical support from the preceding decade.

Q: What is central to Boekaerts' adaptable-learning model?
+ Appraisal and several control systems: metacognitive, motivational and emotional
- Four stages with a COPES structure in each
- Four development levels: observation, emulation, self-control, self-regulation
- A table of four phases times four areas
E: Boekaerts places appraisal at the center: positive appraisal widens resources, negative appraisal leads to ego protection.

Q: Which instrument does Boekaerts use to measure motivation online before and after a task?
+ The On-line Motivation Questionnaire (OMQ)
- The MSLQ questionnaire
- The SRLIS interview
- Trace methodology
E: Boekaerts uses the On-line Motivation Questionnaire (OMQ) before and after a learning task.

Q: What are the two action modes in Boekaerts' model?
+ Fast, automatic (familiar situations) and conscious (new or difficult situations)
- Individual and collective
- Short-term and long-term
- Internal and external
E: Boekaerts distinguishes a fast/automatic action mode for familiar situations from a conscious mode for situations needing control.

Q: What is the focus of Borkowski's model?
+ Process-oriented metacognition: strategy selection and use, executive functions, the self-system
- Emotional appraisal before the task
- Groups of four phases times four areas
- Triadic reciprocal determinism
E: Borkowski stresses process-oriented metacognition and strategy training and transfer.

Q: What did Reid and Borkowski (1987) show to be the most effective training?
+ Self-control training plus attribution, still effective 10 months later
- Strategy training only, with the effect gone after one week
- Attribution training only, with no need to teach strategies
- No training gave a lasting effect
E: The study showed strategy training plus self-control and attribution gave the best effect, lasting up to 10 months.

Q: How is Pintrich's framework described?
+ Four phases (forethought, monitoring, control, reflection) times four areas (cognition, motivation/affect, behavior, context)
- Three cyclical phases and four development levels
- Four stages with a COPES structure
- Two control systems and two action modes
E: Pintrich presents a table of four phases by four areas as a heuristic framework.

Q: What are the four areas of Pintrich's framework?
+ Cognition, motivation and affect, behavior, and context
- Memory, attention, language, movement
- Goals, plans, execution, evaluation
- Person, behavior, environment, society
E: The four areas: cognition, motivation/affect, behavior and context.

Q: Which instrument is associated with Pintrich?
+ The MSLQ (Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire)
- The OMQ (On-line Motivation Questionnaire)
- The SRLIS (Self-Regulated Learning Interview Schedule)
- Trace methodology
E: Pintrich is tied to the MSLQ; Boekaerts to the OMQ; Zimmerman to the SRLIS; Winne to traces.

Q: In Pintrich's analysis of goal orientations, what are the two main axes?
+ Mastery or performance, and approach or avoidance
- Intrinsic or extrinsic, and short-term or long-term
- Individual or collective, and competitive or cooperative
- Cognitive or emotional, and internal or external
E: Goal orientations: mastery or performance, approach or avoidance.

Q: How many stages does the Winne and Hadwin model have, and what are they?
+ Four: task definition, goals and planning, strategy enactment, metacognitive adaptation
- Three: forethought, performance, self-reflection
- Four: forethought, monitoring, control, reflection
- Two: planning and execution
E: Winne and Hadwin describe four stages with a COPES structure in each.

Q: What does COPES stand for?
+ Conditions, Operations, Products, Evaluations, Standards
- Cognition, Observation, Planning, Effort, Strategy
- Control, Organization, Performance, Emotion, Self
- Context, Outcome, Process, Evidence, Skill
E: In Winne's model each stage has a COPES structure: Conditions, Operations, Products, Evaluations, Standards.

Q: What is distinctive about Winne's treatment of metacognitive monitoring?
+ Monitoring is present in every stage, not only in one phase
- Monitoring occurs only after the task is finished
- Monitoring occurs only in the planning stage
- Monitoring is the teacher's job, not the learner's
E: In Winne's model metacognitive monitoring appears at every stage (it is recursive).

Q: Winne and colleagues (2000) compared self-reports of using seven strategies with actual traces. What did they find?
+ A low correlation, meaning self-reports do not accurately reflect what learners actually do
- A very high correlation, so self-report is enough to measure SRL
- No correlation could be computed
- Self-reports are always lower than actual behavior
E: The paper: self-reports and traces (highlighting, writing notes) correlate low, raising a problem for relying on self-report alone.

Q: What did Hadwin and colleagues (1997) find about strategy reports?
+ Students report different strategies depending on context: reading to learn, writing a short essay, exam review
- Students report the same set of strategies in all contexts
- Students cannot describe their own study strategies
- Students report strategies only when taking an exam
E: Self-reported strategies depend on the task context, a reason for caution when generalizing.

Q: What is Winne's "trace methodology"?
+ Recording learners' actual behavior while they study, such as highlighting and notes, rather than asking them
- A questionnaire that learners fill in after studying
- An interview with teachers about students
- A final exam measuring content recall
E: Traces are real behavioral records, as opposed to self-report.

Q: On what theoretical base is Zimmerman's model in the review?

## Interpretation, traps, thesis design
Q: Why is using a single self-report survey to measure SRL risky?
+ Self-reports can diverge from real behavior (Winne) and change with context (Hadwin)
- Because self-reports are always too expensive to collect
- Because students never answer honestly
- Because self-reports measure only memory ability
E: Evidence from Winne and colleagues (2000) and Hadwin and colleagues (1997) shows self-reports diverge from traces and depend on context.

Q: What does combining self-report with behavioral trace data give a measurement?
+ A cross-check between what learners say and what they actually do
- It removes the need for any interview
- It replaces the learner's goal setting
- It makes every learner reach the maximum score
E: Because self-reports and traces correlate weakly, using both gives a fuller picture.

Q: Why is the claim "SRL is a fixed trait, you either have it or not" wrong?
+ SRL is the selective use of processes per task and can be taught
- Because SRL exists only in adults
- Because SRL exists only in high achievers
- Because SRL is determined only by external motivation
E: Zimmerman (2002) states clearly that SRL is an adaptive process, not a fixed trait.

Q: Why is the claim "studying alone equals self-regulation" wrong?
+ Self-regulated learners often seek help; the "self" is in initiative, not isolation
- Because studying alone is always less effective than group study
- Because self-regulated learners never study alone
- Because SRL happens only in crowded classrooms
E: Zimmerman (2002) points out SRL is not asocial; seeking help is one SRL strategy.

Q: Why is teaching a single strategy in isolation usually not enough?

- Because a single strategy cannot be learned
- Because strategies work only for young children
- Because students always forget a strategy after a day
E: Zimmerman (1990) and Ghatala and colleagues show monitoring, attribution and decision-making must be taught, not only an isolated strategy.
