## Risko and Gilbert 2016: the concept
Q: How do Risko and Gilbert (2016) define cognitive offloading?
+ The use of physical action to alter the information processing requirements of a task so as to reduce cognitive demand
- Memorizing a great deal of information so that no outside tools are needed when doing a task
- Splitting a task into many steps so that other people do each step one after another
- Resting between work sessions so that memory recovers and remembers things better
E: The paper: "the use of physical action to alter the information processing requirements of a task so as to reduce cognitive demand."

Q: Which example does the paper give of cognitive offloading?
+ Tilting the head to perceive a rotated image, or programming a phone to remind you of an upcoming appointment
- Closing the eyes to concentrate on a hard problem or counting silently from one to a hundred
- Memorizing friends' phone numbers so that they need not be stored in the phone's contacts
- Reading a lesson aloud many times to remember it faster before walking into the exam room
E: The opening: "If you have ever tilted your head to perceive a rotated image, or programmed a smartphone to remind you of an upcoming appointment, you have engaged in cognitive offloading".

Q: Why does cognitive offloading help people?
+ It helps overcome capacity limits, minimize computational effort and achieve feats that would not otherwise be possible
- It lets the brain rest completely so that brain areas do not have to work during a task
- It lets people avoid learning any new skill at all for the whole of their lives
- It makes every task reach maximum performance whatever the ability of the tool used
E: The paper: "Offloading cognition helps us to overcome such capacity limitations, minimize computational effort, and achieve cognitive feats that would not otherwise be possible."

Q: In which domains has cognitive offloading been shown to improve performance?
+ Perception, memory, arithmetic, counting and spatial reasoning
- Only long-term memory, with no other domain demonstrated in the paper
- Only arithmetic, with no other domain demonstrated in the paper
- Only language and reading comprehension, with no other domain mentioned
E: The paper: "cognitive offloading has been demonstrated to improve performance across several domains (e.g., perception, memory, arithmetic, counting, and spatial reasoning)".

Q: What does the paper say about the history of offloading?
+ It is ancient (finger counting, abacuses, knotted quipus) but only recently the target of systematic study
- It is entirely new, appearing only with computers and the Internet in the last few decades
- It has been studied systematically for hundreds of years by philosophers
- It exists only in modern humans and has no history in ancient societies
E: The paper: "the phenomenon it refers to is ancient (e.g., finger-counting and abacuses in numerical cognition, systems of knots or quipus for memory)" but "has rarely been the target of systematic experimental investigation in and of itself."

Q: What are the two kinds of offloading by where the load goes?
+ Onto the body (such as head tilts, gestures) and into the world (such as writing, typing, sketching)
- Into long-term memory and into short-term memory of the very person doing the task
- Onto the teacher and onto classmates when help is needed while doing the task
- Onto easy tasks and onto hard tasks depending on the difficulty of the task
E: The paper: "actions that offload cognitive demands onto-the-body and into-the-world".

Q: What is external normalization?
+ Using physical action such as head tilting to align a stimulus with a representation stored in memory
- Using internal mental rotation to align a stimulus with a representation stored in memory
- Using an Internet search engine to look up information not yet known when facing a hard question
- Using an external reminder to trigger an intention that will be carried out later on
E: Glossary: "External normalization: the use of physical action (e.g., head tilt) to align a stimulus with a representation stored in memory."

Q: What is internal normalization?
+ An internal transformation (such as mental rotation) that aligns a stimulus representation with one stored in memory
- Tilting the head to align the orientation of a stimulus with a representation in long-term memory
- Writing on paper so as not to keep information in the head during a complex task
- Creating an external cue to trigger a delayed intention at the appropriate time
E: Glossary: "Internal normalization: use of internal transformation (i.e., mental rotation) to align an internal representation of a stimulus with a representation stored in memory."

Q: What drives spontaneous head tilting according to the paper?
+ Internal demand (rotation cost): the more disoriented the display or the more items, the more likely people are to tilt
- Only the color of the words on the screen decides whether people tilt their heads
- Only the length of each word decides whether people tilt their heads while reading
- Nothing decides it, because head tilting always occurs completely at random
E: The paper: "individuals are more likely to spontaneously physically rotate as the display becomes more disoriented or as the number of items in the display increases... as the internal demands... increase, the likelihood of spontaneous external normalization also increases."

Q: Why are internal demand and offloading called "dissociable"?
+ When both words and frame rotate versus only words, costs and physiological demand are similar but tilting rates differ greatly
- Because internal demand is always zero in every condition, so it cannot predict offloading
- Because offloading occurs only when internal demand is zero in every experimental condition
- Because internal demand has never been measured in any study of offloading
E: The paper: the two conditions "yield similar rotation costs and similar responses on a physiological measure of demand. Nevertheless, spontaneous rates of external normalization are much higher when both the words and the frame are rotated".

Q: How does the paper explain that dissociation?
+ People rely on an erroneous metacognitive evaluation of demand, believing word-and-frame rotated displays are slower, more error-prone and more effortful
- People deliberately slow down to be accurate, so rotation cost does not reflect true effort
- People cannot see the frame of the display, so they cannot decide whether to rotate
- People always tilt their heads out of habit and are not influenced by any evaluation
E: The paper: "individuals rely on an erroneous metacognitive evaluation of demand... individuals incorrectly report that rotated word and frame displays are more time-consuming and error-prone, and judge these displays to be more effortful to read".

Q: What is intention offloading?
+ Creating cues in the environment to trigger a delayed intention, such as sticky notes or reminders
- Saving a document's content in the computer so that it can be looked up in the future
- Keeping all one's intentions by rehearsing them continually in one's head
- Handing every decision about one's actions over to other people to carry out
E: Glossary: "Intention offloading: creation of a cue in the external environment to trigger a delayed intention."

Q: What is prospective memory?
+ The ability to remember to carry out intended actions in the future
- The ability to recall past events accurately and completely
- The ability to predict the outcome of an action before carrying it out
- The ability to remember the location of familiar objects at home or at work
E: The paper: "Our ability to remember delayed intentions is termed 'prospective memory'".

Q: Why is prospective memory often supported by external tools?
+ Intentions are often not triggered by perceptual cues so action must be self-initiated; tools supply cues that trigger them
- Because humans have no ability to remember intentions in any circumstances
- Because external tools are always more accurate than any human memory
- Because intentions are always too long to hold completely in the head
E: The paper: "in many cases, our intentions are not effectively triggered by perceptual cues in our environment, and therefore action must be self-initiated... people have long supported prospective memory by using external tools to supply perceptual cues".

Q: Which factors increase intention offloading (setting reminders)?
+ Increased memory load or interruptions, since both impair performance when offloading is prevented
- Plenty of free time and no one disturbing the person during the task
- A very simple task that the person has been used to for many years
- Complete belief that one's memory is better than any external tool
E: The paper: "Individuals are more likely to offload intentions when their memory load increases or when they encounter interruptions; both these factors impair performance when offloading is prevented."

Q: What did people with lower memory confidence do in the intention offloading task?
+ They set more reminders, even after controlling for objective ability
- They set fewer reminders because they think reminders do not work for them
- They set no reminders because they distrust any outside tool
- They set the same number of reminders whatever their confidence
E: The paper: "Individuals with lower confidence in their memory abilities were more likely to spontaneously set reminders, even after controlling for any influence of objective ability".

Q: Is the link between confidence and intention offloading domain-general?
+ Yes; people less confident in perceptual judgments also set more reminders in the intention offloading task
- No; only confidence in memory relates to setting reminders in the task
- No; confidence has no relation to setting reminders in any domain
- Yes; but only in people who have previously suffered brain injury
E: The paper: "this relation with metacognitive confidence is domain-general. When individuals performed a separate perceptual judgment task... individuals with lower confidence in their perceptual judgments set more reminders in the intention offloading task."

Q: What is a salient benefit of offloading intentions into the environment?
+ Offloaded representations may be more durable and less prone to distortion, raising the chance the intention is fulfilled
- The person no longer needs to remember having set a reminder, so no extra effort is spent
- External reminders always fire at exactly the right time, so intentions never fail
- External reminders make people forget the goals they had set altogether
E: The paper: "offloaded representations may be more durable and less prone to distortion than those stored internally, leading to an increased likelihood of intention fulfillment".

Q: In the short-term memory task, how often did participants write items down when they needed to remember only 2?
+ About 40% of the time, even though performance was already at ceiling without offloading
- About 4% of the time, since performance was already at ceiling without offloading
- About 100% of the time, since they did not trust their memory with only two items
- About 0% of the time, since two items are too few for anyone to write down
E: The paper: "did so about 40% of the time when they were required to remember only two items, a memory load at which performance was already at ceiling without offloading."

Q: What is notable about writing when remembering only 2 items?
+ Participants erroneously judged that offloading would improve their performance in this condition
- Participants correctly judged offloading would not help but did it anyway out of habit
- Participants did not even realize they were writing the items down while working
- Participants always found that writing slowed and worsened them, so wrote only when forced
E: The paper: "Crucially, individuals erroneously judged that offloading would improve their performance in this latter condition."

Q: What three explanations are given for superfluous offloading?
+ An undetected benefit, a bias against cognitive effort, and an erroneous metacognitive belief
- Curiosity about the tool, pressure from observers, and a wish to finish the task sooner
- Very poor memory, too little practice time, and no guidance from experienced people
- Fear of low marks, fear of criticism, and fear of having to redo the task again
E: The paper: "(i) an undetected performance benefit (ii) a bias against cognitive effort (Box 2), and/or (iii) an erroneous metacognitive belief that the offloading will in fact benefit performance."

Q: What is the "choice/no choice" paradigm?
+ Some conditions oblige a particular strategy while others let participants freely choose among available strategies
- Some tasks are chosen by participants while others are preselected by the experimenter
- Some questions allow free-form answers while others allow only true or false
- Some participants are paid while others are not paid for taking part
E: Box 1: "in some conditions participants are obliged (i.e., they have no choice) to employ a particular strategy and in others they are free (i.e., they have the choice) to select among a set of available strategies."

Q: What does the "no choice–internal" condition measure?
+ Performance when the person can rely only on internal memory, unable to store information externally
- Performance when the person must store information externally on paper or a computer
- Spontaneous offloading when the person may choose freely between internal and external storage
- Satisfaction of the person with strategies for storing information inside the head
E: Box 1: "No Choice–Internal: Individuals are tasked with remembering a given piece of information without being able to store it externally. This condition provides a measure of performance when the individual has to rely solely on their internal memory."

Q: What does the "choice" condition measure?
+ Spontaneous offloading when people freely choose between storing internally and externally
- Performance when people are forced to use only their own internal memory
- Performance when people are forced to store all information on an external device
- The difficulty of the task when the amount of information to remember is doubled
E: Box 1: "Choice: Participants are allowed to freely choose between storing information internally or externally. This condition provides a measure of the spontaneous offloading of memory demands".

Q: What is one challenge of the choice/no choice paradigm?
+ Obliging a strategy may be ineffective, and may add load from having to inhibit a preferred strategy
- It is so costly that it can be applied only with groups of thousands of participants
- It cannot be used for memory and can be used only for perceptual tasks
- It always gives the same result whether participants are given a choice or are forced
E: Box 1: "attempting to oblige individuals to adopt a strategy might not be effective in some circumstances. In addition, forcing individuals to use a particular strategy could introduce demands associated with being required to inhibit the use of another possibly preferred strategy."

Q: What two opposed views on effort bias does Box 2 present?
+ Cognitive impartiality (no bias about type of effort) and the "cognitive miser" view (a bias against cognitive effort)
- A bias against physical effort and a bias against social effort in the person
- A bias toward modern tools and a bias toward traditional tools among people
- A bias toward easy tasks and a bias toward hard tasks in human beings
E: Box 2: "On the cognitive impartiality view the cognitive system has no bias or is indifferent to the type of effort required... An alternative view, which might be called the 'cognitive miser' view, is that individuals have an inherent bias against expending cognitive effort."

Q: According to the "soft constraints" hypothesis, what determines the preferred solution?
+ The amount of time required, not the type of effort; the faster solution is preferred
- The type of effort required, with cognitive effort always avoided more than motor effort
- The accuracy of the solution, with the most accurate always preferred
- The opinion of the people nearby about the solution the person is weighing
E: Box 2: "according to the soft constraints hypothesis, it is not the type of effort but rather the amount of time required that determines the preferred solution (i.e., the solution with the shorter time being the preferred one)."

Q: What is the "minimal memory" view?
+ The cognitive system is biased toward minimizing memory demands, even at higher perceptual-motor cost
- The cognitive system is biased toward using as much memory as possible to avoid outside tools
- The cognitive system has no bias and is completely neutral to every type of effort
- The cognitive system always picks the solution with the shortest total time
E: Box 2: "the 'minimal memory' view, according to which the cognitive system is biased toward minimizing demands on memory (even in the face of potentially greater perceptual-motor costs)."

## Risko and Gilbert 2016: transactive memory and costs
Q: What is a transactive memory system?
+ A memory system in which knowledge is distributed across two or more individuals so the whole system knows more than any one
- A memory system that exists only inside one person's brain and has no link to others
- A memory system in which everyone remembers exactly the same information in a group
- An artificial memory system that completely replaces natural human memory at work
E: The paper: "knowledge is distributed across two or more individuals such that the system as a whole knows more than any one individual"; glossary: "a memory system composed of a group that collectively encodes, stores, and retrieves knowledge."

Q: Where has recent research extended transactive memory?
+ To human-technology systems, such as computers and the Internet
- To systems among different animal species living in the same area
- To systems among generations within the same family over many years
- To systems among nerve cells in the same region of the brain
E: The paper: "extended this notion of socially distributed memory to human-technology transactive memory systems".

Q: What is the defining attribute of a transactive memory system?
+ A shift from remembering "what" to remembering "where"
- A shift from remembering "where" to remembering "when" information appeared
- A shift from remembering "when" to remembering "why" information appeared
- A shift from remembering "who" to remembering "how much" information was saved
E: The paper: "a defining attribute of a transactive memory system is a shift from remembering 'what' to remembering 'where'."

Q: What does the meeting-file example illustrate?
+ After saving a meeting's content to a file, you need not remember the content but must remember where the file is
- After saving a meeting's content to a file, you need not remember anything about it at all
- After saving a meeting's content to a file, you remember both content and location better
- After saving a meeting's content to a file, you always forget even that there was a meeting
E: The paper: "when you offload information about a meeting to a file on your computer, you no longer need to remember the content of the file, but you do need to remember where to find it."

Q: What did saving an initial word list do for memory of a second list?
+ It enhanced memory for the second list by reducing proactive interference
- It reduced memory for the second list because the brain was overloaded
- It had no effect on memory for the second list
- It made people forget both lists after a while
E: The paper: "when individuals saved an initial list of words it enhanced memory for a second list [35]. The authors argued that saving reduced the likelihood that the first list of words interfered with memory for the second (i.e., reduced proactive interference)".

Q: What does the paper say about online searching and metacognition?
+ Searching for information about one topic can make people believe they have more knowledge "in the head" about another topic
- Searching online always makes people underestimate their own knowledge
- Searching online has no effect on how people evaluate themselves
- Searching online lets people know exactly what they do and do not know
E: The paper: "searching for information online about one topic can lead individuals to believe that they have more knowledge 'in-the-head' and generate more 'brain activity' when answering questions about another topic".

Q: What did people who recently used Google on a quiz tend to do afterwards?
+ Reported higher cognitive self-esteem and predicted better performance on the next quiz even without help
- Reported lower cognitive self-esteem and predicted worse performance on the next quiz
- Reported unchanged self-esteem and predicted their score accurately on the next quiz
- Refused to take the next quiz because they felt unable to do it without the Internet
E: The paper: "individuals who had recently used Google to help them complete a quiz reported higher levels of cognitive self-esteem. They also predicted that they would do better on a subsequent quiz, even without help from external resources".

Q: How did people react to general-knowledge questions when they knew they could look answers up afterward?
+ They were more likely to say "don't know" and reported lower feeling-of-knowing
- They said "don't know" less often and reported higher feeling-of-knowing than without the Internet
- They answered more accurately because they were reassured they could check later
- They refused to answer any general-knowledge question, trusting the Internet to answer
E: The paper: "when they knew they would subsequently have access to the internet, participants were more likely to answer 'don't know' and reported lower feeling-of-knowing to the trivia questions [52]."

Q: In the GPS study of Box 3, how did those who followed turn-by-turn directions do when driving the route again without aid?
+ Worse, although the first time they outperformed the no-aid group, and they remembered route scenes less
- Better than the other group because they had learned the route from the aided drive
- The same as the other group since both had driven the route before
- They could not drive again because they had forgotten the route completely
E: Box 3: "individuals who drove a predetermined route using a turn-by-turn navigation system outperformed individuals who had no aid. However, individuals in the former group had poorer memory for scenes from the route and, when asked to drive the route a second time without an aid, performed more poorly".

Q: What did the photography study show about memory?
+ Photographing an object impaired memory for it a day later, and photographing only part of it reduced the cost
- Photographing an object improved memory for it a day later, most of all if the whole object was photographed
- Photographing had no effect on memory for the object a day after it was taken
- Photographing impaired memory only if the photographer knew the photo would be viewed again
E: Box 3: "Memory for the objects tested a day later revealed impaired memory for the photographed objects. In a subsequent experiment, taking a picture of only part of the object, rather than the whole object, to some extent ameliorated this cost".

Q: What is notable about the photography study?
+ Participants did not necessarily expect the pictures during the memory test, so de-prioritizing might occur spontaneously
- Participants were sure they would see the pictures again during the memory test
- Participants had to delete the pictures right after taking them to avoid copying
- Participants used professional cameras so the pictures were always very detailed
E: Box 3: "individuals did not necessarily expect to have the pictures available during the memory test. Thus, the de-prioritization of information that is potentially available externally might occur spontaneously".

Q: What is "automation complacency"?
+ Failure to be sufficiently vigilant regarding the performance of automated processes
- Uncritical reliance on the output of an automated decision aid
- Gradual deterioration of a developed skill from lack of use over a long time
- The decision to offload always being made actively and consciously by the user
E: Box 3: "'automation complacency', the failure to be sufficiently vigilant with respect to the performance of automated processes".

Q: What is "automation bias"?
+ The tendency to uncritically rely on the output of an automated decision aid
- Insufficient vigilance toward processes that have been fully automated
- Deterioration of a developed skill because it has not been practiced for long
- Bias in an automated tool when giving results to different groups of users
E: Box 3: "'automation bias', the tendency to uncritically rely on the output of an automated decision aid".

Q: What is "skill decay"?
+ Deterioration of a developed ability over time from long-term reliance on automated processes
- New skills being impossible to develop when the learner is very young
- Automated tools working worse and worse after many years of continuous use
- Users forgetting the passwords of the automated devices they use
E: Box 3: "The long-term reliance on automated processes could also lead to cognitive 'skill decay' where a developed ability deteriorates over time".

Q: What does the highly automated vehicle example show about automation's effects?
+ Effects depend on how freed resources are allocated: situation awareness improves if attention goes to the environment, worsens if devoted to unrelated tasks
- Highly automated driving always improves situation awareness whatever the driver does
- Highly automated driving always reduces situation awareness whatever the driver does
- Highly automated driving has no effect on the driver's situation awareness
E: Box 3: "driving a highly automated vehicle can improve situation awareness relative to manual driving if individuals are motivated to attend to the environment, but can impair it if they decide to devote 'freed resources' to driving-unrelated tasks".

## Risko and Gilbert 2016: the framework and implications
Q: How does the paper's metacognitive framework treat offloading?
+ As a strategy chosen after a strategy-selection phase influenced by metacognitive evaluation of internal and "extended" options
- As an automatic reflex that never involves any evaluation or choice by the person
- As a decision made by others on the user's behalf without the user knowing
- As a behavior occurring only when the outside tool is proven perfectly accurate
E: The paper: "offloading represents a type of strategy to achieve some cognitive goal, and follows a strategy-selection phase... influenced by a metacognitive evaluation of the available options (arrow A)".

Q: What does arrow A in Figure 3 represent?
+ Metacognitive evaluation of internal and extended strategies influencing strategy selection
- Use of a strategy altering the user's later metacognitive evaluations
- Offloading directly affecting lower-level cognitive processes such as memory
- The external tool updating itself to fit each user's needs
E: Caption of Figure 3: "selecting between offloading and relying on internal processes is influenced by metacognitive evaluations... (arrow A)".

Q: What does arrow B represent?
+ Engaging in internal or extended strategies changes later metacognitive evaluations
- Metacognitive evaluation determining which strategy is chosen next time
- Offloading directly affecting lower-level cognitive processes such as memory
- The outside tool influencing how fast the user processes information
E: Caption: "engaging in either internal or extended strategies can influence subsequent metacognitive evaluations (arrow B)... after successful use of a GPS system we may come to believe that it is a more reliable guide than our unaided memory."

Q: What does arrow C represent?
+ Offloading directly affects lower-level cognitive processes, such as reduced spatial memory after relying on GPS
- Metacognitive evaluation changing how the user picks tools next time
- Extended strategies being chosen based on beliefs about tool reliability
- The outside tool adjusting itself to compensate for the user's cognitive decline
E: Caption: "Offloading can also directly impact on our lower-level cognitive processes (arrow C). An example of this would be a reduction in our internal spatial memory for a location after reliance on GPS navigation."

Q: Why does the model predict a "self-reinforcing loop" with GPS?
+ Offloading reduces internal memory and confidence (C and B), so next time reliance on the tool is likelier (A)
- Because GPS is always accurate, so the user never needs memory again
- Because users always remember the route after using GPS once
- Because the GPS maker requires users to use the device whenever they travel
E: The paper: "The tendency to offload wayfinding to the more accurate GPS will likely reduce both our internal spatial memory... (arrow C) and our metacognitive confidence in it (arrow B), which will in turn increase the likelihood that we choose to rely on the external artefact in the future (arrow A)."

Q: What does the Inuit wayfinding example illustrate?
+ A drift away from reliance on internal capabilities in an environment with effective cognitive technologies
- That the Inuit never use outside tools for navigating
- That the Inuit have better spatial memory than every other community in every setting
- That the Inuit fully reject modern technology in daily life
E: The paper: "a drift away from reliance on internal capabilities when situated in an environment with effective cognitive technologies ([68] for an example of this type of drift in the context of Inuit wayfinding)."

Q: Is the decision to offload necessarily a conscious one?
+ No; the strategy-selection phase does not imply awareness of choosing (for example gesture often occurs unaware)
- Yes; every act of offloading needs conscious deliberation and an explicit choice
- No; but only for young children, since adults always offload consciously
- Yes; if people are not aware, the behavior does not count as offloading
E: The paper: "the strategy-selection phase postulated above does not necessarily imply that individuals are aware of making a choice... gesture... can occur without individuals necessarily being aware of it."

Q: Which two practical implications does the paper highlight?
+ People with impaired unaided cognition may benefit especially from offloading, and applications in education
- Offloading benefits only healthy people and always harms people with cognitive impairment
- Offloading has applications only in industry and none in education
- Offloading has applications only for children and none for older adults
E: The paper: "First, individuals with impaired unaided cognitive ability may particularly benefit from cognitive offloading... The second general area... is education".

Q: How does gesture help children learn in education?
+ By "lightening the load", a benefit that appears to outstrip offloading onto external manipulatives
- By raising cognitive load so children must try harder and remember better
- By fully replacing the teacher's spoken explanation in every subject in class
- By working only with young children and losing all effect as they grow a little
E: The paper: "gesture helps children learn by 'lightening the load' [11] and, interestingly, this benefit appears to outstrip that garnered by offloading demands onto external manipulatives [77]."

Q: What is the key condition for offloading to benefit learning?
+ The offloaded demand must be unnecessary for the learning goal, and what is saved must be redistributed productively
- The offloaded demand must be the most important part of the learning goal so learners need not do it
- The offloaded demand should be as much as possible so learners have plenty of rest afterwards
- The offloaded demand should be picked randomly by learners without considering anything else
E: The paper: "any benefit of offloading will be contingent on the fact that the demand being offloaded is unnecessary with respect to the learning goal... it is important that what is 'saved' by offloading is redistributed productively rather than being re-allocated to superfluous activities".

Q: Which load distinctions does the paper reference?
+ Necessary/intrinsic/germane load versus unnecessary/extraneous load
- Physical load, emotional load and social load in learning activities
- Short-term load, long-term load and medium-term load in a course
- Teacher load, student load and parent load in the teaching process
E: The paper: "see [78] for relevant distinctions between necessary/intrinsic/germane load and unnecessary/extraneous load in learning" (links to Module 02).

Q: What long-term open question does the paper raise?
+ How extended offloading affects unaided ability ("does Google make us stupid?") and metacognitive evaluations
- Whether extended offloading makes search engines work better over time
- Whether extended offloading lowers the cost of technology purchases for users
- Whether extended offloading raises the number of Internet users worldwide
E: Outstanding Questions: "How does offloading over an extended period of time impact on our unaided mental abilities (does Google make us stupid)? How will it influence our metacognitive evaluation of our abilities?"

## Sparrow, Liu and Wegner 2011: Google effects on memory
Q: What is the central claim of Sparrow, Liu and Wegner (2011)?
+ The Internet has become a form of external or transactive memory, where information is stored collectively outside ourselves
- The Internet makes everyone remember all information better since it is always there to consult
- The Internet is useful only for entertainment and has no effect on human memory
- The Internet always gives wrong information, so users must memorize everything they need
E: The abstract: "The Internet has become a primary form of external or transactive memory, where information is stored collectively outside ourselves."

Q: What two main conclusions from the four studies does the abstract state?
+ With hard questions people are primed to think of computers; expecting future access, they recall content less but recall where to find it more
- With easy questions people think of computers; expecting future access they recall content more
- With hard questions people refuse to answer; expecting future access they forget where to find it
- With easy questions people study further; expecting future access they recall both content and location
E: The abstract: "when faced with difficult questions, people are primed to think about computers and... when people expect to have future access to information, they have lower rates of recall of the information itself and enhanced recall instead for where to access it."

Q: What does the modified Stroop task in Experiment 1 measure?
+ Color-naming time for words, slower when the word draws attention (is primed)
- How many words a participant can recall after seeing a long list
- How many trivia questions a participant answers correctly in a block
- How much a participant likes words related to computers and the Internet
E: The paper: "modified Stroop task (a color-naming task...)... People who have been disposed to think about a certain topic typically show slowed reaction times (RTs) for naming the color of the word when the word itself is of interest and is more accessible".

Q: After the hard-question block, what were color-naming times for computer versus general words?
+ 712 ms versus 591 ms (computer words slower)
- 591 ms versus 712 ms (computer words faster)
- 603 ms versus 559 ms (computer words slower)
- 818 ms versus 614 ms (computer words slower)
E: The paper: "computer words were more accessible [color-naming RT mean (M) = 712 ms, SD = 413 ms] than general words (M = 591 ms, SD = 204 ms) after... difficult question blocks".

Q: What were color-naming times for computer versus general words after easy questions?
+ 603 ms versus 559 ms
- 712 ms versus 591 ms
- 818 ms versus 614 ms
- 638 ms versus 584 ms
E: The paper: "Computer terms also interfered somewhat more with color naming (M = 603 ms, SD = 193 ms) than general terms (M = 559 ms, SD = 182 ms) after easy questions".

Q: How did search engine names (Google, Yahoo) compare with general brands (Target, Nike) after hard questions?
+ 818 ms versus 614 ms: search engine names interfered clearly more
- 614 ms versus 818 ms: general brands interfered clearly more
- 638 ms versus 584 ms: the two kinds of words interfered equally after hard questions
- 712 ms versus 591 ms: the two kinds of words did not differ in any block
E: The paper: "search engine brands after both easy questions (M = 638 ms...) and hard questions (M = 818 ms, SD = 517 ms) created more interference than general brands after easy questions (M = 584 ms...) and hard questions (M = 614 ms...)".

Q: How is the result of Experiment 1 interpreted?
+ Not knowing the answer to general-knowledge questions primes the need to search, so computer interference is especially acute
- Knowing the answer to general-knowledge questions makes people think more about computers
- Easy questions make people think less about computers since they need not look answers up
- The experiment allows no conclusion about priming the concept of computers
E: The paper: "not knowing the answer to general-knowledge questions primes the need to search for the answer, and subsequently computer interference is particularly acute."

Q: What design did Experiment 2 use?
+ A 2 (believing saved or erased) by 2 (told to remember or not) between-subjects design with 40 trivia statements typed into a computer
- A 3 (saved generically, saved to a folder, erased) by 2 within-subjects design with 30 statements
- A 2 (easy, hard) by 2 (computer words, general words) within-subjects design with Stroop blocks
- A single group doing all four conditions in sequence with six different folder names
E: The paper: "a 2 by 2 between-subject experiment by reading 40 memorable trivia statements... typed them into the computer... Half the participants believed the computer would save what was typed; half believed the item would be erased... half... asked explicitly to try to remember".

Q: What were the mean recall proportions in Experiment 2?
+ Erase 0.31 and erase-remember 0.29; save 0.22 and save-remember 0.19
- Erase 0.22 and erase-remember 0.19; save 0.31 and save-remember 0.29
- All four conditions about 0.25 with no meaningful differences
- Erase 0.93; erase-remember 0.88; save 0.85; save-remember 0.61

- Only the instruction to remember was significant; belief in saved or erased was not

E: The paper: "significant main effect for only the saved/erased manipulation"; "The main effect of the instruction to explicitly remember or not was not significant".

Q: To which research area do the authors link Experiment 2's result?

- Working memory: working memory capacity limits how many statements people can type
- Sensory memory: sensory information lasts only seconds so cannot be remembered
E: The paper: "This finding corresponds to previous work on directed forgetting, showing that when people don't believe they will need information for a later exam, they do not recall it at the same rate as when they do believe they will need it".

Q: What conclusion do the authors draw from Experiment 3?
+ Believing information will not be accessible boosts memory for content, while believing it is saved boosts memory that it can be accessed
- Believing information will not be accessible lowers memory for both content and accessibility
- Believing information is saved boosts memory for content and lowers memory for location
- Believing information is saved or erased has no effect on memory for content

E: The paper: "participants recalled the places where the statements were kept (M = 0.49, SD = 0.26) better than they recalled the statements themselves (M = 0.23, SD = 0.14), between-subject t(31) = 6.70, P < 0.001".

- 0.38
- 0.30
- 0.11
E: The paper: "particularly poor at recalling both statement and folder (M = 0.17, SD = 0.16)".

+ 0.30
- 0.17
- 0.11
- 0.38
E: The paper: "equally likely to recall the folder, when they didn't recall the statement (M = 0.30, SD = 0.16)".

Q: What does that pattern show?
+ People do not remember "where" when they know "what", but remember where to find information when they cannot recall it
- People always remember both "what" and "where" together whenever they remember either
- People never remember "where" whether or not they remember "what"
- People remember "what" but forget "where" in most cases in the experiment
E: The paper: "people don't remember 'where' when they know 'what' but do remember where to find the information when they don't recall it."

Q: What limitation of Experiment 4 do the authors mention?

- 18/18 (100%)
E: The paper: "In the LLM-assisted group, 83.3% of participants (15/18) failed to provide a correct quotation, whereas only 11.1% (2/18) in both the Search-Engine and Brain-Only groups encountered the same difficulty."

Q: What is "cognitive debt" according to Kosmyna?
+ Repeated reliance on systems like LLMs replacing the effortful processes needed for independent thinking, deferring effort now but costing later
- The money users must pay to subscribe to an LLM over a long period
- The make-up study hours students must do to pass courses that use LLMs
- The amount of information an LLM has not been trained on and cannot answer
E: The paper: "cognitive debt, a condition in which repeated reliance on external systems like LLMs replaces the effortful cognitive processes required for independent thinking. Cognitive debt defers mental effort in the short term but results in long-term costs".

Q: What long-term costs of cognitive debt do the authors list?
+ Diminished critical inquiry, increased vulnerability to manipulation and decreased creativity
- Faster writing, longer essays and higher average scores for users
- Higher energy costs, higher tuition costs and higher equipment costs
- Smaller vocabulary, weaker typing skills and weaker skimming skills
E: The paper: "long-term costs, such as diminished critical inquiry, increased vulnerability to manipulation, decreased creativity."

Q: What educational model does the preprint suggest?
+ Delaying AI integration until learners have engaged in sufficient self-driven cognitive effort
- Integrating AI from day one so learners get used to it and quickly reach high scores
- Banning AI permanently in all learning activities at every level
- Letting learners choose to use AI at any time with no guidance
E: The paper: "these findings support an educational model that delays AI integration until learners have engaged in sufficient self-driven cognitive effort."

Q: Which sample limitation do the authors state?
+ A limited number of participants from a narrow area (a few large institutions close together), not gender balanced
- A very large sample, so individuals could not be analyzed in detail
- Participants were all long-time experts working in various industries
- Participants were randomly drawn from the whole population of the US and Canada
E: The paper: "a limited number of participants recruited from a specific geographical area, several large academic institutions, located very close to each other... ensuring that the study is more gender balanced."

Q: Why can Kosmyna's results not be generalized to all LLMs and tasks?

- Because the study used many different models without controlling which was used
- Because the study looked only at programming and math tasks, not essay writing
- Because the study was run in a single session with no repeated sessions
E: The paper: "This study was performed using ChatGPT... we cannot directly generalize the obtained results to other LLM models"; "findings are context-dependent and are focused on writing an essay in an educational setting and may not generalize across tasks."

Q: Which measurement limitation is stated?

## Interpretation, traps and thesis design
Q: Why should offloading not be treated as always bad?
+ Risko and Gilbert show offloading helps overcome limits and improve performance; what matters is whether the offloaded load is needed for the learning goal
- Because offloading always makes learners remember longer in every task and setting
- Because offloading is harmful only when the outside tool is inaccurate and always wrong
- Because offloading is harmful only to children and never harmful to adults
E: Risko and Gilbert: offloading improves performance in many domains; the benefit is "contingent on the fact that the demand being offloaded is unnecessary with respect to the learning goal".

Q: Why be careful drawing causal conclusions from the five studies in this module?
+ Gerlich is a cross-sectional self-report survey, Lee is self-report, Kosmyna is a small preprint, Sparrow used trivia
- Because all five studies were retracted from journals for serious method errors
- Because all five studies had randomized control groups, so results are always certain
- Because all five studies studied only animals and not human beings
E: The module's overall remark based on each design: Gerlich cross-sectional; Lee self-report; Kosmyna "Preprint, under review" with 54 people; Sparrow used trivia statements.

Q: What role does erroneous metacognitive evaluation play in offloading?
+ People can offload superfluously (such as writing when remembering only 2 items) by misjudging need and benefit
- People always judge need accurately, so they never offload superfluously
- People do not use metacognitive evaluation when deciding whether to offload
- Metacognitive evaluation affects learning but not offloading
E: Risko and Gilbert: metacognitive evaluations can be wrong, leading to "suboptimal offloading behavior"; for example offloading 40% of the time with only 2 items.

Q: In teaching introductory programming, which part could reasonably be carried by the AI (design inference)?
+ Extraneous load such as typing syntax or looking up APIs, saving effort for analysis, planning and debugging
- All the analysis of the problem, planning the solution and debugging until the code runs correctly
- All of the students' self-evaluation of understanding and reflection on how they learn
- No part at all, since every part of programming must be done entirely by students
E: Design inference from Risko and Gilbert's condition: offload load unnecessary for the learning goal and redistribute the saving productively (link Module 02).

Q: What could the A, B, C model suggest tracking in the thesis (design inference)?
+ Number of AI queries and confidence in one's own ability over time, to see whether a self-reinforcing loop appears
- The number of code lines students write each week, with no need to measure confidence or AI queries
- Only the final exam score, since it fully reflects every interaction with AI
- Only instructor satisfaction, since instructors observe all offloading behavior
E: Inference from the metacognitive model: offloading lowers internal confidence (B) and raises reliance on the tool (A); trackable with confidence (Modules 05, 06) and AI-query logs.

Q: Why should the thesis have a no-AI test as a primary measure (design inference)?
+ To measure real skill with the tool removed, as in Kosmyna's session 4, and to detect superfluous offloading
- Because tests with AI are always too hard to score for students
- Because the control group is always allowed AI so no other test is needed
- Because a no-AI test always yields higher scores than a test with AI in every case
E: Kosmyna used session 4 to swap conditions; design inference: measure no-AI skill for RQ1 and superfluous offloading.

Q: What could asking students to quote or re-explain the work they just did measure (design inference)?
+ Ownership and recall, an indicator of superfluous offloading like Kosmyna's quoting measure
- Students' typing speed when writing code, an indicator of keyboard skill
- Students' satisfaction with the AI tool, an indicator of acceptance
- The number of lines students copy, an indicator of academic integrity violations
E: Kosmyna measured the ability to quote from the essay just written (83.3% versus 11.1%) and ownership; applying it to the thesis is design inference.

Q: Why should the thesis not rely only on cross-sectional correlation like Gerlich (design inference)?
+ Correlation does not prove causation; comparison groups and pre-post measures are needed (Module 13)
- Because cross-sectional correlation always gives results wrong compared with experiments
- Because cross-sectional correlation cannot be computed with more than 100 participants
- Because cross-sectional correlation works only for qualitative variables, not numeric ones
E: Gerlich himself says experiments manipulating AI use are needed for causal evidence; the thesis uses an experimental design (Module 13).

T: State the definition of cognitive offloading and the two triggering mechanisms (internal demand and metacognitive evaluation) in Risko and Gilbert (2016). Give evidence for each.
M: Hint: Definition: using physical action to reduce cognitive demand. Internal demand: the more disoriented the display or the more items, the more head tilting; higher memory load or interruptions lead to more reminders. Metacognition: word-and-frame versus word rotation have similar costs but more tilting because of misjudged demand; less confident people set more reminders; writing about 40% of the time with only 2 items.

T: Describe the four experiments of Sparrow, Liu and Wegner (2011) and the conclusion about the "Google effect". Give two limits on generalizing to learning with AI.
M: Hint: Experiment 1 primes the concept of computers after hard questions (712 versus 591 ms); 2: believing erasure gives better recall (0.31 and 0.29 versus 0.22 and 0.19); 3: content recall best when believing erasure (0.93), saved items recognized more accurately; 4: location recall (0.49) beats statement recall (0.23). Conclusion: remembering "where" rather than "what". Limits: trivia and lookup, not structured learning; a cue existed for the folder; no generative AI.

T: Explain the metacognitive framework of offloading (arrows A, B, C) and the self-reinforcing loop. Apply it to a programming student who uses AI to write code.
M: Hint: A: metacognitive evaluation selects the strategy; B: using a strategy alters evaluation; C: offloading affects lower-level cognition. GPS example. Application (design inference): the student sees the AI writes code better than they do so offloads (A); with few attempts confidence falls (B) and debugging skill weakens (C); next time they offload more. Measure: AI queries, confidence, no-AI scores.

T: Analyze the evidence on generative AI and critical thinking in Lee (2025), Gerlich (2025) and Kosmyna (2025): design, main results and limits of each.
M: Hint: Lee: 319 people, 936 examples; high confidence in AI goes with less critical thinking, high self-confidence with more; self-report. Gerlich: 666 people, r = −0.68 and −0.75, partial mediation (−0.25 of −0.42); cross-sectional, self-report, convenience sample. Kosmyna: 54 people, EEG; 83.3% versus 11.1% could not quote; preprint, small sample, one LLM, essay writing. None proves solid causation.

T: Propose how to measure "superfluous offloading" and skill with AI removed in your thesis, based on Risko and Gilbert and Kosmyna, with limitations.
M: Hint: An AI-removed session (like session 4): a no-AI test; ask students to quote or re-explain the work they just did; log AI queries (frequency, type); confidence before the test (Modules 05, 06); separate extraneous load from load tied to the learning goal. Limits: small sample, one AI tool, practice effects between sessions, self-report; needs comparison groups (Module 13).

T: Why does Gerlich, with strong correlations (r = −0.68 to −0.75), still not justify concluding "AI reduces critical thinking"? Give three alternative explanations and how to test them.
M: Hint: Cross-sectional, self-report, convenience sample from social media. Alternatives: people with low critical thinking choose to use AI more (reverse causation); a third factor such as age, education or occupation; measurement bias (heavy AI users rate themselves differently). Tests: repeated measures over time, experiments manipulating AI use, controlling confounders, and measuring critical thinking with a standardized test rather than self-report.
