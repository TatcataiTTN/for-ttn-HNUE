## Davis 1989: the problem and the definitions
Q: According to Davis (1989), what mainly constrained research on user acceptance?
+ A shortage of high-quality measures for the key determinants of user acceptance
- A shortage of powerful computers on which users could try out new information systems
- A shortage of users willing to answer long questionnaires in large organizations
- A shortage of managers who supported bringing information systems into companies
E: The paper: "research has been constrained by the shortage of high-quality measures for key determinants of user acceptance."

Q: Which practical problem does Davis use to open the paper?
+ The performance gains of information technology are often obstructed by users' unwillingness to accept and use available systems
- Information technology always lowers the performance of office workers because it is too complex
- Organizations always refuse to invest in information technology because purchase costs are too high
- Users always overuse information systems, creating harmful dependence on them
E: The paper: "Information technology offers the potential for substantially improving white collar performance. But performance gains are often obstructed by users' unwillingness to accept and use available systems."

Q: What does Davis observe about subjective measures used in practice?
+ Many are unvalidated and their relationship to system usage is unknown
- All had been carefully validated and had reliabilities above .90 in every study
- Subjective measures are never used in practice because they are not trustworthy at all
- Subjective measures always correlate above .80 with system use in every case
E: The paper: "Most subjective measures used in practice are unvalidated, and their relationship to system usage is unknown."

Q: How does Davis (1989) define "perceived usefulness"?
+ The degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would enhance his or her job performance
- The degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would require no effort at all
- The degree to which a person likes the interface of a system while using it
- The degree to which a person believes that colleagues are also using the same system
E: The paper: '"the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would enhance his or her job performance."'

Q: How does Davis (1989) define "perceived ease of use"?
+ The degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would be free of effort
- The degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would enhance job performance
- The degree to which a person can complete every task with the system without any training
- The degree to which a person finds the system's interface attractive and to their aesthetic taste
E: The paper: '"the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would be free of effort."'

Q: On which word does Davis base the definition of "usefulness"?
+ "Useful": capable of being used advantageously
- "Useful": widely used in the company
- "Useful": available cheaply on the market
- "Useful": licensed by a government regulator
E: The paper: 'This follows from the definition of the word useful: "capable of being used advantageously."'

Q: Why does Davis link effort to the concept of ease of use?
+ Effort is a finite resource that can be allocated to other activities; an easier system is more likely to be accepted
- Effort is an unlimited resource, so however hard a system is to use, users always accept it
- Effort matters only for manual work and has nothing to do with office computer use
- Effort is unrelated to technology acceptance because users only care about price
E: The paper: "Effort is a finite resource that a person may allocate to the various activities for which he or she is responsible... All else being equal, we claim, an application perceived to be easier to use than another is more likely to be accepted by users."

Q: Why is "usefulness" linked to rewards in organizations?
+ People are generally reinforced for good performance by raises, promotions and bonuses; a useful system is one with a believed positive use-performance link
- Because users use only systems proposed directly by their own managers
- Because information systems automatically compute bonuses for the employees who use them most
- Because usefulness is defined as the money the organization saves after using the system
E: The paper: "Within an organizational context, people are generally reinforced for good performance by raises, promotions, bonuses, and other rewards... A system high in perceived usefulness, in turn, is one for which a user believes in the existence of a positive use-performance relationship."

Q: What does Davis's initial theory predict about ease of use, usefulness and use?
+ Both are important determinants of use: people use a system if they believe it helps performance, but may not if it is too hard
- Only usefulness is a determinant; ease of use is unrelated to use of a system
- Only ease of use is a determinant; usefulness is unrelated to use of a system
- Neither is related to use, because use depends only on managers' instructions
E: The paper: "people tend to use or not use an application to the extent they believe it will help them perform their job better... even if potential users believe that a given application is useful, they may... believe that the system is too hard to use... usage is theorized to be influenced by perceived ease of use."

## Davis 1989: theoretical foundations
Q: Which dimension did Schultz and Slevin (1975) find most correlated with use of a decision model?
+ The "performance" dimension (effect of the model on the manager's job performance), r = .61
- The "satisfaction" dimension, i.e., the manager's degree of satisfaction, r = .61
- The "cost" dimension, i.e., the price of implementing the model, r = .61
- The "time" dimension, i.e., the time the model needs to compute, r = .61
E: The paper: 'the "performance" dimension, interpreted by the authors as the perceived "effect of the model on the manager's job performance," was most highly correlated with self-predicted use of a decision model (r=.61).'

Q: How strongly did Robey (1979) find the performance dimension correlated with two objective measures of usage?
+ r = .79 and .76
- r = .09 and .06
- r = .29 and .26
- r = .49 and .46
E: The paper: "Robey (1979) finds the performance dimension to be most correlated with two objective measures of system usage (r=.79 and .76)."

Q: Why does Davis criticize using Schultz and Slevin's performance factor to measure performance expectancies?
+ The instrument was derived by exploratory factor analysis, with a low sample-to-item ratio (2:1) and some items outside the definition
- The instrument was built without asking any users in the organization about their perceptions
- The instrument was copied wholesale from an outdated instrument by another researcher
- The instrument used so many respondents that it was costly and unsuitable for small studies
E: The paper: "the instrument is empirically derived via exploratory factor analysis; a somewhat low ratio of sample size to items is used (2:1); four of thirteen items have loadings below .5, and several of the items clearly fall outside the definition of expected performance improvements".

Q: What range of correlations did DeSanctis (1983) find between "motivational force" and DSS use, and what does Davis conclude?
+ .04 to .26; the contrast with Robey shows the importance of measurement
- .74 to .96; the similarity with Robey shows results are always consistent
- −.26 to −.04; the opposite of Robey shows the theory is wholly refuted
- .50 to .60; the similarity with Schultz and Slevin shows the scale is very trustworthy
E: The paper: '"Force" had small but significant correlations with usage of a DSS within a business simulation experiment (correlations ranged from .04 to .26). The contrast between DeSanctis's correlations and the ones observed by Robey underscores the importance of measurement in predicting and explaining use.'

Q: How does Bandura's (1982) self-efficacy theory relate to Davis's variables?
+ Self-efficacy is similar to perceived ease of use; outcome beliefs are similar to usefulness; behavior is best predicted by considering both
- Self-efficacy is identical to usefulness; outcome beliefs are identical to ease of use
- Self-efficacy is unrelated to Davis's variables because it applies only to children learning
- Self-efficacy predicts behavior only when combined with peer groups' subjective norms
E: The paper: "Self-efficacy is similar to perceived ease of use... Bandura's 'outcome judgment' variable is similar to perceived usefulness... 'In any given instance, behavior would be best predicted by considering both self-efficacy and outcome beliefs'".

Q: Why does the self-efficacy paradigm not directly give a general measure for Davis's purposes?
+ Efficacy beliefs are theorized to be situation-specific, so measures must be tailored to the domain
- Efficacy beliefs are considered innate, so no questionnaire can measure them
- Efficacy beliefs exist only in professional users, so they do not apply to novices
- Efficacy beliefs have already been measured with one standard scale, so no new scale is needed
E: The paper: "The self efficacy paradigm does not offer a general measure applicable to our purposes since efficacy beliefs are theorized to be situationally-specific, with measures tailored to the domain under study (Bandura, 1982)."

Q: How does the cost-benefit paradigm from behavioral decision theory relate to Davis's two variables?
+ Decision makers trade off the effort of a strategy against the quality of its result, parallel to ease of use versus usefulness
- Decision makers always choose the most effortful strategy because they think it is most accurate
- Decision makers always choose the least effortful strategy regardless of the quality of the result
- Decision makers care about neither effort nor quality but only about other people's opinions
E: The paper: "This research explains people's choice among various decision-making strategies... in terms of a cognitive trade-off between the effort required to employ the strategy and the quality (accuracy) of the resulting decision... The distinction made herein between perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use is similar to the distinction between subjective decision-making performance and effort."

Q: Which three innovation characteristics did Tornatzky and Klein (1982) find most consistently related to adoption?
+ Compatibility, relative advantage and complexity
- Cost, speed and durability
- Novelty, beauty and popularity
- Size, weight and color
E: The paper: "Tornatzky and Klein (1982) find that compatibility, relative advantage, and complexity have the most consistent significant relationships across a broad range of innovation types."

Q: Which concept in adoption-of-innovation research is close to perceived ease of use?
+ Complexity (the degree to which an innovation is perceived as relatively difficult to understand and use)
- Relative advantage (the degree to which an innovation is better than the idea it supersedes)
- Compatibility (the degree to which an innovation fits existing values)
- Observability (the degree to which others can see the results of the innovation)
E: The paper: 'Complexity, defined by Rogers and Shoemaker (1971) as "the degree to which an innovation is perceived as relatively difficult to understand and use" (p. 154), parallels perceived ease of use quite closely.'

Q: Which two factors did Larcker and Lessig (1980) find when factor analyzing six items rating information reports?
+ Perceived importance and perceived usableness (how clear and readable the format is)
- Perceived accuracy and perceived newness of the information report
- Perceived cost and perceived time to receive the information report
- Perceived reliability of the writer and the age of the reader of the report
E: The paper: "(1) perceived importance... and (2) perceived usableness, which is defined as the degree to which 'the information format is unambiguous, clear or readable'". Reliabilities of the two dimensions were only .64-.77.

Q: Swanson (1987) found "value" and "accessibility"; which of Davis's variables do they correspond to?
+ Value to usefulness; accessibility to ease of use
- Value to ease of use; accessibility to usefulness
- Both to usefulness, and neither to ease of use
- Neither to Davis's variables, because they measure something else
E: The paper: "value parallels perceived usefulness... Several of Swanson's 'accessibility' items, such as 'convenient,' 'controllable,' 'easy,' and 'unburdensome,' correspond to perceived ease of use". Correlations with use: .20 (value) and .13 (accessibility).

Q: Which two dimensions did Hauser and Simmie (1981) find in their marketing study of communication technologies?
+ Ease of use and effectiveness, both influencing user preferences
- Price and brand, both influencing user preferences
- Size and color, both influencing user preferences
- Speed and durability, both influencing user preferences
E: The paper: "a marketing study by Hauser and Simmie (1981)... similarly derived two underlying dimensions: ease of use and effectiveness... Both ease of use and effectiveness were influential in the formation of user preferences".

Q: What does Davis observe about HCI research on ease of use?
+ It mostly used objective measures (completion time, error rates), while subjective ease of use is more relevant to deciding whether to use
- It mostly used subjective measures, while objective ease of use is more relevant to deciding whether to use
- It mostly did not measure ease of use and only measured usefulness in studies of computer interfaces
- It insisted that subjective and objective ease of use are always exactly the same
E: The paper: "these studies have focused on objective measures of ease of use, such as task completion time and error rates... subjective ease of use is more relevant to the users' decision whether or not to use the system and may not agree with the objective measures".

## Davis 1989: scale development
Q: Why does Davis stress building validity in from the start of a scale?
+ Per Nunnally, rather than testing validity after construction, one should ensure it through the plan and procedures for construction
- Because testing validity after construction is always far more expensive than rebuilding from scratch
- Because old scales were all wrong so everything had to be rebuilt without consulting earlier literature
- Because journals do not accept validity tests of scales after the scales are complete
E: The paper: 'As Nunnally (1978) points out, "Rather than test the validity of measures after they have been constructed, one should ensure the validity by the plan and procedures for construction" (p. 258).'

Q: How many candidate items per variable were generated at first, and why?
+ 14: 10 were needed for reliability of at least .80 (Spearman-Brown), plus 4 to allow elimination
- 20: 20 were needed for reliability of at least .99 as required by academic journals
- 6: exactly 6 because that is the optimal number under a factor analysis rule of thumb
- 30: 30 were needed to ensure multiple regression could be run in later studies
E: The paper: "the formula suggests that 10 items would be needed for each perceptual variable to achieve reliability of at least .80... Adding four additional items for each construct to allow for item elimination, it was decided to generate 14 items for each construct."

Q: How many published studies did Davis review to identify facets of the constructs?
+ 37 studies of user reactions to interactive systems
- 7 studies of user reactions to interactive systems
- 137 studies of user reactions to interactive systems
- 3 studies of user reactions to interactive systems
E: The paper: "In preparing candidate items, 37 published research papers dealing with user reactions to interactive systems were reviewed".

Q: In which system were the candidate items worded in the pretest?
+ The electronic mail system, one of the two test applications of Study 1
- The WriteOne word-processing system used at the University of Michigan in the later study
- The Chart-Master graphics system used in Study 2 of this paper
- A spreadsheet system used for accounting in IBM departments
E: The paper: 'The items are worded in reference to "the electronic mail system," which is one of the two test applications investigated in Study 1'.

Q: Who took part in the pretest and what did they do?
+ 15 experienced users at MIT Sloan; they ranked how well items matched the definition and sorted items into categories
- 200 IBM employees; they filled in a questionnaire on satisfaction and expectations about the email system
- 40 MBA students in Boston; they tried two graphics packages for exactly one hour
- 5 statisticians; they checked the validity of the Spearman-Brown formula in the paper
E: The paper: "Pretest participants consisted of a sample of 15 experienced computer users from the Sloan School of Management, MIT, including five secretaries, five graduate students and five members of the professional staff... participants were asked to perform two tasks, prioritization and categorization".

Q: What three main clusters emerged from the categorization of usefulness items?
+ Job effectiveness; productivity and time savings; importance of the system to one's job
- Cost; training time; software stability
- Interface design; color; screen layout
- Satisfaction; comfort; user safety
E: The paper: "The first cluster relates to job effectiveness, the second to productivity and time savings, and the third to the importance of the system to one's job."

Q: Why did Davis treat "ease of learning" as a substratum of ease of use rather than a separate construct?
+ Research showed ease of use and ease of learning are closely related (Roberts and Moran: r = .79) and people often "learn by doing"
- Because ease of learning had been shown to be entirely unrelated to ease of use in HCI studies
- Because measuring ease of learning required equipment too expensive, so it was merged to save cost
- Because ease of learning matters only to novices while ease of use matters only to long-time users
E: The paper: "Roberts and Moran (1983) find a correlation of .79 between objective measures of ease of use and ease of learning... In this study, therefore, ease of learning is regarded as one substratum of the ease of use construct, as opposed to a distinct construct."

Q: Whom did Study 1 (field) survey and about which systems?
+ 120 users at IBM Canada (Toronto), about PROFS electronic mail and the XEDIT editor
- 40 MBA students in Boston, about the graphics packages Chart-Master and Pendraw
- 107 MBA students in Michigan, about the word processor WriteOne after 14 weeks
- 15 MIT employees, about the IBM PC used in a laboratory
E: The paper: "A sample of 120 users within IBM Canada's Toronto Development Laboratory were given a questionnaire asking them to rate the usefulness and ease of use of two systems available there: PROFS electronic mail and the XEDIT file editor."

Q: What was the response rate and the number of users of each system in Study 1?
+ 112 responses (93%); 109 email users and 75 XEDIT users
- 60 responses (50%); 30 email users and 30 XEDIT users
- 120 responses (100%); 120 email users and 120 XEDIT users
- 84 responses (70%); 84 email users and 0 XEDIT users
E: The paper: "Responses were obtained from 112 participants, for a response rate of 93%. Of these 112, 109 were users of electronic mail and 75 were users of XEDIT."

Q: How much experience did Study 1 participants have with the two systems on average?
+ About six months
- About one week
- About five years
- About twenty years
E: The paper: "Subjects had an average of six months' experience with the two systems studied."

Q: What were the Cronbach alphas of the 10-item scales in Study 1 (pooled across systems)?
+ .97 for usefulness and .91 for ease of use
- .57 for usefulness and .41 for ease of use
- .77 for usefulness and .71 for ease of use
- .99 for usefulness and .99 for ease of use
E: The paper: "When observations were pooled for the two systems, alpha was .97 for usefulness and .91 for ease of use."

Q: What is the multitrait-multimethod (MTMM) analysis used to test?
+ The convergent and discriminant validity of the items
- The length of the questionnaire and the time users need to complete it
- The number of users needed in the sample to reach sufficient statistical power
- The satisfaction of users with the email system and the editor
E: The paper: "Convergent and discriminant validity were tested using multitrait-multimethod (MTMM) analysis (Campbell and Fiske, 1959)."

Q: What were the convergent validity results for usefulness and ease of use in Study 1?
+ Usefulness: all 90 correlations significant; ease of use: 86 of 90 (95.6%) significant
- Usefulness: only 10 of 90 significant; ease of use: only 5 of 90 significant
- Usefulness: 45 of 90 significant; ease of use: 45 of 90 significant
- Usefulness: none significant; ease of use: all significant
E: The paper: "For perceived usefulness, the 90 monotrait-heteromethod correlations were all significant at the .05 level. For ease of use, 86 out of 90, or 95.6%, of the monotrait-heteromethod correlations were significant."

Q: What were the discriminant validity results in Study 1?
+ Usefulness: all 1,800 comparisons passed; ease of use: 58 exceptions (3%), all involving negatively worded items
- Usefulness: 900 exceptions; ease of use: 800 exceptions, mostly in positively worded items
- Usefulness: no comparison was made; ease of use: all 1,800 comparisons passed
- Usefulness and ease of use: 50% of comparisons failed, showing the scales do not discriminate
E: The paper: "For perceived usefulness, 1,800 such comparisons were confirmed without exception. Of the 1,800 comparisons for ease of use there were 58 exceptions (3%)... all involved negatively phrased ease of use items."

Q: Why did Davis drop the five negatively worded ease-of-use items?
+ Apparent common method variance; the five positive items had reliability .92 versus .83 for the negative ones
- Because negative items were too confusing for IBM users and raised the number of blank answers
- Because negative items had higher reliability (.92) than positive ones (.83) so both were not needed
- Because MIS Quarterly forbids negative wording in every questionnaire
E: The paper: "The five positively worded ease of use items had a reliability of .92 compared to .83 for the five negative items... the five negatively worded items were eliminated due to their apparent common method variance".

Q: What "irony" does Davis note about reversed items?
+ Reversed items are typically used to reduce common method variance, yet here they showed signs of adding it
- Reversed items are typically used to lengthen questionnaires, yet here they made the questionnaire shorter
- Reversed items typically make scales less reliable, yet here they made the scale far more reliable
- Reversed items are typically dropped in every study, yet here they were all retained
E: The paper: "This is ironic, since reversed scales are typically used in an effort to reduce common method variance."

Q: What did the factor analysis (principal components, oblique rotation) in Study 1 conclude about the two item groups?
+ Usefulness and ease-of-use items loaded on two distinct factors
- All items loaded on a single factor, so usefulness cannot be separated from ease of use
- Items loaded on five different factors, so the two variables have no clear structure
- Factor analysis could not be run because the sample was too small for the number of items
E: The paper: "The results show that the usefulness and ease of use items load on distinct factors (Table 6). The multitrait-multimethod analysis and factor analysis both support the construct validity of the 10-item scales."

Q: With Spearman-Brown, what did the .97 reliability of the 10-item usefulness scale imply for a six-item scale?
+ About .94, so six items would suffice for reliability above .9 while keeping validity
- Below .5, so 10 items were the minimum that could not be reduced
- Exactly 1.00, so no further validity checks were needed
- It cannot be computed with Spearman-Brown because the formula is only for long scales
E: The paper: "Applying the Spearman-Brown prophecy formula to the .97 reliability obtained for perceived usefulness indicates that a six-item scale composed of items having comparable reliability would yield a scale reliability of .94."

Q: How many people took part in Study 2 (lab) and who were they?
+ 40 evening MBA students at Boston University, paid $25
- 107 full-time MBA students at the University of Michigan, unpaid
- 120 IBM Canada employees in Toronto, rewarded with leave time
- 15 experienced users at MIT Sloan, paid with gift vouchers
E: The paper: "The lab study involved 40 voluntary participants who were evening MBA students at Boston University. They were paid $25 for participating in the study."

Q: Which two systems were evaluated in Study 2?
+ Chart-Master (a menu-driven numeric charting package) and Pendraw (a drawing package with a digitizer tablet and electronic pen)
- PROFS (email) and XEDIT (file editor) on IBM mainframes
- WriteOne (word processing) and Lotus 1-2-3 (spreadsheet) on IBM PCs
- Word Perfect (word processing) and dBase (database) on Apple machines
E: The paper: "The study involved evaluating two IBM PC-based graphics systems: Chart-Master (by Decision Resources, Inc.) and Pendraw (by Pencept, Inc.)."

Q: How much time did Study 2 participants interact with each system before rating it?
+ One hour of hands-on experience with each system
- One week of hands-on experience with each system
- One year of hands-on experience with each system
- None; they only read a written description
E: The paper: "Participants were given one hour of hands-on experience with Chart-Master and Pendraw, using workbooks that were designed to follow the same instructional sequence as the user manuals".

Q: Why is Study 2 important for practical use of the scales?
+ It approximates prototype testing or system selection, where users have only brief experience but must rate future usefulness and ease of use
- Because it is the only study using users with many years of real experience of the two graphics systems
- Because it shows users can rate a system accurately without ever experiencing it
- Because it wholly replaces the need to measure actual use through system logs in all cases
E: The paper: "Study 2 was designed to approximate applied prototype testing or system selection situations... prospective users are typically given a brief hands-on demonstration involving less than an hour of actually interacting with the candidate system."

Q: What were the Cronbach alphas of the six-item scales in Study 2?
+ .98 for usefulness and .94 for ease of use
- .58 for usefulness and .44 for ease of use
- .78 for usefulness and .74 for ease of use
- .68 for usefulness and .64 for ease of use
E: The paper: "Cronbach alpha was .98 for perceived usefulness and .94 for perceived ease of use."

Q: What surprising result about the "flexible" item in the ease-of-use scale was noted in Study 2?
+ Applied to Chart-Master it was not significantly correlated with other items, suggesting flexibility is not always associated with ease of use
- It correlated perfectly negatively with every other item and had to be dropped from the scale
- It had the highest correlation with usefulness and was moved to the usefulness scale
- It was not used in Study 2 because it had been dropped in the initial pretest
E: The paper: "Ease of use item 4 (flexibility), applied to Chart-Master, was not significantly correlated with either items 3... or 5... This suggests that, contrary to conventional wisdom, flexibility is not always associated with ease of use."

## Davis 1989: relationship to use
Q: In Study 1 (pooled), what was the correlation between perceived usefulness and self-reported use?
+ r = .63, higher than that of ease of use (r = .45)
- r = .23, lower than that of ease of use (r = .45)
- r = .45, equal to that of ease of use (r = .45)
- r = .93, far higher than any correlation in other studies
E: The paper: "When data were pooled across systems, usage correlated .63 with usefulness and .45 with ease of use. The overall usefulness-use correlation was significantly greater than the ease of use-use correlation".

Q: In Study 2 (pooled), what was the correlation between usefulness and self-predicted use?
+ r = .85; for ease of use r = .59
- r = .35; for ease of use r = .59
- r = .55; for ease of use r = .85
- r = .05; for ease of use r = .09
E: The paper: "When data were pooled across systems, usage correlated .85 (p<.001) with usefulness and .59 (p<.001) with ease of use."

Q: How was use measured in Study 1?
+ Self-reported current use on six-point categorical scales from "don't use at all" to "several times each day"
- Objectively through system logs recording every login of each user
- Estimated by the participants' direct managers through daily observation
- By the amount the company spent maintaining each person's system
E: The paper: "Participants were asked to self-report their degree of current usage of electronic mail and XEDIT on six-position categorical scales".

Q: How was use measured in Study 2?
+ Self-predicted future use ("I predict I will use it on a regular basis"), on two seven-point scales
- Objectively by counting how often students opened the software during a semester
- Managers rated each student's usage after the experiment
- Students reported the hours used in the week before the survey
E: The paper: 'Participants were asked to self-predict their future use... "Assuming Pendraw would be available on my job, I predict that I will use it on a regular basis in the future," followed by two seven-point scales'.

Q: What did the simultaneous regression in Study 1 (pooled) show?
+ The effect of usefulness (b = .57) was significant; the effect of ease of use (b = .07) was not
- The effect of ease of use (b = .57) was significant; the effect of usefulness (b = .07) was not
- Both were significant with b exactly .50 and no difference between the variables
- Neither was significant because multicollinearity ruined all estimates
E: The paper: "The effect of usefulness on usage, controlling for ease of use, was significant at the .001 level for electronic mail (b=.55), XEDIT (b=.69), and pooled (b=.57). In contrast, the effect of ease of use on usage, controlling for usefulness, was non-significant across the board (b=.01... b=.02... and b=.07 pooled)."

Q: How did Davis rule out multicollinearity?
+ Standard errors of the estimates were low (.08) and covariances between the estimates were negligible (−.004)
- By removing one of the two variables from the regression so one explanatory variable remained
- By doubling the sample size and repeating the regression until results stabilized
- By reporting only simple correlations without using simultaneous regression
E: The paper: "according to tests for multicollinearity they are not large enough to compromise the accuracy of the estimated regression coefficients since the standard errors of the estimates are low (.08 for both usefulness and ease of use), and the covariances between the parameter estimates are negligible (-.004)".

Q: When usefulness is controlled, how did the variance in use explained by ease of use change in Studies 1 and 2?
+ It dropped by 98% in Study 1 and by 91% in Study 2
- It rose by 98% in Study 1 and by 91% in Study 2
- It dropped by 2% in Study 1 and by 9% in Study 2
- It did not change in either study
E: The paper: "the variance in usage explained by ease of use drops by 98% when usefulness is controlled for" (Study 1) and "drops by 91%" (Study 2).

Q: What causal structure among ease of use, usefulness and use do these results suggest?
+ Usefulness mediates: ease of use influences use indirectly through usefulness (EOU → U → use)
- Ease of use mediates: usefulness influences use indirectly through ease of use (U → EOU → use)
- The two variables are wholly independent with no causal relation to use
- Use causes both usefulness and ease of use, i.e., causation runs the other way
E: The paper: "the results are consistent with an ease of use --> usefulness --> usage chain of causality." Davis calls this a preliminary interpretation needing further experimentation.

Q: How does Davis explain why ease of use may influence usefulness?
+ The easier a system is to interact with, the less effort is needed to operate it, so more effort can go to other activities, contributing to overall performance
- The easier a system is, the more expensive it is, so organizations invest more in training
- The easier a system is, the fewer functions it has, so users are less confused in operating it
- The easier a system is, the fewer users it has, so the system has fewer technical faults
E: The paper: "All else being equal, the easier a system is to interact with, the less effort needed to operate it, and the more effort one can allocate to other activities (Radner and Rothschild, 1975), contributing to overall job performance."

Q: What conclusion does Davis draw about the relative importance of usefulness and ease of use?
+ People adopt mainly because of the functions a system performs and secondarily because of how easy it is; ease of use cannot compensate for a system that is not useful
- People adopt mainly because a system is easy and secondarily for its functions; usefulness cannot compensate for a hard system
- People adopt for two equally important reasons; neither is prioritized over the other
- People adopt for neither reason but only because of managers' orders
E: The paper: "users are driven to adopt an application primarily because of the functions it performs for them, and secondarily for how easy or hard it is to get the system to perform those functions... no amount of ease of use can compensate for a system that does not perform a useful function."

Q: What does Davis say about designers in the human factors tradition?
+ They have tended to overemphasize ease of use and overlook usefulness
- They have tended to overemphasize usefulness and overlook ease of use
- They always balance usefulness and ease of use exactly in every design
- They care about neither usefulness nor ease of use, only about aesthetics
E: The paper: "The prominence of usefulness over ease of use has important implications for designers, particularly in the human factors tradition, who have tended to overemphasize ease of use and overlook usefulness".

Q: What did the follow-up by Davis et al. (1989) find about the effect of ease of use controlling for usefulness?
+ Significant after a one-hour session, but no longer significant after 14 weeks
- No effect at either time, and usefulness also had no effect
- No effect after one hour, but a strong significant effect after 14 weeks
- A strong significant effect at both times with nearly equal regression weights
E: The paper: "Davis, et al. (1989) found a significant direct effect of ease of use on usage, controlling for usefulness, after the one-hour training session (Table 9), although this evolved into a non-significant effect as of 14 weeks later."

Q: Which limitations of the study does Davis (1989) name?
+ Generality is not yet shown; usage is self-reported; a halo effect is possible because one questionnaire measured all three variables
- The sample was so large that standard errors could not be computed and only simple correlations were used
- All participants were IT experts, so they do not represent users in general
- Use was measured by system logs, so it did not reflect users' perceptions
E: The paper: "The generality of the findings remains to be shown... Another limitation is that the usage measures employed were self-reported as opposed to objectively measured... since usage was reported on the same questionnaire used to measure usefulness and ease of use, the possibility of a halo effect should not be overlooked."

Q: What does Davis (1989) say about the role of attitude?
+ Both Davis (1986) and Davis et al. (1989) found attitudes do not fully mediate the effect of usefulness and ease of use on behavior
- Attitude fully mediates the effect of usefulness and ease of use on behavior as Fishbein and Ajzen predict
- Attitude plays no role, since every effect goes directly from environment to behavior
- Attitude is the only determinant of behavior, with no need for usefulness or ease of use
E: The paper: "Counter to Fishbein and Ajzen's (1975) position, both Davis (1986) and Davis, et al. (1989) found that attitudes do not fully mediate the effect of perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use on behavior."

## Davis, Bagozzi and Warshaw 1989: TRA and TAM
Q: What was the purpose of Davis, Bagozzi and Warshaw (1989)?
+ To assess how well computer acceptance can be predicted from intentions and how intentions can be explained by attitudes, subjective norms, usefulness and ease of use
- To measure employees' job satisfaction using computers and compare it between departments
- To test the economic return of computer investments in large companies
- To build new scales for the ease of use and usefulness of office software
E: The paper: "This research addresses the ability to predict peoples' computer acceptance from a measure of their intentions, and the ability to explain their intentions in terms of their attitudes, subjective norms, perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and related variables."

Q: According to TRA, what determines behavior?
+ Behavioral intention (BI), jointly determined by attitude (A) and subjective norm (SN)
- Attitude (A) alone, with no need for intention or subjective norm
- Subjective norm (SN) alone, with no need for intention or attitude
- Perceived usefulness and ease of use, with no need for attitude or subjective norm
E: The paper: "According to TRA, a person's performance of a specified behavior is determined by his or her behavioral intention (BI) to perform the behavior, and BI is jointly determined by the person's attitude (A) and subjective norm (SN)".

Q: According to TRA, how is attitude (A) formed?
+ From the sum of salient beliefs about consequences times the evaluation of each consequence (A = Σ bᵢeᵢ)
- From the manager's opinion of using the system in that person's organization
- From the number of hours the user has actually spent using the system in the past
- From the price of the system that the organization bought for users to work with
E: The paper: "a person's attitude toward a behavior is determined by his or her salient beliefs (bi) about consequences of performing the behavior multiplied by the evaluation (ei) of those consequences: A = Σ biei".

Q: How is subjective norm defined?
+ The person's perception that most people important to them think they should or should not perform the behavior
- The number of people in the organization actually using the system as observed by the researcher
- The company's official rule requiring use of the information system
- The users' satisfaction with the system after using it for a long time
E: The paper: 'Subjective norm refers to "the person's perception that most people who are important to him think he should or should not perform the behavior in question"'.

Q: As a general model, what does TRA require of the researcher?
+ Identify the salient beliefs for the behavior under study (5 to 9 beliefs recommended, from free-response interviews)
- Use exactly the two beliefs of usefulness and ease of use for every behavior in every field
- Ignore beliefs and measure only users' actual behavior by observation
- Use system logs instead of any kind of questionnaire on users' beliefs
E: The paper: "TRA is a general model, and, as such, it does not specify the beliefs that are operative for a particular behavior... Fishbein and Ajzen... suggest eliciting five to nine salient beliefs using free response interviews".

Q: How do "external variables" in TRA and TAM affect behavior?
+ Only indirectly, through internal variables (attitude, subjective norm or beliefs)
- Directly, bypassing attitude, beliefs and intention entirely
- Not at all, because behavior is determined wholly by chance
- Directly on attitude without passing through the user's beliefs
E: The paper: "TRA... any other factors that influence behavior do so only indirectly by influencing A, SN, or their relative weights. Thus, variables such as system design characteristics, user characteristics... would fall into this category, which Fishbein and Ajzen refer to as 'external variables.'"

Q: Who introduced TAM and what is it relative to TRA?
+ Davis (1986) introduced it as an adaptation of TRA specifically for computer usage behavior
- Davis (1975) introduced it as a total replacement of TRA in every field of behavior
- Davis (1996) introduced it as an extension of UTAUT for aviation
- Davis (1989) introduced it as a translation of a theory from economics
E: The paper: "Davis (1986) introduced an adaptation of TRA, the technology acceptance model (TAM), which is specifically meant to explain computer usage behavior." (The MIT thesis was submitted December 1985.)

Q: Which two beliefs does TAM focus on?
+ Perceived usefulness (U) and perceived ease of use (EOU)
- Subjective norm (SN) and motivation to comply (MC)
- Perceived cost (C) and perceived benefit (B)
- Perceived reliability (R) and perceived safety (S)
E: The paper: "TAM posits that two particular beliefs, perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, are of primary relevance for computer acceptance behaviors".

Q: In TAM, how is behavioral intention determined (equation 4)?
+ BI = A + U: attitude and perceived usefulness jointly
- BI = A + SN: attitude and subjective norm jointly
- BI = U + EOU: usefulness and ease of use jointly, with no attitude
- BI = SN + EOU: subjective norm and ease of use jointly
E: The paper: "TAM postulates that computer usage is determined by BI... BI is viewed as being jointly determined by the person's attitude toward using the system (A) and perceived usefulness (U)... BI = A + U."

Q: Why does TAM allow usefulness a direct effect on intention, contrary to TRA?
+ In organizations, people form intentions toward behaviors they believe will improve job performance, since performance leads to extrinsic rewards such as pay and promotion
- Because usefulness always makes users happier at work regardless of actual system effectiveness
- Because TRA was proven entirely wrong, so TAM had to replace all of its assumptions
- Because usefulness affects only users who have no attitude toward the system
E: The paper: "within organizational settings, people form intentions toward behaviors they believe will increase their job performance, over and above whatever positive or negative feelings may be evoked toward the behavior per se. This is because enhanced performance is instrumental to achieving various rewards that are extrinsic to the content of the work itself".

Q: In TAM, how is attitude (A) determined (equation 5)?
+ A = U + EOU, with weights estimated by regression
- A = SN + MC, with weights stated by users in the questionnaire
- A = Σ bᵢeᵢ, exactly as in TRA and multiplied by users' evaluations
- A = BI + Usage, i.e., attitude is determined by intention and use
E: The paper: "According to TAM, A is jointly determined by U and EOU, with relative weights statistically estimated by linear regression: A = U + EOU."

Q: Through which two mechanisms does ease of use influence attitude and behavior in TAM?
+ Self-efficacy (intrinsic, tied to motivation) and instrumentality (saved effort contributing to performance)
- Cost (the price of the system) and reward (a bonus for using the system)
- Social pressure (colleagues) and command (managers) regarding use of the system
- Advertising (marketing) and price (promotions) regarding use of the system
E: The paper: "TAM distinguishes two basic mechanisms by which EOU influences attitudes and behavior: self-efficacy and instrumentality."

Q: What determines usefulness (U) in TAM (equation 6)?
+ U = EOU + external variables: ease of use and external variables such as system characteristics
- U = A + SN: the user's attitude and subjective norm
- U = BI: only the user's behavioral intention
- U = Usage: only the user's actual level of use
E: The paper: "U = EOU + External Variables" — e.g., two forecasting systems equally easy to use, but the more accurate one would be seen as more useful.

Q: Why does TAM not include subjective norm (SN)?
+ Its theoretical and psychometric status is uncertain: it is hard to separate direct (compliance) from indirect effects through attitude (internalization, identification)
- Because subjective norm has been proven never to affect behavior in any context
- Because subjective norm applies only to children and not to adults in organizations
- Because subjective norm is so easy to measure that it need not be part of a theoretical model
E: The paper: "Because of its uncertain theoretical and psychometric status, SN was not included in TAM." (It is hard to disentangle compliance from internalization and identification; attitude may influence SN via "false consensus").

Q: What are the two key differences in how TAM and TRA model beliefs?
+ TAM posits U and EOU a priori (general) and keeps them separate; TRA elicits beliefs anew for each context and sums them into one term
- TAM elicits beliefs anew for each context and sums them; TRA posits U and EOU a priori and keeps them separate
- TAM and TRA both elicit beliefs anew for each context and both sum them identically
- TAM and TRA both posit U and EOU a priori and both keep them separate, with no difference
E: The paper: "using TRA, salient beliefs are elicited anew for each new context... In contrast, TAM's U and EOU are postulated a priori... whereas TRA sums together all beliefs... TAM treats U and EOU as two fundamental and distinct constructs."

Q: What is the practical benefit of keeping U and EOU separate?
+ It lets one compare their relative influence and trace the effect of external variables on each belief for diagnosis and intervention
- It lets the questionnaire shrink to exactly one question for every user
- It lets one skip measuring behavioral intention and actual use altogether
- It lets one predict each individual's behavior with absolute precision in the organization
E: The paper: "Modeling beliefs in this disaggregated manner enables one to compare the relative influence of each belief in determining A, providing important diagnostic information... allowing the researcher to better trace the influence of external variables".

Q: Why does TAM use statistically estimated weights instead of multiplying beliefs by self-stated evaluations?
+ Beliefs and evaluations are not ratio-scaled so their product is sensitive to meaningless linear transformations; and U and EOU are expected to be positively valued by most people
- Because users cannot state their own evaluations of usefulness and ease of use in a questionnaire
- Because self-stated weights always predict far better than statistically estimated weights
- Because multiplying two variables is forbidden in all statistical analysis in social science
E: The paper: "Given that neither beliefs nor evaluations are ratio-scaled, the estimated relationship... between A and the product of a belief and evaluation is ambiguous... we expect U and EOU to be positively valued outcomes for most people."

Q: Which of the following is among the four research questions of Davis, Bagozzi and Warshaw?
+ How well intentions predict use; how well TRA and TAM explain intentions; whether attitudes mediate beliefs; whether a better alternative model exists
- What the system costs; who is responsible for installation; when it should be replaced; who should be trained
- Which user is smartest; who uses it most; who saves the most time; who gets promoted
- Which system is cheapest; which is fastest; which is prettiest; which is safest
E: The paper lists: "(1) How well do intentions predict usage? (2) How well do TRA and TAM explain intentions to use a system? (3) Do attitudes mediate the effect of beliefs on intentions? (4) Is there some alternative theoretical formulation that better accounts for observed data?"

## Davis, Bagozzi and Warshaw 1989: design and results
Q: What were the sample and setting of Davis, Bagozzi and Warshaw?
+ 107 full-time MBA students at the University of Michigan voluntarily using the word processor WriteOne in labs
- 40 evening MBA students at Boston University required to use graphics software for one hour
- 120 IBM Canada employees in Toronto required to use email software at work
- 15 experienced users at MIT Sloan trying software to sort questionnaire items
E: The paper: "we gathered data from 107 full-time MBA students during their first of four semesters in the MBA program at the University of Michigan. A word processing program, WriteOne, was available for use by these students in two public computer laboratories."

Q: Why was word processing chosen as the test application?
+ It is used voluntarily (unlike required spreadsheets and statistics packages), students would use it throughout the program, and it is among managers' most-used software
- It is the cheapest software on the market and researchers could obtain it free for all students
- It is the hardest software to use, creating a lot of variation in use among students
- It is required in every course, so all students used it identically in the study
E: The paper: "(1) it is a voluntarily used package, unlike spreadsheets and statistical programs that students are required to use for one or more courses, (2) students would face opportunities to use a word processor throughout the MBA program..., and (3) word processors are among the most frequently used categories of software among practicing managers".

Q: When were the two measurements taken?
+ Right after a one-hour introduction and 14 weeks later at the end of the semester
- Just before enrollment and just after graduation from the MBA program
- After one week and after one year when students had finished the second year
- After one hour and after one day while students still remembered the introduction well
E: The paper: "At the end of this introduction, we administered the first wave of a questionnaire... A second questionnaire, administered at the end of the semester 14 weeks later".

Q: How were the TRA salient beliefs collected?
+ Telephone interviews with 40 second-year students; the seven most frequently mentioned outcomes were chosen (each over 20% and together over 75% of beliefs)
- Face-to-face interviews with 40 first-year students just before the introduction; the three most frequent outcomes were chosen
- An online questionnaire with 107 first-year students after 14 weeks; the ten most frequent outcomes were chosen
- Reading about TRA and choosing seven outcomes that the authors thought most important
E: The paper: "telephone interviews were conducted with 40 MBA students who were about to enter their second year... This belief set complied with the criteria for modal beliefs, since each belief was mentioned by more than 20% of the sample and the set contained more than 75% of the beliefs emitted."

Q: Why did the authors elicit beliefs from second-year rather than first-year students?
+ They needed the questionnaire ready before the introduction; second-years had a similar background and knew the system; omitting a salient belief is worse than including a nonsalient one
- Because first-year students refused telephone interviews in every case when invited
- Because second-year students had graduated and could speak about using WriteOne after leaving the program
- Because second-year students were paid more and so willing to join many interviews
E: The paper: "the consequences of omitting a salient belief are considered more severe than those of including a nonsalient one. To omit a salient belief... degrades the validity... whereas including a nonsalient belief... degrades the reliability".

Q: Which of the following is among the seven salient beliefs?
+ "I'd save time in creating and editing documents", "My documents would be of better quality", "I'd become dependent on WriteOne"
- "I would get a raise", "I would get promoted", "I would be praised by the professor"
- "Friends advise me to use it", "The professor requires me to use it", "The school rewards me for using it"
- "The interface is beautiful", "The computer is fast", "The software is free"
E: The list: "I'd save time in creating and editing documents... My documents would be of a better quality... I'd become dependent on WriteOne" (plus beliefs on ease of editing, not using alternatives, access problems in the computing center, and not using it after leaving the program).

Q: How many items did the U and EOU scales have here and what response scale was used?
+ 4 items each, on 7-point scales with likely-unlikely endpoints
- 10 items each, on 5-point scales with agree-disagree endpoints
- 6 items each, on 9-point scales with good-bad endpoints
- 2 items each, on 3-point scales with yes-no endpoints
E: The paper: "TAM's U and EOU are each operationalized with 4-item instruments... The usefulness and ease of use items were measured with 7-point scales having likely-unlikely endpoints".

Q: How was use measured here and why not logs?
+ Two self-report questions on frequency of using WriteOne; logs were impractical because the software was on different personal computers
- System logs recorded every file opening; logs were feasible because all machines connected to one central server
- The lab manager counted student entries; self-report was not used because it was untrustworthy
- Students submitted document samples; logs were unnecessary because samples were sufficient evidence
E: The paper: "Objective usage logs were not practical in the present context since the word processing software was located on personal computers and subjects use different computers, as well as different applications, from one session to the next."

Q: What were the Cronbach alphas of the BI scale at the two times?
+ .84 (time 1) and .90 (time 2)
- .44 (time 1) and .50 (time 2)
- .64 (time 1) and .70 (time 2)
- .94 (time 1) and .99 (time 2)
E: The paper: "The two-item BI scale obtained a Cronbach alpha reliability of 0.84 at time 1... and 0.90 at time 2."

Q: What was the correlation between intention measured right after the introduction and usage frequency 14 weeks later?
+ r = .35 (R² = .12)
- r = .63 (R² = .40)
- r = .05 (R² = .0025)
- r = .95 (R² = .90)
E: The paper: "Intentions measured right after the WriteOne introduction were correlated 0.35 with usage frequency 14 weeks later (Table 1)."

Q: What was the correlation between intention and use measured together at week 14?
+ r = .63 (R² = .40)
- r = .35 (R² = .12)
- r = .13 (R² = .02)
- r = .93 (R² = .86)
E: The paper: "Intentions and usage measured contemporaneously at the end of the semester correlated 0.63."

Q: Why is the time-1 intention-use correlation (.35) lower than at time 2 (.63)?
+ Intentions can change between measurement and action, and with limited experience they are not yet well formed and stable
- Because students at time 1 had never seen the system so could have no intention at all
- Because the time-1 instrument broke and was replaced by a new one at the second measurement
- Because time 1 used system logs while time 2 used self-report, so the two are not comparable
E: The paper: "Given that intentions are subject to change between the time of intention measurement and behavioral performance, one would expect the intention-behavior correlation to diminish with increased elapsed time... at time 1, given the limited experience with the system, peoples' intentions would not be expected to be extremely well-formed and stable."

Q: Which other variables (A, SN, Σbe, U, EOU) affected use beyond intention?
+ None: intention fully mediated
- Only attitude (A) had a direct effect on use beyond intention at both times
- Only usefulness (U) had a direct effect on use beyond intention at both times
- All variables had direct effects on use stronger than behavioral intention itself
E: The paper: "none of the other TRA or TAM variables (A, SN, Σ biei, U, or E) had a significant effect on usage over and above intentions at either time 1 or time 2, which suggests that intentions fully mediated the effects of these other variables on usage."

Q: What is the role of the .38 intention-behavior correlation from Sheppard, Hartwick and Warshaw's meta-analysis?
+ A benchmark: the .35 and .63 found here compare favorably
- Evidence that intention does not predict behavior in non-IS studies
- A new scale to replace the two-item BI scale of this study
- A mandatory upper limit: no intention-behavior correlation may exceed .38
E: The paper: "Sheppard, Hartwick and Warshaw (in press) calculated a frequency-weighted average intention-behavior correlation of 0.38, based on 1514 subjects, for goal-type behaviors. The intention-usage correlations of 0.35 and 0.63 obtained in the present study compare favorably with this meta-analysis."

Q: What range did correlations predicting use in earlier IS research show?
+ From −.23 to .79, with typical values of .20 to .30
- From .90 to .99, with typical values of .95 to .97
- From −.99 to −.90, with a typical value around −.95
- From .00 to .10, with a typical value around .05
E: The paper: "Overall, the predictive correlations obtained in IS research have varied widely, from -0.23 up to the 0.79 correlation obtained by Robey (1979), with typical values falling in the 0.20-0.30 range."

Q: What percentages of the variance in intention (BI) did TRA and TAM explain at the two times?
+ TRA: 32% (time 1) and 26% (time 2); TAM: 47% and 51%
- TRA: 47% (time 1) and 51% (time 2); TAM: 32% and 26%
- TRA and TAM both explained exactly 50% at both times
- TRA and TAM both explained under 5% at both times
E: The paper: "TRA accounted for 32% of the variance at time 1 and 26% of the variance at time 2. TAM explained 47% and 51% of BI's variance at times 1 and 2 respectively."

Q: In TRA, what were the effects of attitude (A) and subjective norm (SN) on BI?
+ A had a strong effect (β = .55 and .48); SN had no significant effect (.07 and .10)
- SN had a strong effect (β = .55 and .48); A had no significant effect (.07 and .10)
- Both A and SN had strong and equal effects (β = .50 at both times)
- Neither A nor SN had a significant effect at either time (β under .10)
E: The paper: "within TRA, A had a strong significant influence on BI (β = 0.55, time 1; β = 0.48, time 2), whereas SN had no significant effect in either time period (β = 0.07 and 0.10, respectively)."

Q: In TAM, what were the effects of U and A on BI?
+ U was very strong at both times (β = .48 and .61); A was small at time 1 (.27) and not significant at time 2 (.16)
- A was very strong at both times (β = .48 and .61); U was small at time 1 (.27) and not significant at time 2
- Neither U nor A was significant at either time because the sample was too small to detect effects
- U and A had equal effects (β = .50) at both times with no change
E: The paper: "Within TAM, U has a very strong effect in both time periods (β = 0.48 and 0.61, respectively), while A had a smaller effect in time 1 (β = 0.27) and a nonsignificant effect in time 2 (β = 0.16)."

Q: What did testing usefulness beyond A and SN show?
+ U added significant explanatory power beyond A and SN at both times, and SN was not significant
- U added no explanatory power beyond A and SN, and SN was the most important variable
- U and SN added equal explanatory power beyond A at both times
- It could not be tested because U and SN correlated perfectly at 1
E: The paper: "Equation (1b), Table 2, shows that U adds significant explanatory power beyond A and SN, at both time 1 and time 2" (SN: β = .02 and −.04, nonsignificant).

Q: What percentages of variance in attitude did TAM and TRA explain?
+ TRA: 7% (time 1) and 30% (time 2); TAM: 37% and 36%
- TRA: 37% (time 1) and 36% (time 2); TAM: 7% and 30%
- TRA and TAM both explained 100% of the variance in attitude at both times
- TRA and TAM both explained 0% of the variance in attitude at both times
E: The paper: "TRA explained 7% of A's variance at time 1 and 30% at time 2. TAM explained 37% and 36% at times 1 and 2, respectively."

Q: In explaining attitude within TAM, what were the effects of U and EOU?
+ U was strong at both times (β = .61 and .50); EOU was significant only at time 2 (β = .24)
- EOU was strong at both times (β = .61 and .50); U was significant only at time 2 (β = .24)
- Neither U nor EOU was significant at either time in explaining attitude
- U and EOU had equal effects (β = .50) at both times
E: The paper: "U has a strong significant effect on A in both time periods (β = 0.61 and 0.50, respectively), although EOU is significant at time 2 only (β = 0.24)."

Q: Which finding ran against both TRA and TAM regarding attitude as a mediator?
+ Attitude only partially mediated: Σbe had a direct effect on BI at time 2, and EOU had a direct effect on BI at time 1
- Attitude mediated fully and far more strongly than both models predicted
- Attitude does not exist since no scale in the study could measure it
- Attitude mediated only subjective norm and had nothing to do with any belief
E: The paper: "Counter to TRA, the belief summation term, Σ biei, had a significant direct effect on BI over and above A and SN in time period 2 (β = 0.21)... Counter to TAM, EOU had a significant direct effect on BI over and above A and U in time period 1 (β = 0.20)... attitude appears to mediate the effects of beliefs on intentions even less than postulated".

Q: What did the first three TRA beliefs (saving time, easier editing, better quality) form in the factor analysis?
+ A "specific usefulness" factor (Uₛ), correlated with TAM's U (r = .46 at time 1 and .65 at time 2)
- A "cost" factor, negatively correlated with TAM's U at both times
- A "subjective norm" factor, uncorrelated with TAM's U at any time
- An "access" factor, perfectly correlated (r = 1.00) with TAM's EOU
E: The paper: 'TRA beliefs 1, 2 and 3 load on a common factor which taps specific aspects of "expected performance gains"... We will refer to this specific usefulness construct... as Us. Consistent with this interpretation, Us correlates significantly with U (r = 0.46... and r = 0.65)'.

Q: What happened to Uₛ and U at time 2 (after 14 weeks)?
+ They converged into a single factor, the total usefulness index Uₜ (alpha .85 and .93 at the two times)
- They separated completely into two independent factors no longer correlated
- Both vanished from the model because they no longer explained BI significantly
- Uₛ became subjective norm and U became attitude
E: The paper: "At time period 2, a four-factor solution was obtained, with Us converging to TAM's U to form a single factor. We will denote this combined 7-item usefulness index Ut, for total usefulness. Cronbach alpha reliabilities for Ut were 0.85 and 0.93 for time 1 and 2."

Q: Why did U influence BI more than Uₛ right after an hour, while Uₛ gained influence over time?
+ Novices process choices with abstract, general criteria; as they learn concrete consequences, specific criteria gain influence
- Because novices always prefer specific criteria while experienced people always prefer abstract ones
- Because U was measured on a 7-point scale and Uₛ on a 3-point scale, so U was stronger
- Because Uₛ was not significant at first and became significant only after the measurement changed at time 2
E: The paper: "novice consumers are more apt to process choice alternatives using abstract, general criteria, since they have not undergone the learning needed to understand and make judgments about more concrete, specific criteria" (Bettman and Sujan 1987).

Q: What was the final model right after the one-hour introduction?
+ BI = Uₜ + EOU with R² = .45; β(Uₜ) = .62 and β(EOU) = .20
- BI = A + SN with R² = .45; β(A) = .62 and β(SN) = .20
- BI = Uₜ alone; R² = .45; β(Uₜ) = .20
- BI = EOU alone; R² = .45; β(EOU) = .62
E: The paper: "At time 1, Ut and EOU accounted for 45% of the variance in intention, with coefficients of 0.62 and 0.20 respectively."

Q: What was the final model after 14 weeks?
+ BI = Uₜ (R² = .57; β = .76) and Uₜ = EOU (β = .24): ease of use acts only indirectly through usefulness
- BI = EOU (R² = .57; β = .76) and EOU = Uₜ (β = .24): usefulness acts indirectly through ease of use
- BI = A + SN (R² = .57): attitude and subjective norm jointly determine intention
- BI = SN (R² = .57; β = .76): only subjective norm determines intention
E: The paper: "At time 2, Ut by itself accounted for 57% of BI's variance (β = 0.76), and EOU had a small but significant effect on Ut (β = 0.24)."

Q: How do the authors explain the changing role of ease of use over time?
+ At first people treat ease of use as self-efficacy (can I learn it?); once they can use it, it becomes instrumental and acts through usefulness
- At first ease of use is unimportant; later it is the only determinant of use
- Ease of use has exactly the same role at every time and every stage of learning to use the system
- At first ease of use is instrumental; later it is self-efficacy and acts directly on intention
E: The paper: "early on, people appeared to process EOU from a self-efficacy perspective, appraising how likely they would be to succeed at learning to use the system given they tried. As learning progressed over time, this concern became less salient, and EOU evolved into a more instrumental issue".

Q: What did the test of motivation as a moderator show?
+ Importance of performance for getting a job, and the sum of evaluations of the three usefulness beliefs, did not interact significantly with usefulness
- Both moderators interacted strongly with usefulness and changed the study's conclusions completely
- Moderation was not tested because the authors assumed all students had identical motivation
- Only "importance of performance" interacted significantly at time 2 with a very large coefficient
E: The paper: 'we asked subjects to report the extent to which they believed "performance in the MBA program is important to getting a good job." By hierarchical regression, this question did not significantly interact with Ut in either time period.'

Q: What are the paper's three main conclusions about the determinants of managers' computer use?
+ Use is predicted reasonably well from intentions; usefulness is a major determinant of intentions; ease of use is a significant secondary determinant
- Use cannot be predicted from intentions; subjective norm is the main factor; ease of use has no role
- Use is predicted only by attitude; ease of use is the main factor; usefulness is secondary
- Use is predicted only by subjective norm; neither usefulness nor ease of use has any role
E: The paper: "(1) People's computer use can be predicted reasonably well from their intentions. (2) Perceived usefulness is a major determinant of people's intentions to use computers. (3) Perceived ease of use is a significant secondary determinant of people's intentions to use computers."

Q: How much variance in intention did the simple model based on BI, U and EOU explain?
+ 45% (after one hour) and 57% (after 14 weeks)
- 15% (after one hour) and 17% (after 14 weeks)
- 85% (after one hour) and 97% (after 14 weeks)
- 5% (after one hour) and 7% (after 14 weeks)
E: The paper: "This simple model accounted for 45% and 57% of the variance in intentions at the beginning and end of the 14-week study period, respectively."

## Interpretation, limits and thesis design
Q: Why do the authors interpret the "no effect of subjective norm" result narrowly?
+ The SN scale is psychometrically weak, and word processing is a personal application less subject to social influence
- Because subjective norm has been shown to have huge effects in every other study before this one
- Because MBA students are the only group in the world not influenced by others
- Because the authors never measured subjective norm and relied on assumption
